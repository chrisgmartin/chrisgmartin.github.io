Vast.ai: The Marketplace & Its Data

Domain fluency, made specific. This chapter maps Vast.ai's actual product model to the data a first data hire would build and the problems they'd own. Speak this and you sound like you already understand the business.

Sourcing & honesty

Everything here is built from Vast.ai's public product docs and site — the marketplace mechanics, rental types, DLPerf, reliability, and the cost model. I have no internal knowledge of their schemas, pricing splits, or roadmap. In the interview, present this as informed reasoning about their public model, and ask about the internal reality rather than asserting it. That humility is itself a strong signal.

The Vast.ai model

Vast.ai is a marketplace for GPU compute — it connects people who own GPUs (hosts) with people who need them (renters), and competes on price by unlocking underused and heterogeneous supply rather than owning data centers. The defining traits, each with a data consequence:

  • Two-sided & aggregated: supply comes from many independent hosts — professional data centers and smaller GPU-farm/community operators. → You model a fragmented, heterogeneous supply base, not a uniform fleet.
  • Price competition via a real marketplace: hosts set prices; renters can also bid for interruptible capacity. → There's genuine price-discovery data (bids, clearing prices) most clouds don't have.
  • Heterogeneous hardware & reliability: everything from enterprise H100 nodes to consumer cards, with very different uptime. → Quality/reliability scoring and normalization (DLPerf) are first-class data problems.
  • Usage-based, multi-component billing: GPU time + storage + bandwidth, metered. → Metering accuracy is revenue accuracy; reconciliation is core DE work.
  • Perishable supply: an idle GPU-hour is lost forever. → Utilization and liquidity are the central efficiency metrics.

Implication: as the data hire you sit on both sides — renter behavior (demand, search, bids, usage, churn) and host behavior (supply, listings, reliability, payouts, churn). Most analytics questions are about the interaction of the two (liquidity, fill rate, price discovery), which is exactly where two-sided-marketplace modeling (chapter 06b) pays off.

Rental types & the bidding engine

Vast offers three rental modes — this is the most distinctive part of the data model:

  • On-Demand (high priority): host sets a fixed price; runs until the renter stops it. Predictable, premium.
  • Reserved: prepaid commitment at a discount (up to ~50%), same priority as on-demand.
  • Interruptible (low priority): a bidding market. The renter sets a bid price; on a given machine the highest bid runs and lower bids are paused/stopped. An interruptible instance is interrupted when someone outbids it or an on-demand renter takes the machine. Stopping kills running processes — no automatic resume — so checkpointing is essential. Savings can be 50%+ vs on-demand.
Why this is a data goldmine

A bidding market generates price-discovery data: bids placed, bids that won, clearing prices per GPU type/region/hour, and interruption events. That lets you measure the true market-clearing price of an H100-hour, the interruptible discount, and the reliability cost of cheap capacity — analytics a fixed-price cloud simply can't produce. You built the queries for this in 03b (#4 interruptions, #12 median clearing bid).

Modeling it: fct_bid (transaction grain: bid_price, placed_at, won), fct_instance_lifecycle (accumulating snapshot capturing interruptions), and an interruption-rate metric per machine/host. Interruptions are the key UX-vs-cost tradeoff for renters and a churn driver to watch.

The cost model — and its metering subtleties

Billing is multi-component and hosts configure the pieces. The pieces a renter is charged for:

  • GPU/compute $/hr — accrues only while the instance is running.
  • Storage $/GB — accrues continuously while the instance exists, even when stopped, until it's deleted.
  • Bandwidth $/GB — upload/download (ingress/egress), host-configured.
The state machine that trips people up

running → GPU + storage + bandwidth charges. stopped → GPU charges halt, storage keeps charging. deleted → storage stops. Modeling this correctly is the difference between right and wrong revenue. It also creates a real analytics question: "stopped-but-not-deleted" storage revenue and the customer-confusion/refund risk around it.

DE implications:

  • Meter each component on its own grain; total cost = sum across components, not a single $/hr.
  • The instance state log is the source of truth for which charges apply when — model it as the lifecycle fact and derive charges via as-of joins to the rate in effect (02b).
  • Reconcile metered usage (priced) against the billing/credit ledger — leakage in either direction is a revenue or trust problem (03 #12, 05b reconciliation).
  • Credit/prepay model: renters fund a balance; model balance draw-down, low-balance churn, and unrecognized vs recognized revenue.

DLPerf & price/performance

DLPerf (Deep Learning Performance) is Vast's normalized benchmark estimating a machine's throughput (≈ iters/sec on common DL tasks). It's what lets renters compare wildly different hardware on one axis, and it powers search ranking and value comparisons.

  • The killer derived metric: $ / DLPerf-hour — price normalized by performance. It's how "cheapest" is actually measured on Vast, and a clean KPI for marketplace competitiveness vs other clouds.
  • Model: store DLPerf on dim_gpu_type / per machine; compute price-per-DLPerf in the metric layer (it's a ratio → non-additive; keep numerator/denominator additive — chapter 06b).
  • Analytics it unlocks: price/perf distribution per GPU type, where the marketplace is cheap vs expensive, and how interruptible bidding moves $/DLPerf-hour.

Reliability & host verification

Because supply is heterogeneous, Vast surfaces per-host reliability (uptime history) and distinguishes host tiers — professional data-center/colo operators (which can be verified/certified) vs smaller GPU-farm/community operators. Renters filter on this.

  • Reliability is host-specific, not platform-wide — so it's a computed score the data team would build from heartbeat/uptime telemetry, interruption history, and failed-instance events.
  • Supply segmentation: verified/datacenter vs community is a key dimension for quality, pricing, and which workloads route where.
  • Analytics: reliability vs price tradeoff, how reliability affects renter retention, and which supply is "stranded" because it's cheap but unreliable.
  • Modeling: dim_host (tier, verification, SCD2 reliability band), fct_heartbeat/uptime telemetry, derived host_reliability_score as a maintained model with tests.

The models you'd build (Vast-specific star)

Applying chapter 06b's two-sided pattern to Vast:

  • Conformed dims: dim_customer (renter), dim_host (tier/verification, SCD2 reliability), dim_machine (SCD2 specs/price), dim_gpu_type (with DLPerf), dim_region, dim_date.
  • Demand facts: fct_search (factless — filters used, results shown), fct_bid (bid_price, won), fct_usage (instance-hour grain; gpu_seconds, storage_gb_hours, bandwidth_gb, cost components).
  • Supply facts: fct_listing_availability (periodic snapshot of available capacity per machine/hour — semi-additive), fct_host_payout.
  • Lifecycle: fct_instance_lifecycle (accumulating snapshot: requested → provisioned → running → stopped → deleted) → provision latency, interruption count, storage-after-stop.
  • Semantic layer: single definitions for revenue, take-rate, utilization, fill rate, $/DLPerf-hour, interruption rate.

Note on revenue vs GMV

On a marketplace, distinguish GMV (total renter spend flowing through the platform) from net revenue (Vast's take/commission). Leadership cares about both, and confusing them is a classic analyst error — call it out explicitly.

Vast-specific data problems you could be asked to solve

  1. Price discovery: compute the market-clearing price and interruptible discount per GPU type/region/hour; quantify $/DLPerf-hour vs competitors. (03b #12)
  2. Interruption analytics: interruption rate and time-to-resume by host tier; does cheap+unreliable supply hurt retention? (03b #4)
  3. Storage-after-stop leakage: revenue from stopped-but-not-deleted instances, and the refund/confusion risk — model the lifecycle correctly.
  4. Reliability scoring: build a maintained host_reliability_score from heartbeat/uptime/interruption telemetry, with tests and drift monitoring.
  5. Supply churn & concentration: are we dependent on a few large hosts? Host retention; stranded capacity (available but unrented because of price/region/reliability).
  6. Liquidity gaps: demand (searches/bids) that found no acceptable supply — by GPU type/region — to guide what hardware to recruit.
  7. Billing reconciliation: metered components (GPU/storage/bandwidth) vs the credit ledger and host payouts. (03 #12, 05b)
  8. Search-to-rent funnel: which filters predict conversion; where renters abandon. (03b #5)

How to talk about it in the interview

  • Lead with the marketplace, not the tech. "Because it's a two-sided bidding marketplace with heterogeneous supply, the most valuable data is price discovery, utilization/liquidity, and reliability — so I'd build toward those first."
  • Name a concrete first deliverable: "By day 30, a trusted revenue number that correctly handles the running/stopped/deleted billing states and reconciles to the credit ledger."
  • Show you know the subtleties: storage-charges-while-stopped, GMV vs take-rate, semi-additive utilization, interruptible price discovery. These prove you actually understand their business.
  • Ask, don't assert, about internals: "How is reliability scored today? Is there bid-level data retained? How is provision latency measured?" Curiosity about their real schema reads far better than pretending to know it.
  • Tie to the founding-hire frame: sequence by value × reversibility, earn trust in a few numbers first (chapter 01).
