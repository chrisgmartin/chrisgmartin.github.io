Section C · The Big Trade-offs

Build vs Buy & the Platform Landscape

Every data-platform design hits this fork at every layer. The senior move isn't picking a side — it's a framework that lets you buy the commodity, build the differentiator, and name the exact condition that flips the call.

Why this is the decision that recurs

Batch vs streaming and lake vs warehouse each fire once or twice in a design. Build vs buy fires at every layer — ingestion, storage, transform, orchestration, catalog, quality, BI. You'll make this call seven times in one session, and interviewers use it to probe the thing they can't test any other way: do you understand what a data platform actually costs to own, and can you tell what's worth your team's scarce engineering time?

The trap is treating it as a binary or a matter of taste. "We'd build it, we're a strong team" and "just buy Fivetran, everyone does" are both junior answers — one ignores opportunity cost, the other ignores that a $40k/month Fivetran bill at high connector volume can justify a quarter of engineering time. The senior answer is a repeatable framework applied per component, always tied back to the requirements from Chapter 02.

The reframe

"Build vs buy" is really "where do we spend our finite engineering capacity?" You have a fixed number of engineers. Every component you build is a component you now operate, patch, and carry the pager for forever. Buy is the default not because it's cheaper on the invoice, but because it returns headcount to the work only your company can do.

The build-vs-buy framework

Run every layer through the same eight lenses. In the room, you won't recite all eight — you'll pick the two or three that dominate for that component and name them out loud.

  • Total cost of ownership (TCO), not license price. The invoice is the visible tip. Add the headcount to build and operate it, the ops burden (on-call, upgrades, capacity planning), and the opportunity cost of what those engineers aren't building. A "free" self-hosted Airflow cluster that consumes one engineer's attention costs more than a managed orchestrator's bill.
  • Time-to-value. Buy gets you a working ingestion path this afternoon; build is a quarter to first production data, plus a maintenance tail. Early-stage or unproven use cases favor buy hard — you don't build infrastructure for a hypothesis.
  • Differentiation — build core, buy commodity. The single most important lens. Does this component create competitive advantage? Loading Salesforce into a warehouse is a solved commodity — buy it. A recommendation feature-computation layer that is your product might be worth building. Never spend build capacity on undifferentiated heavy lifting.
  • Team size & maturity. A five-person data team can't operate self-hosted Kafka, Airflow, Trino, and a catalog and still ship. Buy-heavy stacks exist precisely so small teams punch above their weight. A 50-engineer platform org has the muscle to run OSS and capture the savings.
  • Reliability / SLA needs. A vendor with a 99.9% SLA and a 24/7 SRE team may beat what your team can operate — or a strict internal SLA on a differentiating path may demand control a vendor won't give you.
  • Vendor lock-in & exit cost. How hard is it to leave? Proprietary storage formats, SQL dialects, and closed metadata raise exit cost. Open formats (Iceberg/Delta, Parquet) and standard interfaces keep the door open. Ask: if this vendor triples the price, what's my BATNA?
  • Data gravity & egress. Data is heavy and expensive to move. Cloud egress fees and the sheer time to move petabytes create gravity — tools that live next to your data (warehouse-native transforms, in-cloud engines) win, and cross-cloud SaaS can incur egress that dwarfs the license.
  • Security & compliance. Can the data leave your VPC? Regulated data (PII, PHI, financial) may forbid a multi-tenant SaaS, mandate a customer-managed key, a private deployment, or in-region processing — sometimes ruling out buy regardless of cost.
Make TCO concrete in the room

Loaded cost of a data engineer is roughly $200–300k/year fully burdened. So "we'd self-host to save the $180k/year vendor bill" is a wash the moment it consumes one engineer — and worse once you count the on-call tax and the features they didn't ship. Saying that number out loud is a strong seniority signal.

Managed OSS is the underrated middle. Confluent Cloud (Kafka), Databricks (Spark), Amazon MWAA / Astronomer (Airflow), and Snowflake-on-Iceberg let you run open, portable technology while a vendor carries the ops. You get most of the exit-cost protection of OSS with most of the low-ops of SaaS — often the right answer for a mid-size team that wants portability without a platform team. Don't jump straight from "managed SaaS" to "self-host it ourselves."

Modern data stack vs consolidated

Two archetypes dominate real designs, and naming them frames the whole conversation.

The modern data stack (MDS) is deliberately buy-heavy and best-of-breed: Fivetran/Airbyte for ingestion, Snowflake/BigQuery for storage & compute, dbt for transformation, a BI tool (Looker/Tableau/Mode), and reverse ETL (Census/Hightouch) to push modeled data back into operational tools. Each layer is a specialist SaaS, glued by SQL and orchestration. It's the fastest way for a small team to a working platform, and it's what most startups run.

The consolidated / OSS archetype trades integration surface for control and cost. Either a single platform absorbs many layers (Databricks doing ingestion + storage + transform + serving; or a warehouse-native stack), or a self-hosted OSS estate (Kafka + Airflow + Spark/Trino + Iceberg + a catalog like Nessie or Polaris) runs on infrastructure you own. Fewer vendors, fewer contracts, lower marginal cost at scale — but a real platform team to run it.

Cargo-cult trap

Reciting "Fivetran, Snowflake, dbt, Looker" as the answer is a tell that you've memorized a stack, not reasoned about one. It's a great default for a startup and a possibly ruinous one for a company loading 50 billion clickstream rows/day where Fivetran's consumption pricing would eclipse a team's salary. Name it as a default, then say what would move you off it.

When building is actually right

Buy is the default, but there are three durable reasons build wins. Name which one applies — a build decision without one of these is usually a mistake.

  • Scale economics. Consumption-priced SaaS is cheap until it isn't. At extreme volume the vendor bill grows linearly with rows/queries while a self-hosted alternative's cost is dominated by fixed infrastructure + a stable team. The classic example: companies that outgrew Fivetran's connector pricing and moved high-volume sources to self-hosted Airbyte or Kafka Connect, or moved off a per-TB-scanned warehouse to Iceberg + Trino. The test: does the annual bill exceed the fully-loaded cost of the team required to run the alternative, with margin?
  • Unique requirements. No vendor fits — a source with no connector, a latency requirement no managed engine meets, a compliance constraint (data can't leave your VPC, in-region only, customer-managed keys) that rules out multi-tenant SaaS, or a transformation that is the product. This is the "differentiation" lens saying yes.
  • Cost at extreme volume, specifically. A special case of scale economics worth calling out because it's the most common real trigger: at petabyte storage or tens-of-billions-of-events/day, the marginal cost of a managed warehouse or a per-row connector dominates everything, and open storage on object stores (Parquet/Iceberg on S3) with an engine you scale yourself becomes dramatically cheaper — even after paying the platform team.
Buy first, build later — and design for the migration

These triggers usually arrive after product-market fit, not before. So the sequencing that wins: buy to start, and choose buy options that don't trap you — open table formats, standard SQL, connectors you can replicate — so that when a bill or a constraint justifies building, the migration is a project, not a rewrite. Lock-in is the tax you pay for choosing buy carelessly.

The decision scorecard

Two artifacts win this discussion. First, the qualitative decision matrix — the axes you weigh. Second, a lightweight scorecard you can run per component on the whiteboard when the interviewer wants rigor.

To make it decisive, score each factor 1–5 for how strongly it pulls, weight the two or three that dominate for this component, and add them up. The point isn't false precision — it's forcing yourself to name which factors carry weight here, which is exactly what a defended trade-off requires.

Worked scorecard: ingestion for a 50-source SaaS analytics platform

Small data team (4 people), standard SaaS sources (Salesforce, Stripe, HubSpot, Zendesk), modest per-source volume, analytics use case, no regulatory blocker.

  • TCO — favors buy strongly. Building 50 connectors and maintaining them against upstream API changes is a full-time job the team doesn't have. Weight: high.
  • Time-to-value — favors buy. Fivetran/Airbyte has all 50 connectors ready. Weight: high.
  • Differentiation — favors buy. Loading Stripe is a commodity; it's not the product. Weight: high.
  • Team maturity — favors buy. No platform team to run self-hosted connectors. Weight: medium.
  • Cost at volume — favors build slightly, but volume is modest so it barely registers. Weight: low.

Verdict: overwhelming buy — Fivetran or Airbyte Cloud. Revisit only if a single high-volume source (say, event data) makes that one connector's consumption bill spike, at which point you'd split that source out to Kafka/self-hosted while keeping the long tail on the SaaS. That split — buy the tail, build the one expensive source — is the staff-level nuance.

The heuristic & how to defend it

The one-line heuristic

Buy to start, build when the bill or the constraint justifies it. Default to managed for everything, reserve build for the layers where scale economics or genuine differentiation appear, and choose buy options with open formats and standard interfaces so the eventual migration stays cheap.

The corollaries that make it operational:

  • Buy the commodity, build the core. If the layer isn't a competitive advantage, it's a candidate for buy. Save build capacity for the thing only your company would ever make.
  • Managed OSS is the hedge. When you want portability but not the ops, sit in the middle — Confluent, Databricks, Astronomer, Snowflake-on-Iceberg.
  • Match the stack to the team. A five-person team runs a buy-heavy modern data stack; a platform org can capture OSS savings. Recommending self-hosted Kafka to a tiny team is a red flag.
  • Design against lock-in from day one. Open table formats (Iceberg/Delta — see Chapter 06), standard SQL, and replaceable connectors keep exit cost low so "buy now" doesn't foreclose "build later."

Deliver it with the decision sentence from Chapter 01: "Given a four-person team and standard SaaS sources, I'd buy ingestion with Fivetran over building connectors, because time-to-value and avoiding connector maintenance dominate here. The cost is a consumption bill that grows with volume — acceptable at this scale — and I'd revisit it the moment a single high-volume source's bill exceeds what a self-hosted Kafka path would cost to run." Naming the flip condition is the whole game.
