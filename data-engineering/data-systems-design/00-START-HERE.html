A study guide for the 60-minute, discussion-based data systems design interview — architecting scalable, reliable data platforms and defending the trade-offs that hold them up.

The interview in one paragraph

This is a 60-minute, discussion-based session. You and the interviewer pick a data systems design scenario and go deep — how you'd build a scalable, reliable data platform, with a whiteboard available. The stated territory is data ingestion pipelines, change data capture (CDC) strategies, and the trade-offs between platforms and architectures — batch vs streaming, data lakes vs warehouses, build vs buy. There's no coding and no single correct answer. You're being measured on whether you can take an ambiguous "design us a data platform" prompt, structure it, and make—and defend—the handful of decisions that determine whether the platform is fast, correct, affordable, and maintainable.

What's actually being tested

Two things, above all: structured breadth (can you cover ingestion → storage → processing → serving → governance without getting lost) and defended trade-offs (can you choose batch or streaming, lake or warehouse, build or buy, and justify it against the requirements rather than by fashion). A candidate who says "it depends" and then actually makes the call, with reasons, beats one who lists options forever.

The method that runs through everything

Because the interview rewards structure, the guide hangs off one repeatable six-step method. Run it on any scenario and you'll never be lost.

StepWhat you do
1 · RequirementsFunctional (which data, which consumers, which queries) + non-functional (volume/velocity/variety; freshness, availability, durability, consistency; budget; compliance).
2 · EstimateBack-of-envelope: throughput, bytes/day and retention → storage, read QPS, rough cost.
3 · ArchitectureSketch sources → ingestion → storage → processing → serving, with orchestration and catalog/governance underneath.
4 · Decisions / trade-offsThe named forks — batch vs streaming, lake vs warehouse vs lakehouse, CDC strategy, build vs buy — each justified against the requirements.
5 · Deep divesFollow the interviewer: CDC correctness, hotspots, exactly-once, schema evolution, failure.
6 · EvolutionScale 10×, failure and recovery, migration, cost control.

Chapter 01 teaches the method in depth; Chapter 10 walks it end-to-end on a full scenario.

The topics they'll pull on

The interviewer has a handful of named levers. Expect real time on each; the guide gives each its own chapter.

  1. Data ingestion pipelines — how data gets into the platform at scale: sources, the log/queue backbone, connectors, delivery guarantees, schema handling. (Chapter 03.)
  2. CDC strategies — replicating source databases continuously: query-based vs log-based, snapshot-plus-stream, deletes and ordering. The topic that separates people who've integrated an OLTP source from those who haven't. (Chapter 04.)
  3. Batch vs streaming — the central processing fork, driven by the freshness SLA against cost and complexity. (Chapter 05.)
  4. Lakes vs warehouses vs lakehouses — where data lives and how it's queried. (Chapter 06.)
  5. Build vs buy — the decision every component triggers, and the one that reveals seniority fastest. (Chapter 07.)

Everything else — sizing (02), scalability & reliability (08), modeling & governance (09) — is the connective tissue that lets you answer those five like someone who has run a platform.

Study schedule

Three calibrations — the same paths are on the hub:

  • A week: one section per day; on day 6 actually whiteboard the worked scenario in 10 out loud, on a timer.
  • Two days: 00, 01, 02, the named topics and trade-offs (03, 04, 05, 06, 07), 10, drill 11, 12. Skim 08, 09.
  • An evening: 01 (the method), 04 (CDC), the decision matrices in 05/06/07, and the framework in 10.

What winning looks like

You don't need to have built every platform in this guide. You need to be the candidate who:

  1. Pins requirements and rough sizing before drawing boxes — freshness SLA, volume, consumers, budget.
  2. Sketches a clean end-to-end architecture and can walk any layer of it.
  3. Makes the named trade-offs explicitly — batch vs streaming, lake vs warehouse, build vs buy — and defends each against the requirements.
  4. Has a real CDC story: knows why log-based beats query-based when completeness and deletes matter, and can land changes correctly.
  5. Talks about cost, lock-in, and operability, not just throughput — the marks of someone who has owned a platform.
  6. States what they'd revisit as the system scales, and where it would break first.

If you can do those six things in a flowing conversation, you're in. Turn to 01 · The Design Interview.
