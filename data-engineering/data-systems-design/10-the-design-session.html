Section E · Putting It Together

The Design Session

The whole guide, run once, end to end — a full platform scenario taken from a blank board to a defended architecture, then a second prompt you attack cold. This is the chapter to rehearse out loud.

The method, in one screen

Everything in this guide collapses into six moves you run in order on any scenario. The point of the method isn't the diagram at the end — it's that every box you draw and every choice you make traces back to a requirement you established in the first eight minutes. Keep this on the inside of your eyelids:

StepMoveWhere it lives
1 · RequirementsFunctional (which data, which consumers, which queries) + non-functional (volume/velocity/variety, freshness SLA, availability, durability, consistency, budget, compliance).Ch 02
2 · EstimateEvents/sec → bytes/day → storage with retention/replication/compression; read QPS; rough monthly cost. Numbers pre-decide half the forks.Ch 02
3 · ArchitectureSources → ingestion → storage → transform → serving, left-to-right, with orchestration and catalog/governance as bands underneath.Ch 03
4 · DecisionsThe named forks — CDC strategy, batch vs streaming, lake vs warehouse vs lakehouse, build vs buy — each tied to a requirement, not a fashion.Ch 04–07
5 · Deep divesFollow the pulls: CDC correctness, hotspots/skew, exactly-once, schema evolution, failure & DR.Ch 08, 09
6 · EvolutionScale 10×, name the first bottleneck, recovery path, migration, cost control.Ch 08
The through-line

The candidate who passes doesn't have a better architecture — they have a traceable one. Watch for it in the walkthrough below: every time a decision lands, it names the requirement that forced it and the condition that would flip it. That is the entire game.

The scenario & clarifying Q&A

Here is the prompt, delivered exactly as an interviewer would:

The prompt

"We're a fast-growing consumer product. We have a transactional Postgres app database and a high-volume clickstream of product events. We want a data platform that serves BI dashboards, ad-hoc analytics, and ML features — and some use cases need data near-real-time. Design it."

Do not draw yet. The prompt is deliberately underspecified, and the first thing scored is whether you close the gaps before committing. Ask five or six sharp questions, then reflect the answers back as the requirements you'll design against. Below is the exchange, with plausible answers — treat the answers as the frozen spec for the rest of the chapter.

You askThey answerWhat it pins down
Who consumes this, and what does each group actually need?Analysts on BI dashboards (daily/hourly is fine); a growth & DS team doing ad-hoc SQL; an ML team needing features for a recommendation model (batch training) and a fraud/abuse model that scores in-session.Three consumer classes with different freshness bars — the key to not over-building.
Which use case actually needs near-real-time, and how near?Only the fraud features — they need event data reflected within seconds to a minute at scoring time. Everything else tolerates hours.The single streaming justification. Everything else is batch.
How big is the clickstream, and how fast is it growing?~50M events/day today, ~1 KB each, roughly tripling year over year.Volume for sizing; the growth rate is the "scale 10×" question in disguise.

Now close the framing by reading the requirements back — this is the sentence that tells the interviewer you listened:

Requirements read-back

"So: two source shapes — Postgres CDC and a 50M/day clickstream — feeding three consumer classes. Freshness is tiered: seconds for fraud features, hours-to-daily for BI, ad-hoc, and ML training. One audited path (revenue must reconcile to the penny) and one best-effort path (product analytics can be approximate). Two-year retention, GDPR deletion, and a 6-person team with no SRE function — so I'll bias hard toward managed services and build only where there's a real reason. Let me size it, then draw."

That paragraph already contains the thesis of the whole design: tiered freshness (so mostly batch, one streaming path), a split correctness bar (so CDC gets a reconciliation story), and a small team (so buy-heavy). Everything downstream is just making those three commitments concrete. See Ch 02 for the full requirements taxonomy.

Back-of-envelope sizing

Two minutes of arithmetic on the board. The goal isn't precision — it's an order of magnitude that tells you whether this is a laptop problem or a distributed-systems problem, and roughly what it costs. Do it out loud.

sizing.txt
CLICKSTREAM
  50,000,000 events/day x 1 KB          = 50 GB/day raw (JSON)
  peak factor ~4x avg  => avg 580/s, peak ~2,300 events/s
  50 GB/day x 365 x 2yr retention        = ~36 TB raw over 2 years
  columnar + compression (Parquet ~5-8x) => ~5-7 TB stored
  growth 3x/yr => ~150 GB/day and ~7,000 events/s peak within a year

POSTGRES CDC
  ~few thousand writes/s peak; change events ~1 KB each
  say ~2,000 changes/s peak => ~170M changes/day worst case
  but most are small; realistic steady state hundreds/s
  initial snapshot: low hundreds of GB, one-time

READ SIDE
  BI: tens of analysts, dashboards refresh hourly/daily -> low QPS, big scans
  ad-hoc: bursty, unpredictable, wants cheap separated compute
  fraud features: online lookups at scoring time -> needs a low-latency store,
    NOT the warehouse (point reads, single-digit ms)

ROUGH COST (order of magnitude, managed cloud)
  object storage ~5-7 TB @ ~$23/TB/mo      => ~$150/mo  (cheap; storage is not the problem)
  warehouse compute (elastic, ~hours/day)  => the real bill; $1-5k/mo range early
  streaming backbone (managed Kafka/Kinesis)=> $ hundreds/mo at this volume
  => compute dominates. Design to keep scans cheap and streaming narrow.
What the numbers already decided

50 GB/day and a few thousand events/sec is comfortably mid-scale — no exotic architecture required. Storage is nearly free; compute is the bill, which argues for separated storage/compute and cheap object storage underneath. Peak is ~4× average, so ingestion needs a buffer that absorbs spikes rather than a warehouse sized for peak. And "3× a year" makes the scale-10× conversation concrete: partition and separate compute now so the answer later is "add nodes," not "re-architect."

Read it back in one breath so the interviewer sees the shape before the detail: "Two sources land in one raw lakehouse zone — clickstream through a log backbone, Postgres through log-based CDC. Raw is append-only and immutable; a MERGE step turns the CDC change log into current-state silver tables; dbt builds gold marts for BI and ad-hoc. One narrow streaming branch peels off the clickstream for fraud features into an online store. Orchestration and governance run underneath everything. There are four decisions on the board — the diamonds — and I've defended each against a requirement." Then stop and invite the pull: "Where do you want to dig in?"

Whiteboard discipline

Draw the batch spine first and completely (sources → raw → silver → gold → BI) — it satisfies most requirements and shows breadth. Add the streaming branch second, explicitly as an exception you're justifying, not as the default. Sequencing it this way signals you didn't cargo-cult streaming; you earned it.

The decisions, defended

CDC: log-based, snapshot-plus-stream

"Postgres feeds the audited revenue path, so I need complete, ordered change capture including deletes — a batch updated_at high-water-mark misses hard deletes and any row that changed twice between polls, and it scans the source on a schedule. So log-based CDC: Debezium reading the Postgres WAL via a replication slot, emitting before/after images with an op type and an LSN. It's low-impact on the source and gives me ordering for free." The mechanics: an initial snapshot to seed current state, then the stream picks up at the snapshot's LSN so there's no gap or double-count at the handoff. Land the change events append-only in bronze, then MERGE into current-state silver tables — I won't re-teach the merge here, that's Ch 05 of the pipelines guide. The honest cost: a replication slot that, if a consumer stalls, holds WAL and can fill the source disk — so I monitor slot lag and alert on it. Full treatment in Ch 04.

The correctness pull they'll push on

"How do you guarantee revenue reconciles?" Answer with three things, not hand-waving: (1) CDC gives an ordered, complete change log, so silver is a faithful replica of the source; (2) a scheduled reconciliation check compares daily revenue totals in gold against the OLTP system of record and alerts on drift — see Ch 06 of the pipelines guide; (3) idempotent MERGE keyed on primary key + LSN so replays and out-of-order delivery converge to the same state. That's a correctness story, which is what "to the penny" demands.

Batch vs streaming: earn the stream

"Only fraud needs seconds, so only fraud gets streaming. I peel a branch off the same clickstream backbone into a Flink job that computes windowed features — events-per-device-per-minute, velocity, geo-distance — and writes them to an online feature store (a low-latency KV store) the model reads at scoring time. Everything else — BI, ad-hoc, ML training features — is batch, orchestrated hourly/daily, because it meets the SLA at a fraction of the cost and complexity. Reaching for streaming across the board would pay latency's tax on data nobody needs fast." Event-time vs processing-time and watermarks matter for the fraud windows — the deep version is Ch 04 of the pipelines guide; here I just flag that late events get a bounded watermark and the offline feature table is the reconciled source of truth for training. Full trade-off: Ch 05.

Storage: lakehouse, not a bare choice between lake and warehouse

"I want cheap raw retention at 36 TB, ACID and schema evolution on that raw layer, and good BI SQL — that's exactly what a lakehouse buys. I'd land data as Iceberg tables on object storage in a medallion layout: bronze (raw/immutable, including the CDC change log), silver (cleaned, deduped, typed, current-state), gold (dimensional marts). BI and ad-hoc query gold via a warehouse-grade engine; the ML team reads silver/gold directly on the open format without a second copy. A warehouse-only design makes raw expensive and puts ML behind an export; a bare lake gives me no ACID and a swamp. Iceberg specifically for engine-neutrality — Trino, Spark, Snowflake, and Databricks can all read it, which is my lock-in hedge." Iceberg vs Delta vs Hudi and the columnar/partitioning detail live in Ch 06.

Build vs buy: buy the commodity, build the glue

"Six engineers and no SRE team is the loudest requirement in the room. Self-hosting Kafka + Debezium + a query engine is a full-time platform job I don't have the headcount for, so I buy the commodity layers and build only what's differentiating." Component by component:

At 3× growth I'd re-run this table annually — the first thing likely to flip is the SaaS CDC bill (Fivetran prices on rows), at which point self-hosted Debezium on the managed Kafka I already run becomes cheaper than the connector." Full framework and scorecard: Ch 07.

Scale, failure & cost

The last eight minutes. The interviewer stresses the design — good, this is where staff-level answers separate. Four pulls, ready to go.

Scaling & hotspots

"At 3×/year the clickstream hits ~150 GB/day and ~7k events/s peak within a year. Because storage and compute are separated, the answer is 'add compute,' not 're-architect.' The real risk is a hot partition: if I key Kafka by user_id, a handful of power users or a bot skews one partition and one consumer lags. Mitigations — key by a higher-cardinality field, or salt the hot key, or partition raw tables by event-time + a bucketed hash. On the warehouse side, cluster gold on the columns BI filters (date, tenant) and compact small files — the small-files problem is the classic lakehouse hotspot. Depth in Ch 08."

Schema evolution

"Two schema surfaces. Clickstream events evolve constantly, so producers publish through a schema registry with backward-compatible rules — additive fields only; a breaking change is a new event version, enforced in CI as a data contract. Postgres DDL flows through CDC — Debezium emits schema-change events; Iceberg's schema evolution lets me add columns without rewriting data. The trap is a producer silently dropping a field the fraud model depends on, so the contract test gates the deploy. Modeling + contracts detail: Ch 09."

Failure & DR

"Each stage degrades independently. Kafka retention (say 7 days) is my replay buffer — if a downstream job dies, I reprocess from offset, no data lost. The CDC replication slot is the fragile spot: a stalled consumer holds WAL and threatens the source disk, so slot-lag alerting is non-negotiable, and I cap it with a dead-letter path. Bronze is immutable, so any silver/gold corruption is fixed by a backfill — reprocess from raw rather than restore from backup. For DR: object storage is already multi-AZ and durable; cross-region replication of raw plus infra-as-code gives an RPO near zero for raw (it's replayable) and an RTO of hours to rebuild marts. That's proportionate — I'm not paying for hot multi-region standby on hourly-SLA BI. Delivery-semantics depth: Ch 07 of the pipelines guide."

Cost

"Storage is ~$150/mo — a rounding error. Compute is the bill, so the levers are: separated elastic compute that scales to zero off-hours; incremental dbt models so I reprocess deltas, not full history (Ch 05 of the pipelines guide); partition pruning so BI scans don't full-table-scan 36 TB; and keeping the streaming path narrow — Flink running 24/7 is a fixed cost I only pay because fraud earns it. For governance, chargeback per team surfaces the ad-hoc users running $400 queries. GDPR deletion is a cost and compliance item: Iceberg's row-level deletes let me honor erasure without rewriting whole partitions."

The move that reads as senior

When they push "what breaks first at 10×?", name one specific bottleneck and its fix, not a shrug. Here: "the CDC replication slot and single-consumer lag on a hot user_id partition — I'd see it as slot-lag alerts firing, and I'd fix it by re-keying and scaling the consumer group before it backs up the source." A named failure with a named signal and a named fix beats any amount of general reassurance.

A second prompt: attack outline

You won't always get the platform-greenfield prompt. Rehearse a cold start on a different shape so the method — not a memorized answer — is what carries you. Here's a second prompt and the bulleted attack I'd run in the room. Try covering the bullets and answering it aloud first.

Prompt 2

"We have ~30 SaaS sources — Salesforce, Stripe, Zendesk, HubSpot, ad platforms, a couple of production DBs — and analysts stitching them together by hand in spreadsheets. Build a platform that consolidates them into one place for reliable company-wide analytics."

This is deliberately the opposite shape from scenario one: low-to-moderate volume, high source variety, and the dominant cost is integration and maintenance, not throughput. The attack:

  • Requirements first. Who consumes (finance, growth, execs)? Freshness — almost certainly daily/hourly is plenty; no near-real-time claim to chase. Correctness: Stripe/finance data must reconcile; marketing attribution can be approximate. Volume is small; variety and connector maintenance are the real problem.
  • Estimate — but the number that matters is different. Not events/sec — it's rows synced/month (drives SaaS ELT pricing) and number of connectors × their fragility (drives ops load). Data volume fits a warehouse comfortably; sizing is about cost of integration, not storage.
  • Architecture. This is the textbook modern data stack, buy-heavy: managed ELT (Fivetran/Airbyte) → cloud warehouse (Snowflake/BigQuery) → dbt → BI, plus reverse ETL to push modeled data back into the SaaS tools. Schema-on-write into the warehouse; no lake needed at this scale.
  • Decisions, defended.
    • Warehouse over lakehouse — volume is small, workload is pure BI/SQL, team wants simplicity; the lake's cheap-raw advantage doesn't pay for itself here (Ch 06).
    • Buy the connectors, emphatically — 30 hand-built, hand-maintained API integrations is a maintenance sinkhole; managed ELT is exactly the commodity to buy (Ch 07). Revisit only for a high-volume source where per-row pricing bites.
    • Batch, full stop — no near-real-time requirement exists; nightly/hourly ELT meets every need (Ch 05).
    • CDC only for the production DBs — SaaS sources come via API connectors; the two app DBs use log-based CDC if they need freshness, batch snapshots if daily is fine (Ch 04).
  • The hard parts to name. Entity resolution — the same customer across Salesforce, Stripe, and Zendesk needs a conformed identity in dbt; that modeling is the actual value. Data contracts / freshness monitoring — SaaS schemas change without warning, so connector-drift alerting and contract tests matter (Ch 09). Cost governance — ELT row pricing and warehouse compute can surprise you.
  • Evolution. 10× here means more sources, not more volume — so invest in a source-onboarding template and a semantic layer so mart definitions don't fork per team.
Why the two answers differ

Same six steps, opposite architectures — and that's the whole point. Scenario one earns a lakehouse and a streaming branch because volume and a hard latency SLA justify them. Scenario two lands on a plain buy-heavy warehouse stack because variety and integration cost dominate and nothing needs to be fast. If you'd drawn the same diagram for both, you cargo-culted. The requirements pick the architecture; you just carry the method.

Driving the room

The design is only half the job; the other half is running the conversation so the interviewer sees the reasoning. Four habits that turn a solid design into a strong signal:

  • Lead the structure, hand over the depth. Get the whole platform on the board fast, then offer forks: "there are real decisions at CDC, at the batch/stream split, and at storage — where do you want to dig?" You control the shape; they choose the pull.
  • Defend with the sentence pattern. Every fork: "Given [requirement], I'd choose [X] over [Y] because [fit]; the cost is [downside], acceptable because [context]; I'd revisit if [flip condition]." The flip condition is what reads as senior — it shows you know the boundary of the decision, not just the decision.
  • Update on pushback without collapsing. If they say "assume 100× the volume and sub-second everywhere," don't defend the old design — say "then my batch assumption breaks; I'd move the spine to streaming-first and reconsider the warehouse." Changing your mind on new evidence while still committing is the judgment they're testing.
  • Never lead with tooling. Requirements, then sizing, then boxes, then names. "I'd stream this because it's modern" fails; "the fraud SLA is seconds, so this one path streams" passes.
Take this into the room

Run the six steps, keep every box traceable to a requirement, earn the fancy pieces instead of defaulting to them, and give a flip condition on every call. Do that and the architecture almost doesn't matter — you've shown the exact thing the interview measures. Drill it in Ch 11, then reread the tactics in Ch 12 the morning of.
