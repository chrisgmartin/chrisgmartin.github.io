Section D · Use cases

Use Cases

Compute futures support hedging by neoclouds and buyers, speculative trading, basis arbitrage, and new capital structures. The biggest impact is on the underlying industry's financial sophistication, not on the trading volumes.

Neocloud revenue hedging

A neocloud with reserved-capacity contracts at fixed prices wants to protect against spot-price collapse on its on-demand surface. Selling futures locks in part of the on-demand revenue.

For neoclouds with significant on-demand exposure (less reserved-heavy than CoreWeave), this is potentially a meaningful operational tool.

AI buyer cost hedging

An AI company planning to consume large GPU compute over the next year can lock forward prices using futures. The cost certainty supports better business planning.

For inference-heavy customers whose costs scale linearly with usage, futures hedging removes a meaningful budget risk.

Speculation

Speculators take views on compute prices without underlying compute exposure:

  • If you expect Blackwell to be delayed, buy futures (you expect H100 prices to stay elevated).
  • If you expect a frontier model release to drive demand, buy spot or near futures.
  • If you expect demand to soften (AI capex slowdown), sell futures.

Speculation adds liquidity to the market, which benefits hedgers.

Basis trading

Basis trading arbitrages the spread between spot and futures. If futures are mispriced relative to spot expectations, basis traders take the spread, returning the relationship to equilibrium.

Capital structure innovations

Once compute futures exist, capital structures can use them. Examples:

  • Lenders may require neoclouds to hedge a portion of expected revenue.
  • Securitization structures can be built around future compute revenue.
  • Customer prepayments can be structured around futures-locked prices.

These structures take time to develop but could fundamentally change how neoclouds finance growth.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Why is hedging more useful for a neocloud with a large on-demand book than for a reserved-heavy one?

    Show answer

    Reserved contracts already fix price. On-demand revenue follows spot, so a price collapse hits it directly, and selling futures locks part of it. See neocloud revenue hedging.

  2. An AI company's inference spend rises in line with usage over the next year. Which side of a futures contract does it take, and what does it get?

    Show answer

    It buys futures, locking forward GPU cost so a price rise is offset by the contract. The gain is a firm cost base for budgeting. See AI buyer cost hedging.

  3. A speculator expects Blackwell to slip two quarters. Which direction do they trade, and how does that help hedgers?

    Show answer

    They buy futures, expecting H100 prices to stay high. Their orders add liquidity, which narrows spreads for hedgers. See speculation.

  4. A lender wants a neocloud to hedge part of its revenue. Why, and what does that make possible?

    Show answer

    Hedged revenue is more certain, so debt service is safer. It also allows securitization and prepayment structures built on revenue that does not swing with spot. See capital structure innovations.

  5. Grain basis traders buy physical and sell forward. What is the compute equivalent, and why is it weaker?

    Show answer

    The nearest equivalent is spot against futures, or reserved capacity against futures. Compute cannot be stored, so a trader cannot buy now and hold, and the spread can persist longer. See basis trading.

Takeaway

  • A neocloud with much on-demand revenue can sell futures to lock part of it against a spot price drop.
  • AI buyers can lock forward costs, which matters most for inference spend that scales with usage.
  • Speculators add liquidity, which benefits hedgers.
  • Basis traders keep spot and futures aligned, but the lack of storage limits how well they can.
  • The largest effect is on financing: lender hedging requirements, securitization and prepayments tied to locked prices.
