The Commodity Analogy

Compute futures borrow patterns from established commodity markets — oil, electricity, semiconductor wafers. Each analogy illuminates something and breaks at specific points. Understanding both is essential to evaluating where compute futures will and won't work.

Oil analogy

Oil futures (NYMEX WTI, ICE Brent) are the most-developed commodity futures market. The structure compute futures imitates includes:

  • Multi-month forward contracts.
  • Standardized contract size and quality specification.
  • Reference price index.
  • Mix of financial and physical settlement.
  • Speculative and hedging participants.

Oil's match to compute is partial. Oil has a single global reference grade (mostly); compute has many distinct GPU types. Oil storage moderates price spikes; compute can't be stored.

Electricity analogy

Electricity futures are more compute-like in several ways:

  • Electricity can't be stored at scale; like compute, it must be consumed in real time.
  • Pricing varies by location (different regional markets).
  • Different "products" (peak vs off-peak) trade at different prices.
  • Capacity (forward access to generation) trades separately from energy (kWh consumed).

The capacity-vs-energy distinction is particularly relevant. Compute futures may eventually distinguish between "reserved capacity" and "actual usage" similarly.

Semiconductor wafer analogy

Semiconductor wafers have informal forward markets but no major listed futures. The analogy:

  • Like wafers, GPUs come in distinct generations with rolling obsolescence.
  • Like wafers, GPU production capacity is concentrated in a few facilities.
  • Like wafers, supply is mostly contract-priced rather than spot.

The semiconductor analogy highlights the obsolescence risk in compute — an H100 in 2028 has very different value than an H100 in 2024 because the alternatives have changed.

What the analogy gets right

The commodity-futures framework works for compute because:

  • Compute is fungible enough within a GPU type to support standardized contracts.
  • Forward pricing is genuinely useful for both hedgers and speculators.
  • Reference indices can be constructed from observable transaction prices.
  • Settlement mechanics (financial primarily) are well-developed.

Where it breaks

The analogy breaks in important ways:

  • GPU generations. H100 in 2027 isn't the same as H100 in 2025 — the relative value vs alternatives shifts. Oil from 2027 is just oil.
  • No storage. Compute is consume-or-lose. Storage smooths most commodity markets.
  • Concentrated supply. NVIDIA controls supply more tightly than any single oil producer controls oil.
  • Demand structure. AI compute demand can change architecture (different attention, custom silicon) in ways oil demand doesn't.
  • Market depth. No compute future trades yet as of November 2026, and the first markets are likely to be thin; oil futures have decades of liquidity.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Name two reasons crude oil is a poor model for compute, and say which is more serious for a contract writer.

    Show answer

    Oil has a single main reference grade, while compute has many distinct GPU types. Oil can be stored, which smooths price spikes; compute is consumed or lost. Lack of storage matters more, because it removes the link that normally ties futures to spot. See where the analogy breaks.

  2. What is the capacity-versus-energy distinction in electricity, and what is the compute counterpart?

    Show answer

    Electricity trades capacity (access to generation) separately from energy (kWh used). The compute counterpart is reserved capacity versus actual usage, and contracts may eventually separate the two. See the electricity analogy.

  3. A buyer wants to hedge H100 costs for 2028 with a future. Which risk does the chapter flag, and what contract designs address it?

    Show answer

    By then the H100 may be several generations behind, so its value depends on alternatives that do not exist yet. Designs include short tenors, cash settlement against an index, and rolling references to current-generation GPUs. See the semiconductor wafer analogy.

  4. In wheat, buying grain and selling it forward ties futures to spot. What happens to that link for compute?

    Show answer

    Compute cannot be stored, so traders cannot buy now and hold to deliver later. Futures prices then reflect expectations and not carrying costs, and spot and futures can drift apart. See where the analogy breaks.

  5. A colleague says compute is fungible enough for standard contracts. What makes that true enough, and what pushes against it?

    Show answer

    Within one GPU type, rental is similar enough to standardize, and reference indices can be built from observable transactions. Generation changes, concentrated supply from one vendor and thin markets push against it. See what the analogy gets right.

Takeaway

  • The commodity framework fits compute where products are fungible within a GPU type and indices can be built from observed transactions.
  • Oil differs because it has a single reference grade and can be stored; compute has many GPU types and cannot be stored.
  • Electricity is the closer model: not storable, priced by location and time, and traded as capacity as well as energy.
  • Wafers show the obsolescence risk: an H100 in 2028 is worth something different than an H100 in 2024.
  • Where the analogy breaks: generation change, no storage, concentrated NVIDIA supply, shifting demand architecture and thin early liquidity.
