Section C · Mechanics

How a Contract Works

A walked-through example of a compute futures contract from initiation through expiry. Margin mechanics, mark-to-market, and the futures curve.

Worked example

Suppose a neocloud wants to lock in revenue for H100 capacity it will deliver in December 2026. It's currently June 2026. This is a hypothetical, because as of November 2026 no compute future trades yet. The sizing follows ICE's announced design.

  1. The neocloud sells a December 2026 H100 futures contract at the current futures price of, say, $2.20/GPU-hour.
  2. The contract is sized to the hours in the contract month. December has 744 hours, so one contract covers one GPU for the month.
  3. The neocloud posts margin (a fraction of the contract value) with the clearing house.
  4. For the next six months, the contract's value moves with the futures price; the neocloud's account is marked-to-market daily.
  5. If the futures price moves down to $2.00, the neocloud's short position gains value.
  6. At expiry in December 2026, the contract settles in cash to the average of the daily index values for the month.

If the neocloud also delivers actual H100 capacity in December at spot prices, the futures gain or loss offsets the spot revenue variance. The net is a locked-in revenue at the original $2.20 contract price.

Margin and mark-to-market

Futures contracts require participants to post margin — collateral that protects against default if the position moves against them. Initial margin is posted at contract initiation; maintenance margin must be maintained as the position moves.

Daily mark-to-market means gains and losses are realized into the account each day. Large adverse moves can trigger margin calls.

Expiry and settlement

The announced ICE contracts settle to the average of the daily index prices over the contract month, and both announced compute contracts are cash settled. A financially settled contract closes out with a cash payment. A physically settled contract would trigger compute delivery, but none has been announced.

The futures curve

The set of contract prices across different expiry months forms the futures curve. The shape of the curve tells you what the market expects:

  • Contango: Futures higher than spot. Market expects prices to rise (or there's a storage / financing cost).
  • Backwardation: Futures lower than spot. Market expects prices to fall (often when new supply is expected).
  • Flat: Stable expectations.

For compute, backwardation is the natural expectation as newer GPU generations bring more efficient capacity online. But supply / demand surprises can flip the curve.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. A neocloud sells one December contract of 744 GPU-hours at $2.20 and the monthly average index settles at $1.85. What is its gain, and how does it combine with spot revenue?

    Show answer

    The short position gains (2.20 − 1.85) × 744 = $260.40. If it also rents out the same hours at spot $1.85, its spot revenue is $260.40 lower than at $2.20, so the two net to $2.20 per hour. That is the locked-in price. See the worked example.

  2. After the sale the index rises to $2.60. What happens to the neocloud's account daily, and why is that a problem even though its physical revenue will rise?

    Show answer

    The short position loses value each day and the loss is paid out of its margin account. Large adverse moves can trigger margin calls, so the neocloud needs cash well before the matching spot revenue arrives. See margin and mark-to-market.

  3. Spot is $2.40, the three-month contract $2.30 and the six-month contract $2.10. Name the shape, give the natural reading, and say what could flip it.

    Show answer

    Backwardation: later prices are lower than spot, consistent with newer GPUs bringing more efficient capacity online. A supply shortage or demand surge could lift later months above spot. See the futures curve.

  4. Should a neocloud sell futures against all of its expected December revenue or part of it?

    Show answer

    Part. Contracts are firm obligations, while expected delivery is uncertain, so hedging all of it turns missed volume into a speculative position. A partial hedge also keeps margin demands manageable. See the worked example.

  5. Why does the clearing house mark positions to market every day instead of waiting for expiry?

    Show answer

    Daily settlement keeps losses small and collateralized, so a counterparty that fails owes little. It protects the clearing house from default. See margin and mark-to-market.

Takeaway

  • A seller of a compute future locks in the contract price, because futures gains and losses offset changes in its spot revenue.
  • Initial margin is posted at entry, and maintenance margin must be held as the price moves.
  • Daily mark-to-market realizes gains and losses each day, so a hedger can face margin calls well before the physical revenue it protects.
  • At expiry a financially settled contract pays the difference to the index; a physically settled one triggers delivery.
  • The curve shows market expectations: backwardation when later prices are lower, contango when higher.
