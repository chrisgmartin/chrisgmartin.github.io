Section C · Commercial

Financial Shape

As a public company, CoreWeave discloses financial structure that other neoclouds don't. Revenue trajectory, capital intensity, debt levels, and margin profile shape how to evaluate the business.

Revenue

CoreWeave's revenue has grown rapidly through the AI-demand surge. Reported trajectory:

  • 2023: $229 million.
  • 2024: $1.9 billion, as H100 deployments came online and Microsoft commitments scaled.
  • 2025: $5.1 billion.
  • First half of 2026: $4.65 billion, with $2.58 billion in the second quarter, as new capacity came online.
  • Contracted backlog (remaining performance obligations) was $60.7 billion at 31 December 2025 and $103.7 billion at 30 June 2026, as of the June 2026 10-Q.

The trajectory is unusual in tech — most companies don't grow from sub-$1B to multi-billion in a year or two. The growth is driven by GPU deployment timing more than by sales-cycle dynamics; once the GPUs are in the datacenter and the customer is committed, revenue recognizes mechanically.

Growth rates

Triple-digit year-over-year growth was sustained through the early ramp years. Growth rate naturally compresses as the base grows:

  • The law of large numbers: 200% growth on a $5B base requires $10B of incremental revenue.
  • Capacity expansion is bounded by GPU procurement and datacenter buildouts, which take 12-18 months.
  • Customer-commitment growth is bounded by deal-cycle pace.

Growth was roughly 170% in 2025 and about 112% in the first half of 2026. Forward growth rates likely settle into the 50-100% range in the near term, then compress further as the base matures. Still very high by mature-company standards.

Capital expenditure

CoreWeave's cap-ex is extraordinary by traditional cloud-services standards:

  • GPU procurement runs multiple billions per year.
  • Datacenter buildouts add hundreds of millions to billions.
  • Networking and ancillary infrastructure add further capex.

The cap-ex-to-revenue ratio is unlike a traditional software company. Customers paying $1 of revenue often required substantially more than $1 of GPU and datacenter investment to deliver. The economics work because the assets earn revenue over multi-year contracts.

Debt structure

CoreWeave funds GPU procurement substantially through debt rather than equity:

  • Multi-billion-dollar credit facilities backed by GPU inventory and committed customer revenue.
  • Several rounds of debt issuance through 2023-2025 at various tenors and rates.
  • At 30 June 2026 total debt principal was $35.6 billion, including $13.6 billion drawn on delayed-draw term loans and $16.6 billion of notes (June 2026 10-Q).
  • Equipment-backed financing structures common in datacenter / infrastructure businesses.

The debt-financed model has implications:

  • Leverage amplifies returns when the model works (committed customers, predictable revenue, hardware appreciating less rapidly than expected).
  • Leverage amplifies risks when it doesn't (customers underutilize, GPU resale value falls faster than expected, refinancing terms tighten).
  • Interest costs grow with debt levels. Servicing the debt is itself a meaningful operating cost.

This capital structure is one of the most-debated features of CoreWeave's business. Bulls argue it's a smart use of customer-committed revenue. Bears argue it's a fragile structure if the commitments soften.

Margins

Indicative margin shape:

  • Gross margins: Reasonably high on the revenue line, before depreciation. The GPUs and datacenter cost a lot but the customer pays substantially more across the contract term.
  • EBITDA margins: Healthy positive, growing with scale.
  • Net margins after depreciation and interest: Closer to break-even or modest profitability in the early phases, expected to expand as scale efficiencies grow.

The margin discussion is complicated because depreciation accounting choices materially affect reported profitability. A GPU depreciated over 3 years shows much higher near-term cost than the same GPU depreciated over 6 years. CoreWeave's chosen schedule and the conservatism of it is a meaningful financial-analysis question.

Profitability path

CoreWeave hasn't reported sustained net-income profitability through the heavy investment phase. The June 2026 10-Q shows a net loss of $626 million for the second quarter and $1.37 billion for the first half. The thesis is that scale brings:

  • Better unit economics as fixed operational costs spread over more revenue.
  • Lower per-GPU procurement costs as volume grows.
  • Improved utilization of committed capacity.
  • Margin expansion on incremental revenue once the base GPU fleet is in place.

Whether the path actually delivers depends on continued customer demand at sustained pricing. If GPU pricing compresses faster than depreciation, margins compress. If demand and pricing hold, the profitability thesis materializes.

Public-market dynamics

CRWV stock has traded with high volatility since IPO. Drivers:

  • Customer announcement news (Meta, additional commitments).
  • Quarterly results — particularly capex guidance and customer concentration.
  • Broader AI / GPU-related sentiment (NVIDIA earnings, frontier model releases).
  • Macro factors (interest rates, capital availability).

The valuation framing is unsettled. Some investors value CoreWeave as infrastructure (low multiples on EBITDA); others as a growth software company (high multiples on revenue). The right framing probably falls between these, and consensus will evolve as the business matures.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Why does CoreWeave's revenue follow GPU deployment dates more than sales cycles?

    Show answer

    Once a customer has committed and the GPUs are racked, revenue recognises mechanically. Timing therefore depends on procurement and datacenter delivery. See revenue.

  2. Every dollar of revenue needed more than a dollar of capital. Why can that still be a sound business?

    Show answer

    The assets earn revenue over multi-year contracts, so the return is measured across the contract term, not in the year of purchase. See capital expenditure.

  3. A bull and a bear look at the same debt. State each case, and name one development that would settle it.

    Show answer

    The bull says committed revenue services the debt and leverage amplifies returns. The bear says weak commitments, falling GPU resale values or tighter refinancing make it fragile. Utilisation of committed capacity or a refinancing round settles it. See debt structure.

  4. Two analysts depreciate the same GPU over three and six years. How do reported results differ, and what should you ask?

    Show answer

    The three-year schedule shows higher near-term cost and lower profit. Ask which schedule the company uses and whether the real economic life supports it. See margins.

  5. What has to stay true for the path to profitability to work?

    Show answer

    Demand and pricing must hold. If GPU prices compress faster than depreciation, margins compress. Scale only helps when utilisation and pricing hold. See profitability path.

Takeaway

  • Revenue grew from sub-billion to multi-billion annual levels, driven by when GPUs came online.
  • Growth rates must compress as the base grows, limited by procurement and 12 to 18 month buildouts.
  • Capital intensity is extreme, and debt secured by GPUs and contracts funds much of it.
  • Leverage amplifies results in both directions: committed customers help, soft utilisation or weak resale values hurt.
  • Depreciation choices change reported profit, so check the schedule before comparing margins.
