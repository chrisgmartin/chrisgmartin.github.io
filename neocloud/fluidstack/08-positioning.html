Section E · Where it stands

Competitive Positioning

Fluidstack against CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, Lambda and the hyperscalers.

Two axes

The comparison guide places the neoclouds on who owns the chips and who owns the building. Fluidstack in 2026 sits in a corner few others occupy: it owns or leases the building and the power, operates the fleet, and does not own the chips. CoreWeave and Nebius own chips and mostly lease buildings. Crusoe owns buildings and chips. The hyperscalers own everything. Lambda and Together own chips and sell hours or tokens. Reading each rivalry along those two axes keeps the comparisons honest.

Against the chip owners

CoreWeave and Nebius carry the GPUs on their balance sheets, finance them with debt against customer contracts, and bear the depreciation. Their advantage is that they can sell to anyone with a purchase order and can be rated and tested on the product they sell, which is why both sit at Platinum in ClusterMAX 3.0. Their weakness is the one the critics of GPU-backed debt describe: chips lose value faster than buildings, and a contract that ends leaves hardware that must be re-rented at a lower price.

Fluidstack avoids the chip risk and gives up the market. A customer who brings its own chips has done the capital raising; the operator earns rent and fees. That is a smaller, steadier share of a larger pie, and it only works when a customer exists who can buy a gigawatt of chips, which in 2026 means a frontier lab or a hyperscaler. Forbes' summary, that CoreWeave and Nebius buy chips and rent them while Fluidstack builds and hosts, is the whole positioning in one line.

Against the builders

Crusoe is the nearest comparison and the most useful one for an interview. Both companies build campuses from factory-made modules; both sell speed; both began somewhere else (Crusoe in flared-gas bitcoin mining, Fluidstack in a GPU marketplace). The differences: Crusoe owns chips and sells hours, so it carries both kinds of risk and keeps both kinds of margin; Crusoe's financing is more conventional; and Crusoe was reported in mid-2026 to be raising at $30 billion against Fluidstack's $18 billion. On the operating record, Crusoe held Gold in ClusterMAX 2.0 and fell to Bronze in 3.0, while Fluidstack went from Gold to untested, which says more about where each company's attention went than about which runs a better cluster.

The other builders in the frame are the developers Fluidstack partners with rather than competes with: Next Frontier in Indiana, and the former miners. A buyer could contract with those landlords directly, and Anthropic has begun to; that is the real competitive pressure on a builder-operator, not another neocloud.

Against the hyperscalers

The hyperscaler comparison is the one Fluidstack chooses to make. Its claim is time: three months to a data centre against at least a year, and six months to 250 MW. The hyperscalers' answer is that they have the balance sheets, the land banks and the power contracts, and that speed at the frontier is now also their priority. The history guide argues that neoclouds exist where the hyperscalers are slow or unwilling; Fluidstack is the purest expression of that argument, because it does nothing the hyperscalers do except pour concrete faster.

Google's position complicates the picture. It is a hyperscaler, and it is also Fluidstack's guarantor, chip supplier and reported shareholder. From Google's side Fluidstack is a way to place TPUs in Anthropic's hands faster than Google Cloud's own build programme can, outside Google's balance sheet. That makes Fluidstack less a rival to one hyperscaler than an instrument of it, which is a different competitive position from any other company in the comparison table.

Beside the landlords

TeraWulf, Cipher and Hut 8 are partners today and could be rivals tomorrow. Each has learned from the Fluidstack deals what an AI tenant wants, each holds the interconnection, and each now has Google as a shareholder or guarantor in its own right. TeraWulf's direct twenty-year lease with Anthropic in July 2026, with Google credit support announced in November, is the first instance of a landlord stepping into the tenant's chair. The miners also carry their own risks, which are Fluidstack's by extension: their bonds are high-yield, their operating history in AI is as short as Fluidstack's, and their shares have moved sharply on each announcement.

SemiAnalysis' "hired gun" phrase is the right frame for the relationship. A hired gun is valuable while the client lacks the skill, and the client learns.

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Place Fluidstack, CoreWeave and Crusoe on the two axes and say what each gives up.

    Show answer

    Fluidstack: sites yes, chips no; gives up the open market and depends on a customer who buys chips. CoreWeave: chips yes, sites mostly leased; carries depreciation. Crusoe: both; carries both risks and keeps both margins. See two axes.

  2. Why is a landlord a more serious competitive threat to Fluidstack than another neocloud?

    Show answer

    Because the landlord holds the asset that is hard to get, the interconnection, and can lease directly to the customer once it has learned what the tenant wants; TeraWulf's direct Anthropic lease is the precedent. See beside the landlords.

  3. In what sense is Fluidstack an instrument of a hyperscaler rather than a rival to one?

    Show answer

    Google guarantees its leases, supplies the TPUs that fill its halls and reportedly owns shares; the campuses place Google's chips with Google's portfolio company faster than Google Cloud builds and off Google's balance sheet. See against the hyperscalers.

  4. Crusoe fell from Gold to Bronze and Fluidstack from Gold to Unavailable between ClusterMAX 2.0 and 3.0. What does that pair of moves show, and what does it not show?

    Show answer

    It shows both builders moved their attention from managed Nvidia clusters to construction and large single-customer programmes. It does not rank their operating quality against each other, since one was tested and one was not. See against the builders.

Takeaway

  • On the two axes of the comparison guide, Fluidstack leases and builds sites and owns no chips, a corner shared with almost no other profiled company.
  • Against CoreWeave and Nebius it trades the open market for freedom from chip depreciation; against Crusoe it is the same build method without the chips and at a lower valuation.
  • Against hyperscalers its claim is months, and its relationship with Google makes it an instrument of one rather than a rival.
  • The landlords are partners who hold the scarce asset and have begun to lease directly; that is the competitive threat to watch.
