Section E · Where it stands

Financial Shape

Rounds, valuation, revenue signals, debt and what the balance sheet depends on.

Equity rounds

Fluidstack ran for eight years on revenue, small debt and little disclosed equity, then raised more than $2 billion in nine months. The company has announced one of the two large rounds; the other rests on Forbes' reporting and has not been denied.

The sequence is unusual in two ways. A company's first priced institutional round at $7.5 billion is rare and reflects that the Anthropic programme preceded the equity. And the step from $7.5 billion to over $18 billion in eight months, with a $40 billion number in the air two months later, is the market pricing the company as a share of the frontier build-out rather than on its own income statement. A reader should hold both figures lightly: neither the $18 billion nor the $40 billion appears in any filing.

Who owns it

Forbes names Situational Awareness as among the largest shareholders, with an estimated $2 billion stake; Jane Street as lead of the latest round; and BlackRock, Atreides Management, Charlie Songhurst, Alphabet and Spark Capital as other holders. It reported that Situational Awareness' founder, Leopold Aschenbrenner, faced an SEC investigation over losses on leveraged AI-stock positions and had sold his hedge fund to Citadel in July 2026, and that Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis holds a stake through a vehicle estimated at about $140 million. Nvidia is not an investor. The three founders are reported to be billionaires on paper at the $18 billion mark.

Two of those holders matter for the business, not just the cap table. Alphabet is the guarantor and chip supplier, so its equity aligns its credit exposure with the upside. Jane Street is also a customer. The rest is capital.

Revenue and the lag

The only audited figure is from UK filings: revenue over $66 million in 2024. After the headquarters moved to New York, UK disclosure stopped. An investor memo seen by Forbes gave about $200 million for 2025 and projected $660 million for 2026; one analyst has estimated an annualised run-rate near $900 million, which is not inconsistent if the second half of 2026 is much larger than the first. None of these is confirmed by the company, and the memo's 2026 number is a projection made before most halls had opened.

The important feature is the lag between commitments and revenue described in chapter 05. Lease commitments of more than $20 billion over their base terms and about 1.4 GW of contracted critical IT load sit beside revenue in the hundreds of millions because the halls that produce the revenue are mostly due in late 2026 and 2027. Rent, by contrast, begins on lease commencement. A reader who wants one number to track is therefore not revenue but energised megawatts against the lease schedule, campus by campus.

Debt, and whose it is

Forbes put the debt raised by Fluidstack "and new partners" at over $15 billion, from SEC filings, and the sum of the public pieces supports that: Cipher's notes (about $1.73 billion in the series), Hut 8's loan at up to 85% of cost and its $3.25 billion of notes, TeraWulf's project financing, and the $5.7 billion Indiana bond. Almost none of it is a Fluidstack obligation on paper. It is borrowed by landlords and a joint venture against leases to Fluidstack, and the leases are guaranteed by Google. The one facility that is Fluidstack's own, the Macquarie line, is secured on GPUs and has no disclosed size.

That allocation is the point of the structure and also its fragility. The landlords' bondholders are exposed to Fluidstack's performance as tenant and to Google's willingness to honour the guarantee. Fluidstack is exposed to rent it must pay from lease commencement. Google is exposed to everything, up to limits it has not disclosed in aggregate. A reported $5 billion loan under discussion with the US Department of Defense in September 2026 would, if it closed, be the first large debt in Fluidstack's own name; neither party has confirmed it.

What the balance sheet depends on

  1. Halls opening on schedule. Every month between lease commencement and customer revenue is rent without income. The three-month and six-month build claims are, financially, a statement about this gap.
  2. Anthropic continuing to need the capacity for ten to fifteen years, and continuing to route it through Fluidstack rather than leasing directly.
  3. Google continuing to guarantee. The equity valuation assumes future campuses will be financed the way the first four were. A change in Google's appetite, or in its relationship with Anthropic, changes the cost of every new site.
  4. The miners staying solvent. Landlords with high-yield debt and short AI operating histories are the counterparties on the other side of each lease.

None of these is a reason to doubt the company; all four are the questions a diligence team, a lender or a candidate should ask, and the answers in November 2026 are "not yet shown", "yes so far", "yes so far" and "so far".

Takeaway

  • One announced round: $830 million at $7.5 billion, led by Situational Awareness, closed January 2026. A reported $1.5 billion at over $18 billion followed in September; a $40 billion pitch was reported and disputed in October.
  • Audited revenue stops at over $66 million for 2024; the investor memo's $200 million (2025) and $660 million (2026) are unconfirmed, and revenue lags commitments by design.
  • Over $15 billion of project debt sits with landlords and a joint venture on Google's guarantee; Fluidstack's own disclosed borrowing is a GPU-secured facility of unstated size.
  • The balance sheet depends on halls opening on time, Anthropic's continued demand through Fluidstack, Google's continued guarantees and the landlords' solvency.
