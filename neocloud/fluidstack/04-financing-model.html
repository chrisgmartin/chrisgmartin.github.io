Section C · How it pays

The Financing Model

Customer-backed leases, Google guarantees, bitcoin-miner conversions and project debt.

The problem the structure solves

A gigawatt of AI data centre costs in the order of $50 billion with the chips, and the building and power alone run to several billion per campus. In 2025 Fluidstack had revenue of tens of millions and no credit rating. No lender would finance a $3 billion campus against a ten-year lease from such a tenant, and no landlord would build one on its promise. Yet the customer behind the demand, Anthropic, wanted gigawatts within two years and did not want to own buildings.

The structure that emerged in August 2025 routes credit around the tenant. The landlord builds and borrows; Fluidstack leases and operates; Google guarantees Fluidstack's rent; the customer's chips, increasingly Google's TPUs, fill the halls. Each party puts in the thing it has and borrows the thing it lacks. This chapter takes the structure apart.

Four parties, one lease

  • The landlord (TeraWulf, Cipher, Hut 8, or the Meridian Arc joint venture) owns the site and the interconnection, builds the halls, and borrows most of the cost: notes at 7.125% due 2030 for Cipher, a bank loan at up to 85% of cost for Hut 8, $5.7 billion of 6.25% notes for Indiana. It earns a long triple-net lease with an escalator.
  • The tenant, Fluidstack, signs the lease, designs the fit-out for the customer's chips, and runs the clusters. It commits to payments it could not fund from its own balance sheet, and it keeps the operating relationship with the customer.
  • The guarantor, Google (through an Alphabet subsidiary), backstops Fluidstack's lease payments and pass-through costs for the base term: $1.8 billion then $3.2 billion at Lake Mariner, $1.4 billion then $1.73 billion at Barber Lake, the base-term payments at River Bend, and the lease behind the Indiana notes. In return it receives warrants in the listed landlords, about 14% of TeraWulf pro forma and about 5.4% of Cipher, and at River Bend it also backstops the separate operations agreement.
  • The customer, Anthropic in every case that can be seen, commits to use the capacity. Its contract with Fluidstack is private; its presence is visible through the joint announcements, the Hut 8 partnership "for Anthropic, leveraging clusters operated by Fluidstack", and the Barber Lake water fund.

Lenders and rating agencies then look through the tenant to the guarantor. Fitch's BB on the Indiana notes is explicit that Google's guarantee of Fluidstack's lease obligations supports the rating while Fluidstack is unrated. Hut 8's $3.25 billion of 6.192% notes in April 2026 were rated BBB-, investment grade, on the same logic.

Why Google does it

Three reasons sit in the public record. First, TPU distribution: Anthropic agreed in October 2025 to use up to a million Google TPUs, and the halls Fluidstack builds are where many of them go, so a guarantee that gets a campus financed is a guarantee that sells chips. SemiAnalysis' observation that Fluidstack's focus moved to "bare-metal TPU deployments at 100K+ chip scale" is the same fact from the operator's side. Second, Google is an investor in Anthropic (about 14%, per Data Center Dynamics) and reportedly a shareholder in Fluidstack, so capacity for Anthropic serves its own book. Third, the warrants: struck at a cent in Cipher's case, they give Google equity in landlords whose shares re-rated sharply on the deals, which offsets the cost of the guarantee if nothing goes wrong.

The guarantee has limits that matter to a tenant. Reporting on the TeraWulf terms says the backstop covers the AI lease revenue only, not mining, and that a construction delay beyond 180 days lets Fluidstack terminate without triggering it. Data Center Dynamics' summary of the mechanism is the one to keep: if Fluidstack fails, Google either takes on the capacity or pays a fee.

What the critics say

The sharpest public critique, from the analyst Dave Friedman in February 2026, calls the structure credit substitution: the landlords' bonds are priced on Google's promise, so the market is lending to Google's willingness to pay rather than to any cash flow Fluidstack or the landlord controls. He adds three points worth testing in an interview. GPUs are weak collateral and may lose 40 to 60% of value in three years, which bears on the earlier Macquarie facility more than on the building leases. Google's aggregate exposure is undisclosed, so no one outside Google can size it. And demand is correlated: a slowdown at one frontier lab would hit every backstop at once, because the same customer sits behind most of them.

Two answers are available and neither is complete. The leases are on buildings and power, which hold value better than chips, and the landlords keep the campuses if a tenant fails. And the counterparty whose demand matters, Anthropic, has itself begun signing twenty-year leases directly with landlords, such as the $19 billion Justified campus with TeraWulf in July 2026, which both confirms the demand and shows that the customer does not need Fluidstack in the middle of every deal. Chapter 10 weighs that.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Name the four parties and what each puts in. Which one is unrated, and why does that not stop the financing?

    Show answer

    Landlord: site and debt. Tenant (Fluidstack): lease, fit-out and operations. Guarantor (Google): credit, for warrants. Customer (Anthropic): demand and chips. Fluidstack is unrated; lenders look through it to Google's guarantee. See four parties.

  2. Fitch rated the Indiana notes BB and Hut 8's BBB-. Both rest on the same guarantor. What else could explain the difference?

    Show answer

    Leverage and sponsor: Indiana is about 92% debt with a joint venture of first-time builders and no guaranteed maximum price, while Hut 8 is an established landlord with bank lenders at up to 85% of cost. The guarantee sets the floor, not the whole rating. See the deals.

  3. Why would Google accept warrants in a bitcoin miner rather than cash for a multi-billion guarantee?

    Show answer

    The guarantee sells TPUs and serves a company it part-owns, so it is paid for elsewhere, and cent-strike warrants in landlords whose shares re-rate on the deal are worth more than a fee if nothing goes wrong. See why Google does it.

  4. State the credit-substitution critique in one sentence, then the strongest answer to it.

    Show answer

    Critique: the market is lending to Google's promise, not to cash flows anyone else controls, with undisclosed total exposure and correlated demand. Answer: the collateral is buildings and power, not chips, and the end customer is now signing twenty-year leases directly, which confirms the demand. See what the critics say.

Takeaway

  • The structure routes credit around an unrated tenant: landlord builds and borrows, Fluidstack leases and operates, Google guarantees the rent, Anthropic fills the halls.
  • Public backstops run to about $3.2 billion at Lake Mariner and $1.73 billion at Barber Lake, plus the base terms at River Bend and Indiana; Google's total exposure is undisclosed.
  • Google is paid in warrants, TPU sales and capacity for a company it part-owns; the guarantee has carve-outs for delay and for non-AI revenue.
  • The critique is credit substitution with correlated demand; the partial answer is real-estate collateral and a customer now contracting directly.
