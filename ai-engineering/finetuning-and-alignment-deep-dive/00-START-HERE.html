Section A · Orient

Start Here

Fine-tuning is the most expensive way to change a model's behaviour and the only way to change some of it. This guide is about knowing which case you are in, then doing the work so the result can be defended: the data, the training run, the alignment step, the evaluation and the serving that follows. Start here for the frame, the vocabulary and the two Harbourline models the chapters build.

Why fine-tune, and why it is rarely the first move

Three tools change what a language model does. A prompt changes the instructions and the context the model sees on every call. Retrieval changes which facts are in that context. Fine-tuning changes the weights, so the model behaves differently with no instruction at all. The three are not interchangeable, and most expensive mistakes in this area come from reaching for the third when the first or second would have done.

Fine-tuning reliably changes four things: output format, so a model emits the schema you want without a page of examples; style and register, so a summary reads the way adjusters write; a narrow skill the base model does poorly, such as classifying claims against an internal taxonomy; and cost and latency, because a small model that has been taught the task can replace a large one that is being talked into it. It does not reliably add fresh knowledge, which is retrieval's job, and it does not raise a model's reasoning ceiling. A fine-tuned 8B model that has seen 20,000 claim summaries will write excellent claim summaries and will still not reason about an unusual coverage dispute better than the frontier model it replaced.

The cost is also larger than the training bill. A tuned model is a thing you own: it needs an eval set, a serving path, a retraining cadence and someone on call when the base model it was built on is deprecated. This is why the guide's first rule is that the eval set comes before the training run. If you cannot measure the behaviour you want, you cannot tell whether a prompt already gives it to you, and you cannot tell whether the fine-tune made it better. Chapter 01 turns this into a decision procedure and applies it to two Harbourline systems, choosing to fine-tune one and not the other.

Fine-tune behaviour, retrieve knowledge, prompt everything else

If the thing you want the model to do differently can be written as an instruction, start with the instruction. If it depends on facts that change, retrieve them. Fine-tune when the behaviour is stable, the volume is high enough to pay for owning a model, and the eval set already exists to prove the change.

What post-training is

Every model you can download or call has been through a pipeline, and fine-tuning is the act of running the later stages of that pipeline again on your own data. Pre-training produces a base model: a next-token predictor over trillions of tokens of text that knows a great deal and follows no instructions. Supervised fine-tuning on instruction and response pairs produces an instruct model that answers when asked. Preference tuning on pairs of responses, one preferred over the other, produces the model's judgement about which of two good answers is better. Reinforcement learning against verifiable rewards, the newest stage, is how reasoning models learn to spend tokens thinking before they answer.

A product team almost never pre-trains. It picks a checkpoint that someone else pre-trained and instruction-tuned, then runs supervised fine-tuning on its own pairs and, when the task has a quality dimension that pairs cannot capture, a preference stage on top. Which checkpoint to start from is a decision of its own, covered in chapter 02, and the data for the supervised stage is where most of the work and most of the risk sits, which is why chapter 03 is the longest in the guide.

The vocabulary

The field reuses words loosely. These are the meanings this guide holds to:

TermMeaning here
Base modelA pre-trained next-token predictor with no instruction tuning. Completes text; does not answer questions.
Instruct modelA base model after supervised fine-tuning and usually preference tuning. What "the model" means in product work.
CheckpointA specific set of saved weights, identified by name and revision. Two checkpoints of the same family can behave very differently.
SFTSupervised fine-tuning: training on prompt and response pairs so the model imitates the responses.
Full fine-tuningUpdating every weight. Needs memory for the weights, their gradients and the optimiser state.
LoRA, QLoRATraining small low-rank matrices beside frozen weights (LoRA), optionally with the frozen weights quantised to four bits (QLoRA). Most product fine-tuning is one of these.
AdapterThe trained LoRA matrices, saved separately. Can be merged into the weights or loaded at serving time.
Preference pairOne prompt with two responses and a label saying which is better. The raw material of alignment.
Reward modelA model trained on preference pairs to score responses; used by PPO-style RLHF.
DPODirect preference optimisation: learns from preference pairs without a separate reward model or RL loop.
GRPO, RLVRReinforcement learning methods that score sampled responses against a verifiable reward (a test passes, an answer matches) rather than a learned one.
Held-out setExamples the model never trains on, used to measure it. Built before training, frozen, versioned.
Catastrophic forgettingLoss of general capability or safety behaviour as a side effect of training on a narrow task.
Distillation, mergingTeaching a small model from a large one's outputs; combining two trained models' weights without training.

The running example: Harbourline Insurance

Harbourline is a fictional mid-size property-and-casualty insurer, about two million policies in the US and UK, shared with the other AI Engineering guides. Two of its systems are candidates for fine-tuning, and the guide follows both so that the decision in chapter 01 has a yes and a no. All figures are illustrative.

CandidateWhat it does todayWhat fine-tuning would changeDecision
A: nightly claims batchClassifies and summarises about 6,000 claims a night for adjusters on Claude Sonnet 5.5 through the API; 18 months of adjuster-edited summaries exist, about 40,000 pairs before filteringA tuned open-weight model in the 7B to 9B class writes summaries in the adjusters' register at a fraction of the per-claim cost, hosted in the UKFine-tune, because UK claims data must stay in the UK; chapter 01 shows the cost case alone does not carry it (chapters 02 to 09)
B: claims-intake agentReads a first notice of loss, asks for what is missing, checks coverage, routes to an adjuster; the complaint is tone and output formatA tuned model would hold the format without a long promptDo not fine-tune: a tighter prompt and retrieval of the format examples meet the eval (chapter 01)

Candidate A gives the guide its numbers: a 40,000-pair corpus that filtering reduces to about 22,000, a held-out set of 400 claims sliced by claim type and region, about 6,000 adjuster preference pairs for the alignment step, and one rented H100 for the training runs. Candidate B is there so that every later chapter can say what it would have cost to be wrong about the first decision.

Takeaway

  • Prompts change instructions, retrieval changes facts, fine-tuning changes weights; pick the cheapest tool that the eval shows is enough.
  • Fine-tuning reliably changes format, style, a narrow skill and the size of model you need; it does not add fresh knowledge or raise the reasoning ceiling.
  • A tuned model is something you own: an eval set, a serving path, a retraining cadence and a dependency on a base checkpoint someone else controls.
  • Build the held-out set before the training run, or you cannot tell whether the run helped.
  • Product teams run the later stages of the same pipeline that produced the model: supervised fine-tuning on their own pairs, sometimes preference tuning on top, never pre-training.
