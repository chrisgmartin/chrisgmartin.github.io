Section A · Orient

Start Here

AI engineer interviews in 2026 have converged on a small set of questions. This guide works sixteen of them, one per chapter, on a single running example so the answers reinforce each other. Start here for how the loop is scored, who Harbourline is, and how to get the most out of each chapter.

What the loop tests

Whatever the title says (AI engineer, applied AI engineer, LLM platform engineer, forward-deployed engineer), the loop is built around one worry: can this person put a probabilistic model into a system that other people depend on, and know when it is working? Every round is a different angle on that worry.

Coding rounds still exist and are usually ordinary: data structures in Python, sometimes an async retry loop or a small pipeline. They are not covered here; the data-engineering and compliance guides on this site have drill sets if you need them.

How answers are scored

Interviewers in these loops are rarely working from a key. They are listening for five things, and a strong answer shows all five in the first two minutes:

  1. Scope. You ask what the system is for, who is harmed when it is wrong, and what the volumes are, before you design. "What happens if this answer is wrong?" is the single highest-value question you can ask in any round.
  2. Mechanism. You explain why something fails (the embedding never saw the policy number as a token; the model cannot see a passage ranked eleventh when k is ten) rather than naming a fix from a list.
  3. Trade-off. Every recommendation comes with its price and the case where it flips. A reranker buys recall and costs 150 ms. Say both.
  4. Measurement. You name the metric, the set it is measured on, and the number that would change your mind.
  5. Failure instinct. You reach for "what breaks first" before "what is possible", and you put the human in the loop where the cost of error is highest.
The answer is a conversation, not a speech

The chapters give you a structured answer for each question. Do not deliver it as a monologue. State the structure in one sentence ("I'd split this into retrieval failures and generation failures and bisect"), then let the interviewer choose where to go deep. The "Interview probes" block in each chapter is the list of places they usually choose.

The running example: Harbourline Insurance

Every chapter uses the same fictional company, so that the design in chapter 06 reuses the retrieval you debugged in chapter 01 and the evals from chapter 07 decide whether chapter 15's fix shipped. Harbourline is a mid-size property-and-casualty insurer with about two million policies across the US and UK. Its AI programme has six surfaces:

The numbers are there so you can do arithmetic out loud. When a chapter says "at 40k sessions a day, caching the system prompt saves roughly X", that is the kind of sentence that separates a candidate who has run a system from one who has read about it. Use your own numbers in the interview; the point is to have some.

How to work a chapter

Each chapter has the same shape, and the shape is the study method:

  1. The question and what it scores. Read this, close the page, and give your current answer aloud for two minutes. Notice where you run out.
  2. The mechanism. This is the part to understand rather than memorise. If you can explain why a failure happens, you can answer the variant you were not prepared for.
  3. The worked answer on Harbourline. Read it for structure, not words. Your own answer should use your own system.
  4. Interview probes. Turn on drill mode, answer each probe aloud, then open the model answer. These are the follow-ups interviewers actually ask.
  5. Check yourself. Scenario questions with folded answers. Answer before opening. If you miss one, the answer links back to the section.
  6. Takeaway and Further reading. The claims to carry into the room, and the primary sources if you want the depth.
Say it out loud

Reading an answer and being able to give one under questioning are different skills. The probes and the Check yourself blocks only work if you speak the answer before you look. A silent "yes, I know that" is the most common way to arrive at the interview unprepared.

Takeaway

  • Every round in an AI engineer loop is a different angle on one question: can you put a probabilistic model into a system people depend on, and know when it is working?
  • Strong answers show scope, mechanism, trade-off, measurement and failure instinct within the first two minutes, then let the interviewer pick the depth.
  • "What happens if this answer is wrong?" is the first question to ask in any design or debugging round.
  • One running example with real volumes lets you do arithmetic aloud, which is the clearest signal that you have operated a system rather than read about one.
  • The chapters are a method: answer first, read second, drill the probes aloud, then check yourself.
