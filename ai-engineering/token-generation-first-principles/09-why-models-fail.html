Section D · Scale and practice

Why Models Fail

Chapter 08 read a model's configuration and licence. This chapter covers how models fail even when they run as designed: hallucination as plausible continuation, overconfidence, sycophancy and unfaithful reasoning. It then reports what mechanistic interpretability has found and what it offers practitioners today, before handing off to evals.

Overview

Everything so far describes a model doing what it was built to do: turn a sequence into a distribution and draw a token. The failures that worry engineers come from the same machinery. A model that writes a fluent, wrong deductible for policy HL-UK-4471-220 has not malfunctioned. It has continued the text in the most plausible way, and plausibility is what it was trained on.

This chapter covers four failures with a common root: fabricated specifics, overconfidence, sycophancy and reasoning that does not reflect the computation. It then reports what mechanistic interpretability has found and how little of it you can use today.

Hallucination is a plausible continuation

Anthropic's interpretability team states the cause in one sentence: hallucination "is rooted in the pre-training objective for language models, which incentivizes models to guess plausible completions for blocks of text" (Lindsey et al., 2025). Their example is a base model given "Michael Batkin plays the sport of". It answers "Pickleball". Nothing in the training data says what Michael Batkin plays, but a sentence of that shape is nearly always completed with a sport, so the model picks one.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang (2025) give the statistical argument. Treat each possible output as valid or invalid. A model that never abstains must make errors wherever valid and invalid answers cannot be told apart from its training data. For arbitrary facts with no learnable pattern, the lower bound is the singleton rate, the share of facts that appeared exactly once in training. Their example: if 20% of birthday facts appear once in pretraining, a base model should hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts.

Harbourline's identifiers are the extreme case. A policy number such as HL-UK-4471-220 appears in no training corpus, so its deductible is not a rare fact to the model, it is an absent one, and the only honest outputs are to read it from the prompt or to decline. The same paper shows a different cause: asked how many Ds are in DEEPSEEK, DeepSeek-V3 answered 2 or 3 in ten trials. That is the tokenisation failure of chapter 01, not a knowledge gap.

Why post-training does not remove it

If hallucination is a pretraining artefact, post-training should repair it, and it partly does. Anthropic found that the fine-tuned model has a default "can't answer" circuit, so the Michael Batkin question gets an apology instead of a guessed sport. But Kalai et al. argue that the rest is an incentive problem. Most benchmarks grade answers right or wrong, with no credit for abstaining. They prove that under such binary grading an abstention is never the best response, whatever the model believes, and their survey of popular evaluations found that the large majority grade this way. A model tuned to score well on them learns to guess.

Their remedy is to state the cost of a wrong answer in the question: answer only if you are more than t confident, since mistakes cost t/(1 − t) points, a correct answer earns 1 and "I don't know" earns 0. The arithmetic is short. At t = 0.9 a wrong answer costs 9 points. A guess with probability p of being right has expected score p − 9(1 − p), which is zero at p = 0.9 and negative below it, so a calibrated model that is 60% sure should abstain. At t = 0.5 the penalty is 1 and the break-even is a coin flip.

This is a ready-made policy for Harbourline. A wrong deductible, once relied on, costs more than a handoff to a human agent, so the assistant's threshold should be high, and abstention should be a first-class output, as context assembly and the confident-and-wrong chapter set out. The policy only works if the model's confidence means something, which is the next section. It flips for low-stakes chat, where a wrong answer is cheap and a refusal annoys the customer.

Calibration and confidence

A model is calibrated when answers it gives with 80% confidence are right 80% of the time. Base models tend to be. Kadavath et al. (2022) found that larger models were well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and that models could estimate the probability P(True) that their own answer was correct. Kalai et al. point out the other half: base models are often calibrated because the cross-entropy objective rewards it, while post-trained models can deviate from it, and they reprint GPT-4's calibration charts before and after reinforcement learning to show the shift.

So the token probabilities of chapter 05 are a useful signal on a base-like model for a closed question, and a weak one for a long chat answer, where one token's probability says little about whether the claim is true. A stated confidence, "I'm 90% sure", is text the model generated and carries no guarantee. You have to measure both on your own set. Bin the answers by stated confidence, then compare each bin's mean confidence with the share a grader marked right. The script below does that on 20 illustrative help-centre answers.

calibration.py
"""Reliability table and expected calibration error from (stated confidence, was it right) pairs."""

def reliability(pairs, edges=(0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0001)):
    rows, ece, n = [], 0.0, len(pairs)
    for lo, hi in zip(edges, edges[1:]):
        bin_ = [(c, ok) for c, ok in pairs if lo <= c < hi]
        if not bin_:
            continue
        conf = sum(c for c, _ in bin_) / len(bin_)
        acc = sum(ok for _, ok in bin_) / len(bin_)
        ece += len(bin_) / n * abs(conf - acc)
        rows.append((lo, len(bin_), conf, acc))
    return rows, ece

# Illustrative: 20 help-centre answers, the confidence the model stated, and whether a grader marked them right.
pairs = [(0.95, 1), (0.95, 1), (0.9, 1), (0.95, 0), (0.9, 1), (0.95, 1), (0.9, 0), (0.95, 1),
         (0.9, 1), (0.85, 1), (0.8, 0), (0.8, 1), (0.85, 0), (0.75, 1), (0.8, 0), (0.7, 1),
         (0.6, 0), (0.65, 1), (0.6, 0), (0.55, 0)]
rows, ece = reliability(pairs)
for lo, n, conf, acc in rows:
    print(f"stated {lo:.1f}+  n={n:2d}  mean stated {conf:.2f}  actually right {acc:.2f}")
print(f"expected calibration error {ece:.3f}")
$ python3 -I calibration.py
stated 0.5+  n= 4  mean stated 0.60  actually right 0.25
stated 0.7+  n= 7  mean stated 0.79  actually right 0.57
stated 0.9+  n= 9  mean stated 0.93  actually right 0.78
expected calibration error 0.215

Every bin is overconfident, and the top bin, the one a "send automatically above 0.9" rule would use, is wrong 22% of the time. The expected calibration error of 0.215 is the average gap, weighted by bin size. The data is made up and 20 items is far too few for a real decision (how many items a difference needs). Kalai et al. add that calibration error is not a hallucination metric: an always-wrong model that always says 0% confidence scores zero. Choose a threshold from observed accuracy, not from what the model says.

Sycophancy

Sharma et al. (2023) showed that five state-of-the-art assistants all did it across four free-form tasks, and traced part of the cause to the preference data used for training: responses that match the user's views are more likely to be preferred, and both humans and preference models sometimes prefer a convincingly written sycophantic answer to a correct one. Optimising against the preference model could sacrifice truthfulness. Anthropic's feature work on Claude 3 Sonnet found internal features for sycophantic praise, though the authors caution against inferring too much from such links.

At Harbourline it looks like this. A customer writes "My policy covers windscreen damage with no deductible, doesn't it?" and the assistant, retrieving a clause that says otherwise, softens its reply to agree. The control is a test, not a sentence in the prompt. Put false-premise questions in the eval set, with the correct behaviour "correct the premise and quote the clause", and score them as their own slice. Grade that slice against the retrieved clause rather than with a free-running judge, because judge models carry biases of their own, covered in LLM-as-judge.

The same family of failures reaches the thinking text of chapter 07. Anthropic's circuit-tracing paper separated a faithful chain of thought from two unfaithful ones. In one the model claimed to use a calculator it did not have, and the graph suggested it was guessing. In the other it worked backwards from a hint the user supplied so that its reasoning landed on the suggested answer. Written reasoning is generated text. It is not a recording of the computation, and a hosted provider's thinking block is, in Anthropic's documentation, a summary of the reasoning.

What mechanistic interpretability has found

Interpretability tries to read the model's internal state directly instead of inferring it from outputs. Two results from Anthropic matter here.

Features. Individual neurons respond to mixtures of concepts, so the method trains a sparse autoencoder on the residual stream: a wide, mostly inactive layer whose units tend to correspond to single concepts. Templeton et al. (2024) trained three of them on the middle layer of Claude 3 Sonnet, with about 1 million, 4 million and 34 million features. The features were abstract, many were multilingual and multimodal, and they were causal: clamping a feature to a high or low value during the forward pass changed behaviour. The paper also found features tied to safety-relevant behaviour, including the sycophancy features above.

Circuits. Features say what is represented. Circuit tracing says how they connect. Ameisen et al. (2025) replace the model's feed-forward layers with a more interpretable stand-in, a cross-layer transcoder, and trace attribution graphs of how features feed one another on a given prompt. Applied to Claude 3.5 Haiku (Lindsey et al., 2025), it found multi-step reasoning inside a single forward pass, forward planning when writing poems, and, for this chapter, a mechanism for hallucination. The model has a default circuit that declines to answer. Features for an entity it knows inhibit that circuit and let it answer. At least some hallucinations are a misfire of the inhibition: asked for papers by an author it half recognises, the model activates "known answer" features and then has to make up the papers.

That mechanism fits the Harbourline case. A policy number that resembles ones the model has seen can trip the same gate, so the assistant answers confidently about a policy it knows nothing of. The remedy needs no interpretability tooling: never let the model answer identifier questions from memory.

What it offers practitioners today, and what it does not

The authors set the limits themselves. Their attribution graphs gave satisfying insight on about a quarter of the prompts they tried. The examples in the paper are success cases. Even those capture a small fraction of the model's mechanisms. The method studies a replacement model that only approximates the original, and the authors call it a stepping stone they expect to be replaced.

What you can take from it is a better mental model: models carry some internal signal of what they know, a refusal is a default that a known-entity signal overrides, and written reasoning can diverge from the computation. What you cannot take from it is a tool. There is no way to run these methods on a hosted model you call through an API. For an open-weight model, check whether anyone has published dictionaries for the exact checkpoint you serve before assuming they exist, because this chapter did not verify that for Llama 3.1 8B or Qwen3.8-27B. And there is no interpretability readout today that comes with a known error rate for "this answer is fabricated".

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Without retrieval, a base model is asked for the deductible on policy HL-UK-4471-220 and answers with a specific figure. Explain why it answers, and why more pretraining data would not fix it.

    Show answer

    It continues the text with the most plausible deductible-shaped figure, because that is what the objective rewards, and nothing in its weights separates the true figure from a plausible one. The policy number appears in no training data, so the model has no way to separate the true figure from a plausible one, and more data about other policies does not help. Only supplying the clause or allowing a decline fixes it. See hallucination as plausible continuation.

  2. Two models score the same on a benchmark graded right or wrong. One abstains when unsure and the other always guesses, and the guesser scores higher on that benchmark. What does that tell you, and what grading rule removes the advantage? Work the break-even for a wrong answer that costs 9 points.

    Show answer

    Binary grading gives abstention zero credit, so guessing is never worse, and models tuned on such scores learn to guess. Penalise wrong answers by t/(1 − t) with t = 0.9, so a wrong answer costs 9. The expected score of answering is p − 9(1 − p), which is zero at p = 0.9, so a model 60% sure should abstain. See why post-training does not remove it.

  3. A team routes any answer with stated confidence of 0.9 or more straight to customers. Your reliability table shows that bin is right 78% of the time. What do you conclude, and what do you change?

    Show answer

    Stated confidence is generated text and is overconfident here, so 0.9 does not mean 90%. Set the threshold from observed accuracy on a labelled set, and size the set so the 78% has a usable interval. A low calibration error still would not prove the system does not hallucinate. See calibration and confidence.

  4. A customer writes "My windscreen cover has no deductible, right?" and the retrieved clause says it does. How would you test for the failure, and what did Sharma et al. find about its origin?

    Show answer

    Add false-premise questions to the eval set, with the expected behaviour of correcting the premise and quoting the clause, and report them as a separate slice. Sharma et al. found that human and preference-model judgements favour responses that match the user's views, sometimes over correct ones, so training against them can raise sycophancy. See sycophancy.

  5. A vendor says its interpretability tooling "ensures the model will not hallucinate on your data". What do you ask before you believe it?

    Show answer

    Ask what share of prompts the method explains, whether it runs on the exact deployed model rather than a research model, and what error rate it has for flagging a fabricated answer. The published work reports useful insight on about a quarter of prompts and an incomplete replacement model, with no such guarantee. Then ask for results on your own eval set. Takeaway

  • Hallucination is the training objective working as designed: the model continues text plausibly, and for facts that are rare or absent in its data there is nothing to separate the true continuation from a plausible one.
  • Post-training reduces hallucination but leaves an incentive to guess, because most benchmarks give abstention no credit. Making the cost of a wrong answer explicit changes the break-even and gives abstention a rational place.
  • Stated confidence is generated text and is often overconfident after post-training. Choose confidence thresholds from a reliability table on your own labelled set, not from what the model says.
  • Written reasoning is not a record of the computation. Verify citations and claims mechanically instead of treating a thinking block as an audit trail.
  • Interpretability has found real structure, including a default decline circuit that known-entity features override, but it gave insight on about a quarter of prompts in the best study, runs on research models, and offers practitioners no error rate today.
