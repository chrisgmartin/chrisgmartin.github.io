Section B · Threats

Jailbreaks and Safety Bypass

Role-play, encoding, many-shot and multi-turn attacks, adversarial suffixes, what model providers defend against, what an application team must still do, and measuring attack success rate.

What a jailbreak is, and what it is not

A jailbreak is an attack on the model's own safety behaviour. The user wants the model to produce something its training or your policy says it should refuse, and they supply the pressure themselves. Prompt injection, from the last chapter, redirects the application's task using text the user may never see. The two overlap, since a jailbreak phrase can ride inside an injected document, but the questions you ask differ. For a jailbreak: what is the worst thing this model will say or do for a user who wants it to? For an injection: what can this stream of text make the agent do on someone else's behalf?

That split drives the economics later in the chapter. A jailbreak against an agent with no tools produces words. The same jailbreak against an agent with a payment tool produces an action, and you are back in the territory of excessive agency.

Why safety training can be bypassed

Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt (2023) asked why safety-trained models fail and gave two modes that still organise the attack catalogue. Competing objectives: the model is trained to follow instructions and to predict plausible text, and those goals pull against the safety goal, so an attacker can build a prompt where compliance looks like the better completion. Mismatched generalisation: pretraining gave the model capabilities in domains where the safety training never reached, so a request in an unusual form is understood but not refused. They built attacks from the two modes and report success on over 96% of the prompts they evaluated against the models of that time, including on a curated set of 32 prompts that earlier safety work had targeted.

The relevance for a builder is that jailbreaks are not a list of known strings that a patch removes. They exploit gaps between what the model can do and what it was taught to refuse, and the gap moves with each new capability. Treat the model's refusals as a probabilistic layer that raises the cost of misuse, not as a boundary.

The families, and what each tells you to build

Five families cover the public literature. The descriptions below stay at the level you need to test for them.

Role-play and persona

The request is wrapped in a fiction: the model plays a character with no rules, or an expert in a scenario where the harmful content is "just dialogue". Wei et al. group these under competing objectives, since staying in character competes with refusing. The framing is often long and unusual, but do not rely on spotting it. Your defence is on the output side: judge what the model produced, not how it was asked.

Encoding and obfuscation

The request is transformed so the model can read it and the safety layer does not recognise it: an encoding such as Base64, a low-resource language, character substitution. Wei et al. evaluated a Base64 attack and attribute its success to the gap between broad pretraining and narrower safety training, the mismatched-generalisation mode. A text filter on the input misses what it cannot decode. Normalise before you classify: decode common encodings and translate, or classify the output as well.

Many-shot jailbreaking

Anthropic (April 2024) described a prompt that contains a long run of faked dialogue in which an assistant answers harmful questions, followed by the real request. In their tests it followed a power law: more shots made harmful output more likely, with up to 256 shots tried, and larger models needed fewer. It exploits long context windows, which grew from about 4,000 tokens at the start of 2023 to a million or more. Their finding on defences is useful. Limiting context length stops it but costs the long-context benefit, fine-tuning to refuse only delayed the effect, and classifying and modifying the prompt before it reached the model cut one attack from 61% success to 2%. If you accept long user-supplied context, a classifier in front of the model is warranted.

Multi-turn escalation

Crescendo (Russinovich, Salem and Eldan, 2024) starts with a general, harmless question and escalates over several turns, each referring to the model's earlier replies. They report high success across the models they tested, including ChatGPT, Gemini and Llama-2 and Llama-3 70B chat models, and their automated tool outperformed other jailbreak techniques on a subset of AdvBench. The point for defenders is that no single message looks bad. A per-message filter sees nothing; you need to evaluate the conversation, or the model's output, as a whole.

Adversarial suffixes

Zou et al. (2023) showed that a string of tokens, found by automated greedy and gradient-based search, appended to a request makes an aligned model likely to begin with an affirmative reply. A suffix optimised on open-weight Vicuna models transferred to black-box systems including ChatGPT, Bard and Claude, and to LLaMA-2-Chat, Pythia and Falcon. These suffixes read as gibberish, so a perplexity or format check can flag them, but an attacker who knows the check can search for suffixes that pass it. The takeaway is that the attack is automated and cheap once the search is built, so assume any single-model refusal can be argued with at scale.

What providers do, and where it stops

Model vendors train refusals into the model, which preference alignment covers, and they ship classifiers around it. Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers paper (Sharma et al., January 2025) is the clearest public measurement. The classifiers are trained on synthetic data generated from a written constitution of permitted and restricted content. In Anthropic's account, on 10,000 synthetic jailbreak prompts the success rate fell from 86% without classifiers to 4.4% with them, refusals on harmless queries rose by 0.38%, which was not statistically significant in a 5,000-conversation sample, and compute rose 23.7%. A prototype bug bounty with 183 participants and over 3,000 hours found no universal jailbreak. A later public demo in February 2025, with 339 jailbreakers, produced one universal jailbreak.

Read that as two facts. A well-resourced vendor can cut the rate sharply at a modest cost. And a motivated public still found a universal bypass during the week-long demo, so the layer is strong but not closed. The numbers are the vendor's own, on its own models, in early 2025, and your provider may differ.

The gap that matters to you is scope. Provider safeguards target broad harm categories. They know nothing of Harbourline's rules: that the claims agent must never state a loss is covered, that the help-centre assistant must not quote another tenant's terms, that no agent gives legal advice. A model that refuses to explain weapon synthesis may still draft a coverage promise if asked in a way that looks helpful. Those rules are yours to enforce.

What an application team must still do

  1. Write your policy as testable rules. A list of things the assistant must not say or do, each short enough to turn into a check. "Never state a loss is covered" is testable. "Be responsible" is not.
  2. Classify the output against that policy, using a model or rules, before it reaches the customer or a tool. Output checks hold across role-play, encoding and multi-turn because they judge the result. Chapter 07 covers the classifier options.
  3. Keep authority out of the model's hands. Authorise tool calls in code, so that a jailbroken model asking for an action is refused by the API, exactly as in chapter 01.
  4. Rate-limit and track by account. Jailbreaking by search needs many attempts. A per-account limit and an alert on repeated refusals raise the cost, and the logs give you the attack corpus for testing.
  5. Handle long context and multi-turn explicitly. Cap user-supplied context where the product does not need it, and evaluate the whole conversation for escalation instead of the last message.

The exception is a closed product. A model that classifies claims into six fixed labels and returns JSON has little room to jailbreak into anything harmful, so a strict output schema beats a heavy classifier there.

Measuring attack success rate

Attack success rate (ASR) is the share of attempts that elicit the forbidden behaviour. It needs three decisions: the attempt set, the success criterion, and the interval.

Two public benchmarks give you a common yardstick. HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) is a standardised framework for automated red teaming; it compares 18 red-teaming methods against 33 target models and defences and includes an adversarial-training method that improves robustness. JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024) supplies 100 behaviours, a repository of adversarial prompts, a fixed threat model, chat templates and scoring functions, and a leaderboard. Use them to compare models or defences on equal terms. They do not measure Harbourline's rules, so build a second set from your own policy, with a few dozen prompts per rule.

Success needs a grader. A judge model reading the output is usual, and it is an instrument with biases, so calibrate it against human labels first, as the evals guide describes. Then report the interval, because ASR is a proportion from a sample. With the Wilson interval at 95%, 12 successes in 200 attempts is 6.0%, with a range from 3.5% to 10.2%. Zero successes in 200 does not mean zero risk: the upper bound is still about 1.9%.

Fix the attacker too. Nasr et al. (2025) showed that defences reporting near-zero ASR against fixed prompts lost to adaptive attacks, mostly at over 90% success. An ASR measured against your own static prompt list measures that list. Re-run it whenever the model, the prompt or the defence changes, which is the loop in chapter 08.

The ordinary jailbreak costs tens of dollars a day because the model has no tools and the harm stays inside one conversation. The tail is what matters. At 2 successes a day, a 1-in-2,000 chance that one goes public is 2 ÷ 2,000 = 0.001 a day, or one event every 1,000 days, which at $50,000 is $50 a day. The tail doubles the expected cost and concentrates it in a bad week. That is why the brand matters in the asset list, and why an output classifier on a public-facing assistant earns its cost even when the average loss looks small.

The flip: attach a write tool and the jailbreak starts to resemble the injection row. A customer who jailbreaks the agent into changing their own address has gained nothing, but one who jailbreaks it into a refund they are not owed has, which is again fixed by authorising the tool in code. Spend the first effort where the money is, which is the write path, and the second on the output policy checks that guard the brand.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Explain the difference between competing objectives and mismatched generalisation, and give a family of attack that exploits each.

    Show answer

    Competing objectives: instruction-following and plausible-text goals pull against safety, and role-play exploits that. Mismatched generalisation: the model has capabilities that safety training did not cover, and encoded requests exploit that. See why safety training can be bypassed.

  2. A user pastes 200 faked dialogues into a long-context request. Which family is this, and which defence did Anthropic's study find most effective?

    Show answer

    Many-shot jailbreaking. Classifying and modifying the prompt before the model saw it cut one attack's success from 61% to 2%. Limiting context length would work but loses long-context benefits, and fine-tuning alone only delayed the effect. See the families.

  3. Your provider reports that its classifiers cut jailbreak success from 86% to 4.4%. Why does that not settle whether the Harbourline claims agent may promise coverage?

    Show answer

    The classifiers target broad harm categories, and the measurement is the vendor's own, on its own prompts, in early 2025. A coverage promise is a business rule outside that scope, so you enforce it with your own output checks. See what providers do.

  4. A red team runs 200 attempts against your output classifier and finds 0 successes. What can you claim, and what must you add?

    Show answer

    That the rate is probably below roughly 1.9% at 95% confidence, not that it is zero. Add adaptive attempts that are tuned to the classifier, since a static set measures only itself. See measuring attack success rate.

  5. Why does a per-message input filter miss multi-turn escalation, and what do you evaluate instead?

    Show answer

    Each message is mild and the harm lies in the trajectory. Evaluate the whole conversation or the output produced, so the check sees what the model was led to say. See multi-turn escalation.

  6. Using the illustrative table, why does the jailbreak cost roughly $100 a day while the injection costs $2,700, and what single change would make the jailbreak resemble the injection?

    Show answer

    The jailbreak assistant has no tools, so harm stays in one conversation, with a small public-incident tail. Give the jailbroken agent a write tool without code-level authorisation and its cost per success approaches the injection's. See the cost comparison.

Takeaway

  • A jailbreak attacks the model's own safety behaviour, an injection redirects the application's task, and the harm from either is set by what tools the model can reach.
  • Safety training fails through competing objectives and mismatched generalisation, so refusals are a probabilistic layer that raises the cost of misuse and are not a boundary.
  • Role-play, encoding, many-shot, multi-turn and adversarial-suffix attacks each need a different check, and output-side classification covers all five because it judges the result.
  • Provider classifiers cut jailbreak success sharply, from 86% to 4.4% in one vendor's test, but they cover broad harms and not your business rules.
  • Report attack success rate with an interval, against an attacker that adapts, because zero successes in 200 attempts still allows a rate near 2%.
  • The expensive failures are the ones that reach a write tool, so authorise tool calls in code and spend the first effort there.
