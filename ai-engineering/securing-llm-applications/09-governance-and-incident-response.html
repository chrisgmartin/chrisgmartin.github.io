Section D · Defence and assurance

Governance and Incident Response

Logging for forensics, the incident playbook for an injection that landed, disclosure, and the regulation and standards an application team must map to as of November 2026.

Overview

Defences fail, and red teams do not find everything. Chapter 08 ended with a permit decision, and the decision will sometimes be wrong. What separates a bad week from a bad year is whether you can answer four questions within hours: what did the agent do, to whom, how did the attacker get in, and who must be told.

This chapter works through the logs that answer those questions, a playbook for an injection that landed, the notification clocks, and the rules and frameworks that now apply, as of November 2026. It is engineering guidance, not legal advice. The people who decide what Harbourline must report are its lawyers and its data protection officer, and your job is to give them facts quickly.

Logging for forensics

Start from the question the log must answer after the fact. Take the claim PDF attack. A customer reports that their payout went to the wrong account. To reconstruct the event you need to link five things: the upload, the text the model saw, the tool call it made, the value in that call and where the value came from, and the human who confirmed or did not. If any link is missing, you fall back on guessing from the account change alone.

So record per agent step, keyed by a request ID that follows the session:

  • the version of the prompt, the model identifier and the tool schemas, so you can replay the run;
  • a hash of every document and tool result read, with the content itself in a separate restricted store;
  • every tool call with its arguments and the provenance tag of each argument, from the rule in chapter 07;
  • classifier labels and scores, and any guard that blocked or flagged;
  • the confirmation shown to the human, their decision and their identity.

The hash lets you prove which file the agent read without copying customer documents into the observability tool, and that matters because traces are a leak surface of their own, as chapter 05 covered. Redact personal data before it reaches the searchable index, and keep the raw record in a store with tighter access and a deletion schedule. The compliance guide treats the same tension in its audit trail chapter: completeness pulls toward keeping everything, and data protection pulls toward keeping little.

Retention is a choice with two costs. Too short, and a slow fraud that surfaces in month five has no trace. Too long, and you hold personal data you cannot justify and make a breach larger. An illustrative Harbourline split: 90 days for full traces, 13 months for the metadata and hashes that identify who, what and when, and longer only for records tied to a claim that the business must keep anyway. The EU AI Act gives one reference point. For high-risk systems it requires logs kept for at least six months, for providers in Article 19 and for deployers in Article 26(6), unless other law says otherwise. Harbourline's chat and claims agents are probably not high-risk under the Act, as the regulation section below explains, so six months is a benchmark and not a duty. Your counsel sets the number.

Logs written after the incident are not evidence

Decide what to capture before launch, since you cannot add a missing field to an event that already happened. A tool call logged without the source of its arguments leaves you unable to tell an injection from a user mistake, and that is the first question the incident team asks.

The incident playbook

Five steps, in order, with owners named in advance. The times are illustrative targets for a high-severity event.

1. CONTAIN first hour disable the write tool or set it to human-only; revoke leaked credentials; freeze the payout batch │ 2. ASSESS hours 1-24 which sessions, which documents, which tools │ ran with attacker-sourced values; which │ customers and which data were touched │ 3. NOTIFY clock runs privacy, legal, affected customers, regulators │ as the law requires (next section) │ 4. FIX days close the path, not the instance: rule, │ schema, allowlist, model or prompt change │ 5. RE-TEST before restore replay the attack plus variants; add to the regression suite; sign off the permit decision

Contain by switching off the capability, not the whole product. Because chapter 07 put the write behind a policy check and a feature flag, Harbourline can drop the payment-method tool to "propose only" and keep chat running. If the tool were fused into the agent's prompt, the only switch would be the agent. Hold any payments already queued to the changed account until reconciled.

Assess by querying the logs from the previous section for the signature of the attack, not only the report you received. Search every session where a payout argument carried a document-sourced tag, or where a canary string appeared, or where the same document hash was uploaded under several accounts. One reported victim usually means several unreported ones. Scope the result as a count of customers, a list of fields exposed and a time window, since each of those feeds a notification decision.

Notify starts its clocks at awareness, not at proof, so open the notification question in hour one and let legal stop it if it proves unnecessary.

Fix the class. If the cause was a document that said "change the account", patching that phrase is the instance. The class is that a document-sourced value reached a write, and the fix is the provenance rule, or its repair if it had a gap. Re-test with the original attack, then variants a human writes, using the process in chapter 08, and restore the capability only when the permit rule is met. Hold a blameless review within a week and write down the timeline. Each landed attack is a case for the suite.

Telling customers and regulators

Two kinds of duty usually apply to an injection that moves money or exposes data. The first is data protection. Under Article 33 of the UK GDPR, a controller must notify a personal data breach to the regulator "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it", unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people's rights and freedoms, and a late notice must give its reasons. A payout account changed through an agent, with customer names and addresses exposed in the same session, will often meet that bar. The decision belongs to Harbourline's data protection officer, and the 72 hours run from the moment the team became aware, which is why hour one matters.

The second is sector and contract duty. Insurers answer to their financial regulators, and broker tenants will have contract terms on notice. The specific rules of insurance regulators are outside what this chapter verified, so treat this as a prompt to ask legal for the list. Build the list before the incident and keep it with the playbook, with a named contact and a deadline beside each entry.

On customer communication, say what happened, what data or money was involved, what you have done and what the customer should do, in plain words and without a defence of the technology. If the cause was a prompt injection, say that an attacker tricked an automated system, because an account of "a technical issue" will not survive the first press enquiry. Do not publish attack detail beyond what lets people protect themselves until the fix is verified. Good-faith external reporters who found the flaw should hear back from you, as chapter 08 set out.

EU AI Act as of November 2026

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024. The Commission's timeline page gives these dates: prohibited practices and AI literacy duties from 2 February 2025, general-purpose model duties from 2 August 2025, and general application from 2 August 2026, when the transparency rules also take effect. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, adopted on 8 July 2026 and published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, moved the high-risk dates. Obligations for Annex III systems now apply from 2 December 2027, and for systems embedded in regulated products from 2 August 2028. The Commission page also lists a new prohibition from 2 December 2026, on systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit content or child sexual abuse material.

What does that mean for Harbourline? Insurance appears in Annex III, in point 5(c): AI systems intended for risk assessment and pricing in relation to natural persons in the case of life and health insurance. Harbourline writes property and casualty cover, and its agents handle service, intake and routing, not pricing. On that reading, none of the six surfaces is an Annex III system today, which is a point for counsel to confirm and not for engineers to assume. Two things would change the answer: adding an underwriting or pricing model for life or health lines, and letting the claims-intake agent make decisions that a regulator reads as risk assessment.

Some duties apply regardless. Article 50(1) requires that people are told they are dealing with an AI system unless it is obvious to a reasonably well-informed person, so the customer-service chat needs a visible disclosure. If a system is high-risk, the logging rule above applies, and Article 73 sets serious-incident reporting: not later than 15 days after awareness in the general case, not later than two days for a widespread infringement or a serious incident of the kind Article 3(49)(b) defines, and not later than 10 days where a person has died. Those clocks run for providers and deployers of high-risk systems only, but they are a useful model for your internal targets. The flip side is that the dates have already moved once, so check the Commission page before you plan around them.

NIST, ISO and the UK

Frameworks give a structure that an auditor, a customer or a broker recognises. Three matter here.

  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, published 26 January 2023, organises the work into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. Its Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1 of 26 July 2024, names information security as a generative AI risk, notes that these systems are open to attacks such as prompt injection and data poisoning, and includes suggested actions on incident response and disclosure, such as updating those processes as required and keeping a retention policy for test and evaluation records. The framework is voluntary. For this guide, Map covers the threat model of chapter 01, Measure covers chapter 08, and Manage covers this chapter.
  • ISO/IEC 42001:2023, published in December 2023, is a standard for an AI management system: policies, objectives and processes an organisation sets up and keeps improving. It is certifiable, which suits a firm that sells to enterprises who ask for it. ISO's own page could not be checked when this was written, so check the scope and the price on the ISO store before relying on this summary.
  • The UK took a principles route. The government's 2023 white paper asks existing regulators to apply five cross-sector principles, safety, security and robustness; transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress, and said it would not create an AI regulator or, at first, put the principles on a statutory footing. No UK AI statute turned up in the sources checked for this chapter, so UK duties for Harbourline come from data protection law and its sector regulators. Confirm the position with counsel, since policy there has been moving.

The choice is one of cost. NIST is free and fits a team that wants a shared vocabulary. ISO 42001 costs money and audit time and pays when customers demand a certificate. The EU Act is law, and the question is only which parts apply. Most teams begin with NIST's structure and add certification when a sale needs it.

Run the playbook as a drill once a year, using the claim PDF attack as the scenario. A drill finds the missing contact, the log field that was never captured and the owner who left. Skip the drill, and you will meet those on the real day. Teams with a single product and few customers can merge several artefacts into one document, but the fields must remain.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. A customer reports that a payout account changed after they uploaded a claim PDF. Your trace has the tool call and its arguments but not the source of each argument. What can you not do, and what should the log have held?

    Show answer

    You cannot tell an injection from a user mistake or a staff action, or find other affected sessions by signature. The log should record the provenance of every tool argument and a hash of each document read. See logging for forensics.

  2. Why does the playbook disable the write tool before it investigates, and what does that choice depend on in the design?

    Show answer

    Each hour of delay lets the same path run again, so containment comes first. It works only if the write sits behind a switch that can be flipped to propose-only without taking the whole agent down. See the playbook.

  3. The team confirms a data exposure on Friday at 17:00 but only begins assessing on Monday, since weekends are off. When did the 72-hour clock start, and what follows?

    Show answer

    It runs from awareness on Friday, so the deadline falls on Monday at 17:00 unless the breach is unlikely to risk people's rights. A late notice must state its reasons. Staff the on-call rota for weekends. See telling regulators.

  4. An engineer says Harbourline's chat is subject to the EU AI Act's high-risk rules because it is an insurer. What do you check before agreeing?

    Show answer

    Annex III point 5(c) covers risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance, so being an insurer is not enough. Check what each surface does, which lines Harbourline writes, and ask counsel. Chat still owes the Article 50 disclosure. See the regulation section.

  5. Your CISO wants 24-month retention of full traces to be safe. Argue the other side, and say what decides it.

    Show answer

    Full traces hold personal data and documents, so long retention enlarges any breach and strains data minimisation. Keep metadata and hashes longer than content. The deciding factors are how long frauds take to surface and what law requires for the record. See retention.

Takeaway

  • The log must let you link the upload, the text the model saw, the tool call, the source of each argument and the human decision, and it must be designed before launch.
  • Store hashes in the trace and content in a restricted store, so forensics does not turn the observability tool into a leak.
  • Contain by switching off the capability, assess by signature across all sessions, fix the class of failure and re-test before restoring.
  • Notification clocks, such as 72 hours under UK GDPR Article 33, start at awareness, so open the question in the first hour.
  • As of November 2026 the EU AI Act's Annex III obligations apply from 2 December 2027, and Annex III covers insurance only for life and health risk assessment and pricing, so applicability to Harbourline is a question for counsel.
  • NIST AI RMF gives a free structure, ISO/IEC 42001 gives a certificate, and the artefacts matter more than the framework name.
