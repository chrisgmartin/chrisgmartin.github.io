Section D · Defence and assurance

Red Teaming and Evaluation

Manual and automated red teaming, attack success rate as an eval metric, adversarial benchmarks for agents, continuous testing in CI, bug bounties, and the permit decision at Harbourline.

Overview

A defence you have not attacked is a hope. Chapter 07 stacked layers on Harbourline's claims-intake and customer-service agents, and each layer came with a claim about what it stops. This chapter turns those claims into measurements and then into a decision: may the agent hold a write tool, yes or no.

The work has four parts. People find the attacks no tool imagines. Tools replay thousands of known ones on every change. A metric with an interval tells you how much to believe the result. A rule written before the test turns the number into a decision. The numbers for Harbourline here are illustrative and labelled as such.

Manual red teaming

Start with people, because people are the only source of attacks nobody has written down. Microsoft's AI Red Team summarised its work on more than 100 generative AI products (Bullwinkel et al., 2025) and drew lessons that apply directly. Understand what the system can do and where it is deployed before choosing attacks. You do not need gradient computation to break a system. Red teaming differs from safety benchmarking. Automation widens coverage, but human judgement remains necessary. Securing AI is never finished.

For Harbourline, a useful session has a fixed shape. The team picks a goal that matches a real loss, such as "get the claims-intake agent to change the payout account on a claim the attacker does not own". The testers get the same access a hostile customer or a hostile document has, which is an upload form and a chat window, plus a second round with the architecture diagram, since an insider or a supplier knows the diagram. Every attempt is logged with the input, the full trace of tool calls and the outcome. A session of five people for two days is cheap against one payout fraud, and its main product is not the list of bypasses. It is the new test cases, one per successful attack, that go into the automated suite.

Include people who do not build the system. Authors test what they designed against, and fraud analysts bring the tricks that real fraudsters use. The exception is regulated or safety-critical content where you need a trained team and a legal basis for the test data. In that case use synthetic claims, never real customer records.

Automated tools

Three open-source tools cover most of the automated side, and they differ in what they assume about your system.

  • garak (NVIDIA) is a command-line scanner that probes a model for failures including prompt injection, data leakage, jailbreaks and toxicity, and marks each probe's attempts pass or fail with a failure rate. Its authors compare it to nmap for LLMs. It is a good first scan of a model or endpoint, and you choose probes with a flag such as --spec or run them all. It tests the model more than your application's logic.
  • PyRIT (Microsoft, MIT licence; the repository now lives at microsoft/PyRIT) is a toolkit for building red-team workflows against generative AI systems. It is a framework you program, which suits multi-turn attacks and custom targets such as Harbourline's chat API.
  • promptfoo generates adversarial inputs from plugins, runs them against the application, grades the responses and produces a report. Its plugins include authorisation tests such as broken object-level and function-level authorisation, which map onto "can one customer reach another's data through the agent". It documents CI integration.

Choose by where the risk sits. If you are comparing models, a garak scan is the quick answer. If your risk is the application, with its tools and tenants, you need tests that exercise the real tool path, and those you will mostly write yourself on top of PyRIT or promptfoo. Whatever the tool, the quality of the result depends on the attack set and the grader, and both can be wrong. A weak grader that checks only for a refusal phrase will report a bypass as a pass, so use a check the harness can verify: did the write tool run with an attacker-chosen value. That is a fact in the trace, not an opinion.

A scanner's clean run tests the scanner's list

Nasr et al. (2025) bypassed 12 published defences with attacks tuned to each, where the original papers reported near-zero rates against fixed attack sets. A tool that replays a fixed library gives you a floor on your exposure, not a ceiling. Keep a human adaptive round in the plan.

Attack success rate with an interval

The core metric is the attack success rate, the fraction of attack attempts that reach the attacker's goal. Define success mechanically before running anything. For the claim PDF it is "the payout tool was called with the attacker's account number". Count attempts and successes per attack class, not in one pool, since a pooled number hides the class that matters.

Report the interval with the rate. Suppose a baseline run on 200 cases of the claim-PDF injection suite succeeds 62 times. The rate is 31%, and the 95% Wilson interval is 25.0% to 37.7%. After the layers from chapter 07, suppose 3 of 200 succeed. The rate is 1.5%, and the interval is 0.5% to 4.3%. Both figures are illustrative. The second shows why the interval matters. The point estimate says 1.5%, but the data are also consistent with 4.3%, which is nearly three times larger. A zero count is no better: 0 of 200 gives an upper bound of 1.9%, and 0 of 1,000 gives 0.4%. The shortcut known as the rule of three says that with zero failures in n trials the 95% upper bound is near 3/n, which is 1.5% for 200 and close to the Wilson figure.

Two details stop the number lying. Models are stochastic, so run each attack several times, and treat the attack as the unit when you compute the interval, otherwise 20 repeats of 10 attacks pose as 200 independent trials. And separate fixed from adaptive attacks. Report the fixed-suite rate for regression tracking and the adaptive rate, from a human or an optimiser given your defences, as the number you believe. The first moves with every prompt tweak, and the second is the one an attacker will see.

The statistics are the same as in the evals guide, where the ship decision covers intervals on pass rates and sample size says how many cases you need. Attack success is an eval metric like any other, with one difference. The attacker chooses the cases, so the sample is never random.

Agent benchmarks

Public benchmarks give you a shared yardstick for agents. AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024) is an extensible environment for prompt injection against tool-using agents. Its first release has 97 realistic tasks, such as managing an email client, using an e-banking site or booking travel, and 629 security test cases in which data returned by a tool tries to hijack the agent. It measures utility and security together, which matters: the authors found that current models fail many tasks even with no attack, and that existing injection attacks break some security properties but not all. The CaMeL paper used it as its test bed, which is why the 77% and 84% figures in chapter 07 are comparable.

Use it in two ways. To compare defence designs before you build, run candidate patterns on AgentDojo's suites and read both columns, since a defence that cuts attacks by blocking tasks has traded one failure for another. To check your own build, treat it as a smoke test, because its tasks are not Harbourline's. A pass on an e-banking suite says little about whether your payout tool is safe. The answer to that is your own suite, built from your tools and your traces, in the style of the agent evals in evaluating agents.

Continuous testing

The attack surface moves each time someone edits a prompt, swaps a model, adds a tool or installs an MCP server. A red team run once before launch measures a system that no longer exists a month later. So wire the fixed suite into the pipeline the way the evals guide wires its regression set, as described in the CI section.

A workable split for Harbourline follows. On every pull request that touches a prompt, a tool schema or a model identifier, run a fast subset of about 200 injection cases against the staging agent, and fail the build if any case reaches a write tool, because that outcome should be impossible given the provenance rule. Nightly, run the full library, including garak or promptfoo probes, and track the attack success rate per class with its interval on a chart. Before each release, run the human adaptive round on anything that changed in architecture. Promptfoo's documentation describes teams making an evaluation mandatory for every prompt or workflow change, which is the same habit.

Two failure modes recur. Suites rot, so every production incident and every bounty report adds a case. And a CI gate that flakes gets disabled, so make the hard gate the deterministic one, "write tool called with untrusted-source value", and keep the statistical rates as a dashboard with an alert rather than a build breaker.

Bounties and disclosure

Outsiders will find things you did not. Give them a way to tell you, and decide in advance what you will do with what they say. Model providers run programmes of this kind. Anthropic's model safety bug bounty, as its help page describes it in November 2026, covers universal jailbreaks that defeat its classifiers on systems with ASL-3 protections, runs on HackerOne for accepted applicants, requires an NDA, and pays up to $35,000 for a novel universal jailbreak. It sends ordinary technical vulnerabilities such as injection flaws in web code to a separate responsible disclosure policy. The split is the useful lesson. Model behaviour and software defects need different intake, owners and timelines.

Harbourline is not a model provider, and its needs are smaller. Publish a security contact. Say in writing what testing is permitted and that good-faith reports will not be pursued. Name an owner who triages within a stated number of days. Route a report that shows a landed injection into the incident process in chapter 09, and turn it into a regression case. Whether to pay is a business choice. For a small attack surface a clear contact and a fast reply earn most of the goodwill, and money matters once strangers begin to find real flaws.

The permit decision

The question is whether the customer-service agent may hold the payment-method write tool. Decide the rule before the test, in a one-page note that the security owner and the product owner both sign, so the result cannot argue the threshold down afterwards. Here is a rule in that form.

  1. Structure first. The provenance rule and the human confirmation from chapter 07 are in place and tested. Without them the answer is no, whatever the rate.
  2. Hard gate. Zero of the fixed-suite cases may reach the write tool with an untrusted-source value.
  3. Rate bound. On the adaptive suite, the upper end of the 95% interval for the attack that reaches a human confirmation with a convincing false request stays under a threshold chosen from the attempt rate.

Choose the threshold from arithmetic. Say Harbourline sees an illustrative 100 hostile attempts a week and tolerates at most one landed attempt a quarter, which is 13 weeks and 1,300 attempts. The tolerable rate is 1 in 1,300, about 0.08%, and to show a bound that low you need roughly 3,900 independent trials with zero failures by the rule of three. That is a lot of cases. So most teams do two things. They accept a looser bound on the layer that merely raises cost, and they rely on the structural rule, which holds by construction, for the harm they cannot tolerate. The confirmation step then owns the residual risk, and its measurement is the human reviewer's catch rate on seeded false requests.

The decision flips when attempts change. If 1,000 hostile attempts a week appear, the same tolerance needs a ten times lower rate, and the answer may become "no write tool until the action selector pattern is in". The interview-prep guide has the high-risk framing in confident and wrong in high-risk work. Record the decision, the numbers and the date, because chapter 09 asks you to produce exactly that record.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. A red-team run of 200 injection cases against the new build shows 0 successes. A manager reads that as a 0% attack success rate. What is the most the data supports?

    Show answer

    A 95% upper bound of about 1.9% on this suite, close to the rule-of-three figure of 1.5%. Zero observed failures does not mean zero rate, and the bound shrinks only with more independent trials. See attack success rate.

  2. Why does the fixed-suite attack rate stay near 1% while a human attacker with a week still gets through?

    Show answer

    A fixed suite measures attacks written before the defence existed, and adaptive attackers tune against the specific defence. Published defences with near-zero reported rates were bypassed above 90% when attacked adaptively. Track both numbers. See tools and the metric.

  3. You run 20 variants of each of 10 base attacks and get 200 trials. Why is a plain interval on n = 200 too narrow?

    Show answer

    The variants of one attack are correlated, so there are nearer 10 independent attacks than 200. Compute the interval with the attack as the unit, or add more distinct attacks. See attack success rate.

  4. A CI job runs a nightly garak scan and fails the build on any failure rate above 2%. Within a month the team disables it. What went wrong, and what is the better gate?

    Show answer

    A stochastic rate near a threshold flips between runs, so the gate flakes and loses trust. Gate on the deterministic fact that no case reached a write tool with an untrusted value, and show statistical rates as a tracked alert. See continuous testing.

  5. Harbourline sees about 100 hostile attempts a week and can tolerate one landed attack a year. Is a red-team result of 1 success in 500 enough to permit a write tool with no other control?

    Show answer

    No. A year is 5,200 attempts, so the tolerable rate is about 0.02%, and 1 in 500 is 0.2% with an interval reaching higher. The write tool needs a structural control, since no affordable test shows a rate that low. See the permit decision.

Takeaway

  • Manual red teaming finds attacks that no library holds, and each success becomes a permanent regression case.
  • Tools such as garak, PyRIT and promptfoo replay known attacks at scale, and a clean run tests the tool's attack list, not your defence.
  • Define attack success mechanically from the trace, report it with an interval, and treat a zero count as an upper bound, not as zero.
  • Fixed-suite rates track regressions, and only adaptive attacks show what a determined attacker would get.
  • Gate CI on deterministic facts, such as no write call with an untrusted value, and keep statistical rates as dashboards.
  • Set the permit threshold from the attempt rate before testing, and rely on structural controls where no affordable test could prove a low enough rate.
