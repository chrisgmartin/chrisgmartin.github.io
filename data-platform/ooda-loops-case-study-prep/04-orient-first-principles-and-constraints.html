Orient: First Principles and Constraints

Where the thinking happens: constraints from physics and contracts, the one bottleneck, and hypotheses with the evidence that would kill them.

Where the thinking happens

Orientation is the stage between a ledger of facts and a target. It answers two questions: why is the current state what it is, and what is the best state that can be reached from here? Both answers come from constraints, and the method of this chapter is to list the constraints before you explain anything. People who skip the list explain the situation with whatever they saw last.

Boyd's name for the wrong way is analogy. "This is a vendor problem, we had one of those at my last job, here is what we did." The company whose principles this guide quotes puts it more bluntly on its hiring page: no analogy thinking. The right way is to take the situation apart into things that are true regardless of what happened elsewhere, and build the explanation up from them.

An inventory of constraints

Constraints in an operations problem come in four kinds, and it is worth writing a line for each kind even when a line says "none that matters".

KindExamples in a GPU fleetHow hard it is
PhysicalPower a hall can draw; cooling capacity; floor space; hours in a shift; time a truck takes from port to site; how many servers a person can rack and cable in a dayFixed on the timescale of the problem. You plan around these, not through them
ContractualThe promised date; penalty clauses; what the vendor committed to; carrier terms; what the colocation provider allows after hoursFixed unless renegotiated, and renegotiation is itself an action with a cost and a clock
OrganisationalWho can approve overtime; who owns the customer relationship; whether the site lead can call the broker directly; what has to go through procurementSofter than they look. Often the first thing worth changing
InformationalWhat is measured and how often; which systems disagree; what nobody knows until FridayThe constraint most often mistaken for one of the others. Buying information is usually the cheapest action available

The inventory is also where first principles get specific. "Unless it violates the laws of physics, nothing is impossible" is the hiring page's phrase, and it is a useful sorting rule: the physical row is what you cannot argue with, and everything below it is a decision someone made that could be made differently. An operator who can say which row a constraint is in has already done half the orientation.

Find the one that governs

Among the constraints, one usually decides the outcome. Goldratt's name for it, from manufacturing, is the bottleneck: the step whose capacity sets the throughput of the whole chain, so that improving anything else changes nothing until that step is improved. Finding it is a matter of drawing the chain and asking at each step how much can pass per unit time.

In the delivery case the chain is: vendor ships, carrier moves, customs clears, site receives, crew racks and cables, burn-in runs, hand-over. The upstream steps are events with uncertain dates. The racking step is a rate: about ten servers a week with the current crew. Forty servers are not yet racked: sixteen on the floor, sixteen at customs, eight not shipped. At ten a week, forty servers take four weeks. The date is 30 November. There are three weeks. Whatever happens at customs, the crew is the constraint, and the intended state has to be written around it.

This is the moment in a case where a candidate either sees the governing constraint or spends the remaining time on the loudest one. The customs hold is the loudest. The racking rate is the one that governs. Say so, with the arithmetic, and the rest of the conversation has a spine.

Explanations that can be killed

Why is the current state what it is? Write two or three explanations, and for each one write the observation that would show it false. An explanation without such an observation is an opinion, and an interviewer cannot do anything with it.

  • The customs hold is paperwork. Killed if the broker reports a physical inspection order. Checked by one phone call today.
  • The vendor's ship dates slip by a week on average. Killed if the last three purchase orders shipped on the promised day. Checked in the order history in an hour.
  • The crew's rate is limited by people, not by parts. Killed if there are servers on the floor waiting for rails or cables. Checked by asking the site lead what is sitting unracked and why.

Notice that each check is cheap and fast. That is not an accident. In orientation, the first actions worth taking are the ones that buy information, because they change the picture before anything expensive is committed. Chapter 05 calls these probes, and ranks them.

Taste is a constraint you bring

The round scores engineering taste: specific, opinionated views backed by real examples. In orientation, taste shows up as the constraints you add that the primer did not mention. An operator who has run a racking crew knows that cabling takes longer than bolting, that burn-in finds the bad units and that handing over before burn-in moves the failures to the customer's first week. An engineer who has built the systems knows that "racked" in NetBox means whatever the person updating it decided it means.

Bring those. State them as constraints, with the example that taught you. "At a previous site we handed over before burn-in once to make a date and spent the next week swapping units in front of the customer, so I treat burn-in as part of delivery, not after it." That is a view, it is specific, and it has evidence. It is also the kind of thing the five principles are listening for, which chapter 09 comes back to.

Analogy and experience are not the same

Analogy imports a conclusion: "last time it was the vendor". Experience imports a constraint: "cabling takes a day a rack". The first short-circuits orientation. The second feeds it. Boyd lists previous experience as an input for exactly this reason.

The intended state falls out

Once the constraints are listed and the governing one is named, the intended state almost writes itself, because the best reachable state is the one the governing constraint allows. Ten a week on one shift gives 30 more servers by the 30th, so 54 racked whatever arrives. A second shift doubles the rate to 20 a week and makes 64 reachable even if the customs units clear on the 13th and the last eight land on the 23rd. No second shift and the honest intended state is 54 by the 30th and 10 by 7 December, with the customer told early.

Write both versions down. Which one becomes the target depends on an organisational constraint (can a second shift be approved this week) and a contractual one (what a week's slip costs). Those are the two questions to ask next, and asking them in that order, after the arithmetic, is what a well-oriented candidate sounds like.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Name the four kinds of constraint and say which is most often mistaken for another.

    Show answer

    Physical, contractual, organisational, informational. The informational kind is mistaken for the others most often: "we cannot know until Friday" is treated as a fixed fact when buying the information today is usually the cheapest action available. See the inventory.

  2. In the delivery case, which constraint governs the date and what is the arithmetic?

    Show answer

    The racking rate. Forty servers still to rack at ten a week is four weeks; three weeks remain. Customs could clear tomorrow and the date would still be missed on one shift. See find the one that governs.

  3. What turns an explanation into something an interviewer can work with?

    Show answer

    The observation that would show it false, and how cheaply that observation can be made. "The hold is paperwork" becomes testable with one call to the broker. See explanations that can be killed.

  4. Distinguish analogy from experience in one sentence each.

    Show answer

    Analogy imports a conclusion from a similar-looking case. Experience imports a constraint you have measured, with the example that taught you. See taste is a constraint you bring.

  5. Why does this chapter say the intended state "falls out" of orientation?

    Show answer

    Because the best reachable state is whatever the governing constraint allows. Once the rate and the calendar are on the table, the two candidate targets and the two questions that choose between them are determined. See the intended state falls out.

Takeaway

  • List constraints before explaining anything: physical, contractual, organisational, informational. The physical row is the one you cannot argue with.
  • Draw the chain, put a capacity on each step, and name the step that sets the date. The loudest problem is rarely the governing one.
  • Each explanation comes with the cheap observation that would kill it. Taste is the constraints you add from experience, stated with the example that taught you.

With the governing constraint named, chapter 05 chooses what to do and how to know it worked.
