Observe Like an Operator

Reading a primer for facts, sources and ages; separating measurements from claims; the questions to ask in the first five minutes.

The page is a source, not the truth

The primer is one page, written by someone on the team, some time before the interview. That makes it a source like any other: it has an author, a date and a point of view. It will contain measurements ("40 servers received"), reports ("the vendor says Friday"), and judgements ("the customs delay is the main risk"). Your first job is to tell them apart, because they deserve different levels of trust and different follow-up questions.

A measurement is a number read from a system or a sensor, with a time attached. A report is a person's statement about the world, including a vendor's email and the primer author's own summary. A judgement is an interpretation: "the main risk", "unlikely", "on track". The primer will mix all three in the same paragraph. Operators separate them by habit, and the habit shows.

From prose to a ledger

Read the page twice. The first time, do not take notes; get the shape of the situation and resist forming a theory. The second time, pull every factual claim out onto its own line and fill in four columns: the fact, where it came from, how old it is, and how far you trust it. Two or three minutes of silence doing this is fine. Say what you are doing ("I am going to pull the facts out before I say anything") and the interviewer will wait.

The trust column has three values and they are enough.

  • Measured. A system or sensor produced it and the page says when. NetBox counts, telemetry, carrier scans, timestamps.
  • Reported. A person said it. Vendor emails, Slack messages, the site lead's estimate, the primer's own narrative. True until checked, but check before building on it.
  • Inferred. Someone's conclusion, including the author's. "The main risk is customs." Useful as a hypothesis. Never a fact.

Age matters as much as trust. A carrier status four days old at a customs hold is a different fact from one scanned this morning. A racking rate derived from six weeks of timestamps is sturdier than one from the last two. If the page does not give an age, write "unknown" and ask. The ontology guide calls this the fact's envelope; the habit is the same whether the fact lives in a system or on a sheet of paper.

What the page does not say

The second list is shorter and matters more: what you would need to know to act, that the page does not tell you. It comes from asking, for each line of the ledger, "what would change my plan if this were different?" The customs hold: is it paperwork, which clears in a day with the right form, or inspection, which can take two weeks? The vendor's Friday: has this vendor's Friday been Friday before? The racking crew: how many people, what shifts, is a second shift possible, and who pays for it? The customer: what happens on their side if the servers are a week late?

Not every unknown is worth a question. Rank them by how much the answer would move the plan. In the delivery case the customs reason and the crew's capacity each change the intended state; the vendor's history changes a contingency. Ask the first two, note the third.

The question that gives the game away

"What tools do you use for tracking?" is a question about solutions wearing the clothes of a question about the problem. Interviewers hear it as the candidate reaching for the familiar. Ask about the situation. The tooling conversation comes later, if at all.

The first five minutes

Problem-first thinking is one of the five things the round scores, and it is scored almost entirely in these minutes. The sequence that works:

  1. Read, then say the situation back in two sentences. "A customer was promised 64 servers at Reno by the end of the month. Forty are on site and twenty-four of those are racked, sixteen are stuck at customs, eight have not shipped, and the crew racks about ten a week." If the interviewer corrects you, good: that is a fact you had wrong, found early.
  2. Name what you trust least. "The vendor's Friday is an email, and the customs status is four days old. I would want both refreshed before I plan around them."
  3. Ask the two questions that would most change the plan. Not five. Two, chosen because their answers move the intended state.
  4. Say what you are not yet asking. "I have more questions about the crew, but I think the shape is clear enough to say what the target should be." That shows you have a list and are managing the time.

What this is not: a monologue of clarifying questions that runs until the interviewer stops it. Two or three questions, answered, is enough information to write an intended state. The rest arrive as the plan develops, and asking them then, when they bite, shows better judgement than asking them all up front.

Go and see

Toyota's production system has a phrase for the observation habit, genchi genbutsu, usually translated as "go and see for yourself". A report about a line is not the line. In an interview you cannot go to Reno, but you can say what you would look at if you could, and that answer is scored too. "Before I believed the 24 racked, I would want to know whether NetBox is updated when a server is bolted in or when it passes burn-in, because those are days apart at ten a week." That sentence shows you know where counts come from.

The same habit protects you from the primer's framing. If the page says the customs hold is the main risk, the operator's response is not to accept or reject it but to ask what evidence it rests on. Often it is the most recent thing that went wrong rather than the thing that governs the outcome. The racking rate in the delivery case is the quieter fact, and it is the one that decides the date.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. The primer says "the vendor confirms the remaining units ship Friday". Which trust level is that, and what is the follow-up?

    Show answer

    Reported. A person said it, in an email. The follow-up is whether this vendor's ship dates have held before, and what the contingency is if Friday becomes the Friday after. See from prose to a ledger.

  2. How do you choose which unknowns to ask about in the first minutes?

    Show answer

    Rank them by how much the answer would move the plan. Ask the two that change the intended state; note the ones that only change a contingency and raise them when they bite. See what the page does not say.

  3. Why is "what tools do you use for tracking?" a weak opening question?

    Show answer

    It is a question about solutions dressed as a question about the problem. It tells the interviewer you are reaching for the familiar before you understand the situation. See what the page does not say.

  4. You cannot visit the site. How do you still show the go-and-see habit?

    Show answer

    Say what you would look at and why: for instance, whether the "racked" count is set at bolt-in or after burn-in, because the two differ by days at ten a week. Knowing where a number comes from is the habit. See go and see.

Takeaway

  • The primer is a source with an author and a date. Separate measurements, reports and judgements, and mark each fact's age.
  • Build the ledger before you speak: fact, source, age, trust. Then list what the page does not say, ranked by how much each unknown would move the plan.
  • First five minutes: say the situation back, name what you trust least, ask two questions, say what you are deferring.
