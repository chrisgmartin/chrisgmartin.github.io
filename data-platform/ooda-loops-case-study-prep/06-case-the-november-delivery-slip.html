Section C · Three cases

Case: The November Delivery Slip

Sixty-four servers promised, forty on site, three weeks left. The loop run end to end on a supply problem.

The primer

Read this as you would in the room: twice, the second time pulling facts onto a ledger. Then write your own current state, intended state and gap before reading the worked answer. The fixture is GridDP's Reno site; every number is made up for teaching.

Primer: Reno, Monday 9 November

A reserved-capacity customer has a signed order for 64 servers (512 GPUs) at Reno, hand-over by 30 November. Forty servers have been received at the site; 24 are racked and burned in, 16 are on the floor. Sixteen more are on two pallets that the carrier has shown as "held at customs" since Thursday. The vendor emailed on Friday to say the final eight ship this Friday, 13 November. The Reno crew is four people on one shift and has racked and burned in about ten servers a week over the last six weeks. The customer's programme manager has asked twice for a status update and has had none since the order was signed. The account owner believes the customs delay is the main risk. What is the current state, what is the intended state, and how do we close the gap?

Current state

The ledger, as chapter 03 would build it. Trust is marked where it is not a measurement.

  • Commitment: 64 servers at Reno by 30 November. Signed order. Fixed.
  • Racked and burned in: 24. NetBox, current. Measured.
  • On the floor: 16. NetBox receiving records. Measured.
  • At customs: 16, held since Thursday. Carrier portal, four days old. Measured but stale.
  • Not shipped: 8, vendor says Friday. Email. Reported.
  • Crew rate: about 10 a week, four people, one shift. Six weeks of NetBox timestamps. Measured.
  • Customer: two unanswered requests. Reported.
  • "Customs is the main risk." Account owner's view. Inferred.

What the page does not say, ranked by how much the answer moves the plan: why customs is holding the pallets; whether a second shift is possible and who approves it; what a week's slip costs the customer; whether this vendor's Friday has held before; whether "burned in" in NetBox is set by the crew or by the telemetry system.

Said back in two sentences: "Sixty-four promised in three weeks; twenty-four done, sixteen waiting on the floor, sixteen at customs, eight not shipped. The crew does ten a week." Then the two questions: what is the customs hold, and can we run a second shift.

Orientation

Constraints, by kind. Physical: ten servers a week per shift, and burn-in takes about two days per batch regardless of crew size. Contractual: 30 November, and a penalty clause nobody has mentioned, which is worth asking about. Organisational: a second shift needs approval and either overtime or a contractor crew; the account owner, not the site lead, talks to the customer. Informational: the customs reason and the vendor's reliability are both knowable today and nobody has asked.

The governing constraint is the crew. Forty servers remain to be racked. At ten a week that is four weeks; three remain. If every pallet were on the floor tomorrow the one-shift date would be 7 December. The customs hold matters, but it is not what decides the date, and saying that out loud reorganises the conversation. The account owner's "main risk" is the loudest problem, not the governing one.

Two explanations to test. The hold is paperwork, killed if the broker reports an inspection order; one call. The vendor's Friday is real, killed if the last three orders slipped; one hour in the order history. Both probes are cheap and both change the plan, so both run today.

Intended state

Two versions, because the organisational constraint has not been resolved yet.

  1. With a second shift from Thursday 12 November: 64 servers racked, burned in and handed over at Reno by Monday 30 November. Requires customs to clear by Friday the 13th and the last eight to land by the 23rd. Twenty a week from the 12th covers it with about four days of slack.
  2. Without a second shift: 54 by 30 November and the remaining 10 by 7 December, with the customer told by Wednesday 11 November which version they are getting.

Both are measurable on the dates named. Both are reachable. Choosing between them is the approval question, which is asked today and answered by Wednesday. Promising the customer either date before Wednesday is the one-way door, so the call waits for the broker and for the approval.

On Thursday, with the broker's answer and the shift decision in hand, the two versions collapse to one and the customer has been told which.

What the interviewer will push on

  • "The broker says inspection, two weeks." New information into orientation. Sixteen servers now land around the 23rd. With two shifts the 30th still holds, with two days of slack instead of four; without, the intended state is version two and the customer call says so. Nothing else in the plan changes.
  • "Overtime is refused." Contractor crew, which is the fallback already in the table. If that is also refused, version two becomes the target and the customer call happens Wednesday as planned. The organisational constraint has become fixed; the plan absorbs it.
  • "Why not hand over the 24 now and the rest as they come?" A reasonable option that was missing from the list. Ask the customer whether partial hand-over is useful to them; if it is, it reduces their exposure and is reversible. Add it. Credit the interviewer for the idea.
  • "Why not skip burn-in to make the date?" The one-way door. Burn-in is where infant failures are found; skipping it moves them to the customer's first week. Say no, with the reason, and offer the partial hand-over instead.

How this case goes wrong

Candidates lose this case in three ways. They spend fifteen minutes on customs because the primer said it was the main risk, and never do the arithmetic that shows the crew governs. They write "deliver on time" as the intended state and have nothing to measure. Or they produce a list of tasks without owners, dates or observations, so the interviewer cannot tell how they would know on Thursday whether the plan is working. Each failure is a stage of the loop skipped: orientation, orientation again, and the return to observation.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Which fact in the primer is a judgement, and how should it be handled?

    Show answer

    "The customs delay is the main risk" is the account owner's inference. Treat it as a hypothesis and test it against the arithmetic, which shows the crew rate governs the date. See current state.

  2. Why are two intended states written rather than one?

    Show answer

    Because an organisational constraint, the second-shift approval, is unresolved and decides which target is reachable. Both are measurable; the approval question chooses between them by Wednesday. See intended state.

  3. The interviewer says the vendor will not split the last eight. What changes?

    Show answer

    Little, if Friday holds: the eight land by the 23rd and two shifts absorb them. If Friday slips a week they land on the 30th and cannot be racked in time, so the intended state becomes 56 by the 30th and the customer call includes that branch. See what the interviewer will push on.

  4. What is the one-way door in this case, and what is the reversible alternative?

    Show answer

    Skipping burn-in to hit the date, which moves infant failures to the customer. The reversible alternative is partial hand-over of the burned-in units, if the customer finds it useful. See what the interviewer will push on.

Takeaway

  • Forty to rack at ten a week is four weeks against three. The crew governs; customs is loud.
  • Two intended states until the shift approval lands; the customer call waits for Wednesday because a promised date is a one-way door.
  • Six-row closing plan, each with an owner, a date and an observation, and a last row that rewrites the table every morning.
