Section A · The loop

Current State, Intended State, Gap

The interview's version of the loop: say what is, say what should be and by when, and name each difference with a cause.

The three moves the primer asks for

The primer will not say "observe" or "orient". It will give you a page about a situation and ask for three things: the current state, the intended state, and how to close the gap between them. That is a practical restatement of the loop for people who have to run things, and it is a good one, because it forces two of the loop's stages into writing.

The mapping onto Boyd's stages is close but not exact, and the inexact part is instructive.

The primer asks forBoyd's stageWhat you produce
Current stateObserve, with orientation checking the observationSix to ten lines of fact, each with its source and its age, plus a list of what you do not know
Intended stateOrientOne or two sentences with a number and a date, and the constraints that make them the right ones
Close the gapDecide, then act, then observe againNamed differences, each with a cause, an action, an owner and the measurement that shows it closed

The intended state sits in orientation, not decision. Choosing what "should be" is a judgement about purpose and constraints: what the customer was promised, what the building can take, what the money allows. People who treat it as given ("the target is obviously on-time delivery") skip the stage the loop is named for.

Writing a current state

A current state is a list of facts, not a story. Each line says one thing that is true now, where that knowledge came from, and how old it is. The discipline of writing the source and the age next to every fact does two things: it separates what was measured from what someone said, and it shows which facts are stale enough to re-check before acting on them.

Take the first case in this guide. A current state for it reads like this.

  • Commitment: 64 servers to the customer at Reno by 30 November. Source: the signed order. Age: fixed.
  • On site: 40 servers received at Reno, 24 racked, 16 on the floor. Source: NetBox, updated by the site lead this morning.
  • In transit: 16 servers on two pallets, carrier shows "held at customs" since Thursday. Source: carrier portal, four days old.
  • Not shipped: 8 servers. Vendor email says they ship Friday. Source: an email, not a system.
  • Racking rate: the Reno crew racks and burns in about 10 servers a week. Source: last six weeks of NetBox timestamps.
  • Today: Monday 9 November. Three weeks remain.

Then a shorter list: what the page does not say. Whether the customs hold is paperwork or inspection. Whether the vendor's "Friday" has been true before. Whether the crew can work weekends. Those gaps are your first questions, and asking them is itself part of the current state. Chapter 03 is the method for getting from a page of prose to a list like this.

Writing an intended state

The intended state is where most candidates are vague and where the interviewers are listening hardest. "Get the servers delivered" is a wish. An intended state has three parts.

  1. A measurable condition. "64 servers racked, burned in and handed over at Reno."
  2. A date. "By 30 November." Or, if that is not reachable, the honest alternative: "54 by 30 November and the remaining 10 by 7 December, with the customer told by 12 November."
  3. The constraints that make it the right target. The contract says 30 November. The crew's rate says 64 is possible only if everything on the page goes right. The customer's own plan says what a week's slip costs them, which you do not know yet and should ask.

The word is intended, not ideal. The ideal state has no late shipments. The intended state is the best reachable one given what is true today, and saying so out loud is a mark of someone who has run deliveries. An interviewer who hears "the intended state is on-time delivery" learns nothing. One who hears "the intended state is 64 by the 30th, which needs the customs hold cleared by Wednesday and a second shift, and if either fails the intended state becomes 54 plus a conversation on the 12th" learns that you think in constraints.

A test for the sentence

Could two people look at the same system on 1 December and agree whether the intended state was reached? If the answer needs a discussion, the sentence is not finished.

Naming the gap

The gap is not one thing. It is a list of differences between the two states, and each difference has its own cause and its own fix. In the delivery case the gap has at least four parts: 16 servers stuck at customs, 8 not yet shipped, a racking rate that cannot get through 40 servers in three weeks, and a customer who does not yet know any of this. Those four have four different causes, four different owners and four different clocks.

Writing the gap as a list does two things in the interview. It stops the conversation collapsing into a single solution ("call the vendor") that addresses one part. And it lets you say which part governs the outcome. Here it is the racking rate: even if every server arrives tomorrow, 10 a week means the last ones are racked on 7 December. That is the bottleneck, and chapter 04 is about finding it before you plan anything else.

Each line of the gap then gets an action, a person, a date and the observation that will show it closed. That is the closing plan, and it is the subject of chapter 05. The thing to notice now is that the loop is visible in the shape: the last column of the plan ("how we will know") is the first column of the next current state.

Why the interview uses this shape

A team that builds data centres on a six-month schedule lives in gaps. The plan says a hall energises on a date; the site says otherwise; someone has to say by how much and what to do. The current-state, intended-state, gap framing is how such a team talks to itself, and the case is a sample of that conversation. It also happens to be the shape of a good incident review, a good quarterly plan and a good status update, which is why it is worth practising even if you never hear the word OODA again.

It is collaborative because that is how it is used. The interviewer will add facts, remove assumptions and push on your intended state. Treat each push as new information entering the loop, say what it changes, and carry on. A candidate who defends the first plan against new facts has shown exactly what the loop is meant to prevent.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Which of the three moves belongs to orientation, and why is it not a decision?

    Show answer

    The intended state. Choosing what should be true, and by when, is a judgement about purpose and constraints, not a choice among actions. Treating the target as given skips orientation. See the three moves.

  2. Every line of a current state carries two things besides the fact. What are they, and what does each protect you from?

    Show answer

    Its source and its age. The source separates measurement from claim (NetBox versus an email). The age shows which facts to re-check before acting. See writing a current state.

  3. Rewrite "deliveries back on track" as an intended state.

    Show answer

    Something like: 64 servers racked, burned in and handed over at Reno by 30 November; failing that, 54 by the 30th and 10 by 7 December with the customer told by 12 November. A condition, a date, and the constraints that make it the best reachable target. See writing an intended state.

  4. Why write the gap as a list rather than a sentence?

    Show answer

    Because its parts have different causes, owners and clocks, and a single solution usually addresses one of them. The list also lets you name which part governs the outcome. See naming the gap.

  5. The interviewer adds a fact that breaks your plan. What does the loop say to do?

    Show answer

    Treat it as new information entering orientation: say what it changes in the current state, the intended state or the gap, and revise. Defending the first plan is the failure the loop exists to prevent. See why the interview uses this shape.

Takeaway

  • Current state is observation written down: facts with sources and ages, plus what you do not know.
  • Intended state is orientation written down: a measurable condition, a date, and the constraints that make it the right target. Intended, not ideal.
  • The gap is a list. Each line has a cause, an action, an owner, a date and the observation that shows it closed. That last column is the next loop's first.
