Decide and Act

Options including doing nothing, one-way doors, the cheapest probe that changes your mind, and closing the loop with a measurement.

Options, including doing nothing

Deciding starts with a list, and the list has a rule: doing nothing is always on it. Not because it is often right, but because every other option is measured against it. "Wait for the vendor's Friday" is a decision with a cost (a week of the crew's capacity unused if the floor runs empty) and a benefit (no money spent on expediting). Writing it down makes the cost visible. Leaving it off makes it the thing that happens by default while everyone argues about the alternatives.

For the delivery case the list is short: add a second shift this week; expedite the customs clearance through the broker; ask the vendor to split the last eight and air-freight four; tell the customer now and renegotiate the date; do nothing and see where Friday lands. Five options. Each has a cost, a clock and a chance of working, and most of them are not exclusive.

One-way doors

Sort the options by how hard they are to reverse. Bezos's 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders calls the two kinds Type 1 and Type 2 decisions: one-way doors that deserve deliberation, and two-way doors that should be made quickly by small groups and reversed if wrong. Most operational decisions are two-way doors wearing one-way costumes. Adding a shift can be cancelled. Expediting a shipment costs money once. Telling the customer a date you then miss is the one-way door in the list, and so is handing over servers that have not finished burn-in.

The sorting does two things in the room. It lets you act on the reversible options without ceremony, which shows urgency. And it shows where you slow down, which shows judgement. "I would add the shift today and call the broker today, those are reversible. I would not call the customer with a date until Wednesday, when I know what customs is." That sentence is a plan.

The cheapest probe that changes your mind

Among the options, some exist to buy information rather than to close the gap. Chapter 04 called them probes: the broker call that reveals whether the hold is paperwork or inspection, the order history that shows whether the vendor's Friday is real, the question to the site lead about what is sitting unracked. A probe is worth running when its result would change which option you choose. If you would add the shift regardless of what the broker says, the broker call is not a probe; it is a task.

Rank probes by cost against how much they would move the decision. The broker call is a few minutes and decides whether sixteen servers arrive this week or in three. It goes first. The vendor history is an hour and only changes a contingency. It goes second. A full audit of NetBox's racked timestamps would be a day and would not change this week's plan at all. It goes on the list for later. The order matters more than the completeness.

Say the probe's two outcomes

"If the broker says paperwork, the sixteen arrive by Friday and the second shift gets us to 64. If inspection, they arrive in two weeks, and the intended state is 54 plus a conversation with the customer on Wednesday." Naming both branches before the call is what makes it a decision rather than a hope.

The closing plan

A closing plan is the gap list from chapter 02 with four columns added: the action, who owns it, when it is done, and what observation will show it worked. The last column is the one candidates leave off and the one that turns a list of tasks into a loop.

Read the last column again. Each entry is a fact that will appear in a system by a date. That is what makes it checkable on Thursday without a meeting. It is also what the next loop observes: on Thursday the current state is rewritten from those four facts, the intended state is confirmed or revised, and the gap list shrinks or changes. The plan has a built-in return to observation.

Loop time and nesting

How often should the loop run? As often as a measurement can change the plan. For the delivery case that is daily: every carrier scan and every racked count can move the date. A weekly review would be three loops too few. For the quarterly question (how many servers to order for the next customer), daily would be noise; monthly fits. The loop's period follows the rate at which the facts change, and saying so is a sign you have run one.

The loops nest. The quarterly loop's intended state (600 servers deployed by year end) sets the weekly loop's target (this customer's 64 by the 30th), which sets the daily loop's (20 racked this week). When a daily decision would break the weekly target, that is the signal to escalate, and the escalation is itself an observation entering the larger loop. In an interview, saying which loop you are in, and which one above it sets your target, stops a planning conversation from drifting between timescales.

When to stop

A loop closes when the intended state is observed, not when the actions are done. The crew can finish racking and the servers can still fail burn-in; the plan is not complete until the hand-over is recorded. The other reason to stop is that the intended state has changed: the customer agreed to the 7th, so the second shift is cancelled and the gap list is rewritten. Both are legitimate. What is not legitimate is stopping because the actions were taken and nobody looked.

This is also where ownership shows. The team whose principles are quoted in chapter 09 calls it being a barrel: owning the thing end to end, before anyone asks. In loop terms, the owner is whoever reads the last column on Thursday and rewrites the current state. If no one is named, no one does, and the loop stays open while everyone believes it is closed.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Why is "do nothing" always on the options list?

    Show answer

    Because every other option is measured against it, and because leaving it off makes it the default that happens while the alternatives are debated. Writing it down makes its cost visible. See options.

  2. Which option in the delivery case is the one-way door, and what follows from that?

    Show answer

    Telling the customer a date (and handing over before burn-in). It gets the deliberation and waits for the broker's answer. The reversible options, the shift and the broker call, are taken today without ceremony. See one-way doors.

  3. What makes an action a probe rather than a task?

    Show answer

    Its result would change which option you choose. If you would do the same thing either way, it is a task. Rank probes by cost against how much they move the decision, and say both outcomes before running one. See probes.

  4. What is in the last column of a closing plan, and why does it matter?

    Show answer

    The observation that will show the action worked: a fact that appears in a system by a date. It makes the plan checkable without a meeting and is the first column of the next current state. See the closing plan.

  5. How do you choose a loop's period?

    Show answer

    From how fast the facts that move the plan change. Daily for a delivery with carrier scans; monthly for ordering decisions. Loops nest, and the larger one's intended state sets the smaller one's target. See loop time and nesting.

Takeaway

  • List options with doing nothing among them. Sort by reversibility; take the two-way doors today and deliberate on the one-way door.
  • Run the probe whose result would most change the choice, cheapest first, and say both branches before you run it.
  • The closing plan has an owner, a date and a "we will know because" for every line. A loop closes when the intended state is observed, not when the tasks are done.
