The Forty-Five Minutes

A minute plan for the conversation: problem first, think aloud, apply the loop to your own work, ask back, and close.

The minute plan

Forty-five minutes, three parts, and the interviewers control the clock. You control how much of each part is spent on the things they score. The plan below is for the typical split; if the behavioural part runs long, the case shrinks from the pushback end, not from the first five minutes.

Thinking aloud without narrating

The case is collaborative, so the interviewers need to hear your orientation, not just your conclusions. The trick is to say what you are doing and what you are concluding, and skip what is in between.

  • Say what you are doing when you go quiet. "I am pulling the facts onto a list before I say anything." Thirty seconds of silence with a stated purpose reads as method. Thirty seconds without one reads as being stuck.
  • Say the conclusion with its reason, once. "The crew governs, because forty at ten a week is four weeks and we have three." Not the three sentences of working that got you there.
  • Say what a new fact changes. "Inspection, not paperwork. That moves the sixteen to the 23rd; with two shifts the 30th still holds, with two days of slack." Then stop and let them respond.
  • Say what you are not doing. "I am going to leave the vendor's history for now; it changes a contingency, not the target." A stated omission is a decision. An unstated one is a gap.

The test the round calls "crisp communication" is mostly this: direct sentences with the reason attached, and a stop at the end of each one. Interviewers who have to interrupt to get a word in are not collaborating; they are waiting.

Fast pickup: your own work as a loop

One of the five things scored is whether you can apply the framework to your own work with little nudging. The nudge will be something like "have you faced something like this?" or "how would you have handled this at your last company?" Have the answer ready before the room: one past project, retold in the case's vocabulary. Current state in three facts, intended state with a date, the governing constraint, the closing plan, the observation that closed it.

Pick the project by fit to the primer's shape if you can. A supply problem wants a story about a dependency that was late. A data problem wants a story about systems that disagreed. A reliability problem wants a story about a number that moved. If none fits, any loop you ran honestly is better than a forced match.

The retelling should take ninety seconds and should end with what you would do differently, because that is a loop observing itself. "We fixed the count. We did not add the weekly check, and the duplicates came back in the spring. The next time I built the check first."

Questions to ask back

The last five minutes are scored for curiosity and for whether you did the reading. A question that could be asked of any company fails both tests. The public record on this one is unusually specific, and this site's profile has the dates. Questions that work:

  • The public filings put the first large hall deliveries in late 2026 and early 2027. Which site is furthest ahead of its schedule right now, and which is furthest behind, and how does the team know?
  • When two systems disagree about a machine or a hall, who decides today, and how long does it take?
  • What is the loop time between a measurement on site and a changed plan at headquarters? What is the slowest part of it?
  • The primer I just worked: what did the team actually do, and what did they learn afterwards that they would do differently?
  • Of the five principles, which one does the team find hardest to live up to in practice, and what does that look like?

The fourth question is the best one on the list, because it turns the case into a conversation between peers and because the answer tells you whether the team closes its own loops. Ask it before you run out of time.

One question to avoid

Anything whose answer is on the website. The interview FAQ answers process, offices and hybrid questions; the jobs page lists the teams. Asking them spends curiosity points on things you could have read.

Honesty under pushback

The round scores "direct, concise, and honest". The honesty test arrives as pushback: a fact that breaks your plan, or a question about your story's numbers. The right response to the first is to say what changes and change it. The right response to the second is the true answer, including "I am not sure of that number; it was in that range". Candidates who defend a plan against a new fact, or round a number up under questioning, fail a test they did not know they were taking.

There is a second honesty test in the other direction. If the interviewers' primer has a mistake in it, say so, politely and with the arithmetic. If the team's own answer to the case, when they tell you, has a hole, name it as a question. "Did the weekly check catch anything?" The principles say the best idea wins and there are no egos. Behave as though that is true of the room, and see whether it is. That observation is yours to take away.

The day before

  1. Read the profile, chapters 03, 06 and 10, and the company's interview FAQ. Write three facts you could be asked to defend.
  2. Skim Situational Awareness if you have not read it. Write one sentence on its claim and one on its weakest step.
  3. Tell your six to eight stories aloud, timed, in loop form. Cut any that run past two and a half minutes.
  4. Work one of the three cases in this guide cold, aloud, with a timer set to fifteen minutes. Chapter 07 if you have not done it yet.
  5. Write the five questions you will ask back on a card. You will not need the card; writing it is the point.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. If the behavioural part runs long, what part of the case gives way, and what does not?

    Show answer

    The pushback and closing-plan end gives way. The first five minutes, the ledger and the two questions, do not, because problem-first thinking is scored there and cannot be recovered later. See the minute plan.

  2. Give the four things to say aloud and the one thing not to.

    Show answer

    Say what you are doing when quiet, the conclusion with its reason once, what a new fact changes, and what you are deferring. Do not narrate the working between fact and conclusion. See thinking aloud.

  3. How should the retelling of your own project end, and why?

    Show answer

    With what you would do differently. It shows the loop observing itself, and it is the honest ending; a project with nothing to change was not observed afterwards. See fast pickup.

  4. Which question to ask back does this chapter rank first, and what does its answer tell you?

    Show answer

    "What did the team actually do with this case, and what did they learn afterwards?" It turns the case into a peer conversation and tells you whether the team closes its own loops. See questions to ask back.

Takeaway

  • Protect the first five minutes of the case and the questions at the end; let the pushback shrink if time is short.
  • Say what you are doing, the conclusion with its reason, what a new fact changes and what you are deferring. Stop after each.
  • Have one past project ready in the case's vocabulary, ending with what you would change. Ask what the team did with the case and what they learned.
