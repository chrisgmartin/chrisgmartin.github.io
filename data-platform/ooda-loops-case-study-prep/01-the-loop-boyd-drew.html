Section A · The loop

The Loop Boyd Drew

Observe, orient, decide, act: the real sketch, why orientation is the big box, and what tempo means for an operator.

Four words everyone knows

Observe, orient, decide, act. Most people who use the phrase picture four boxes in a ring and read it as "look, think, choose, do, repeat". That picture is useful for about a minute. It says a plan should come from looking rather than from habit, and that you should look again after acting. Then it stops helping, because it makes the four stages look the same size, the same difficulty and the same order every time.

John Boyd was a US Air Force colonel who spent the 1970s and 1980s working out why some fighter pilots, and some armies, kept beating opponents with better equipment. The loop is his answer, and he drew it once, in a 1996 briefing called "The Essence of Winning and Losing". That drawing is the one to know.

The sketch Boyd drew

In Boyd's drawing the four stages are not equal. Orientation is a large box in the middle with five things feeding it: cultural traditions, genetic heritage, previous experience, new information, and a process he labels "analysis and synthesis". Observation feeds it from the left. Decision and action are smaller boxes to the right. Then there are the arrows most slide versions leave out.

Three features of the sketch change how you use it.

  1. Orientation shapes observation. An arrow runs from orientation back to observation. What you already believe decides what you notice. An operator who believes deliveries slip at customs looks at customs dates and misses the racking crew. This is why the primer must be read before you have a theory, and why chapter 03 spends its time on reading.
  2. Implicit guidance and control. Two dashed arrows run from orientation straight to observation and to action, bypassing decision. Boyd's point: someone with a good enough picture of the situation does not deliberate on every move. They act, and the act is right because the orientation was. In an interview this is the difference between a candidate who lists options for everything and one who says "that part is obvious, we do X, the real question is Y". Both are fine. Knowing which parts deserve a decision is the skill.
  3. Feedback from everything. Decisions and actions both feed back into observation, and the whole thing sits inside what Boyd labels "unfolding interaction with environment". Acting changes the world you then observe. A plan that does not say what to look at after acting has left the loop open.

Why orientation is the big box

Boyd's earlier essay, "Destruction and Creation" (1976), is the argument behind the big box. He says a mind works with concepts, each a pattern built from past observations. New observations that do not fit the pattern create mismatch. You can ignore the mismatch, which is what analogy does: "this is like the last time, so do what we did then". Or you can take the old pattern apart into its parts (destruction) and build a new one that fits the evidence (creation). He calls the second move the only way to keep up with a changing situation.

That is the whole of what people now call reasoning from first principles. Break the situation into the facts and constraints you can verify. Build the explanation up from those. Do not import an explanation from a similar-looking case. In an operations setting the first principles are usually boring and physical: how many hours a crew has, how long a shipment takes, how much power a hall can draw, what a contract says happens on the thirtieth. Chapter 04 is a method for listing them.

The five inputs to orientation are also a checklist for bias. Previous experience and cultural tradition are the two that tell you what "normally" happens. New information is the primer. Analysis and synthesis is the work of reconciling them. When a candidate says "in my experience this is usually the vendor", they have named which input they are leaning on. Good interviewers hear it.

What tempo means for an operator

Boyd's loop is often summarised as "be faster than the other side". In air combat that meant completing your loop inside the opponent's, so your actions arrived before their picture had caught up, and their orientation fell further behind with each turn. Operations have no opponent, but the idea survives in two forms.

First, the time between a measurement and the corrected action is the real speed of an organisation. If a slipped shipment is known on Tuesday and nobody re-plans the racking crew until the Friday stand-up, the loop time is three days, whatever the dashboards refresh at. Shortening the loop usually means moving the observation closer to the person who can act, not making anyone work faster.

Second, speed is not the same as skipping stages. The company whose hiring pages this guide quotes lists "insane urgency" as a principle, and in the same breath "reason from first principles". Those are not in tension in Boyd's loop. Implicit guidance is how a well-oriented actor is fast: they have already done the thinking, so the obvious moves cost nothing, and the saved time goes to the moves that are not obvious. Urgency without orientation is just activity. The interviewers have seen plenty of it.

Three ways the loop gets misread

  • As a checklist. "First we observe, then we orient…" The stages overlap and run at different speeds. You observe while acting; you orient while observing. In the interview, do not announce stages. Do the work and let the interviewer see the shape.
  • As a reason to act before thinking. "Just ship and iterate" borrows the loop's name for a method that spends nothing on orientation. Iteration is cheap when actions are reversible and observation is quick. Racking two hundred servers in the wrong hall is neither.
  • As one loop. A delivery programme has a quarterly loop (how many servers do we order), a weekly loop (which site gets this week's shipment), and an hourly loop (which rack does this pallet go to). They nest. The intended state of the small loop is set by the big one. Chapter 05 comes back to this.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Name the five inputs to orientation in Boyd's sketch and say which one the primer is.

    Show answer

    Cultural traditions, genetic heritage, previous experience, new information, and analysis and synthesis. The primer is new information. Your past jobs are previous experience, and are the input most likely to pull you toward analogy. See the sketch.

  2. What does the implicit guidance and control arrow let a well-oriented actor skip, and why does that matter in a case interview?

    Show answer

    The decide stage. Not every move needs options weighed. Spending the forty minutes deliberating over the obvious parts leaves no time for the parts that are not obvious, which are the ones being scored. See the sketch.

  3. An operator says deliveries always slip at customs and reads the primer for customs dates. Which arrow in the sketch describes what went wrong?

    Show answer

    The arrow from orientation back to observation. The existing belief chose what got noticed. Boyd's remedy is to break the old pattern into verifiable parts and rebuild it from the evidence on the page. See why orientation is the big box.

  4. Give one meaning of tempo that applies to an operations team with no opponent.

    Show answer

    The time from a measurement to the corrected action. Three days from a known slip to a re-planned crew is a three-day loop whatever the dashboards do. Shortening it usually means moving observation closer to the person who can act. See tempo.

Takeaway

  • Boyd drew the loop once, in 1996. Orientation is the big box; observation is shaped by it; implicit guidance lets a well-oriented actor skip deliberation; everything feeds back.
  • First-principles thinking is Boyd's destruction and creation: take the inherited pattern apart and rebuild it from facts you can verify.
  • Tempo for an operator is the time from measurement to corrected action. Urgency is what orientation buys you, not what replaces it.
