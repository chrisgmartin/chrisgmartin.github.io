Section C · Three cases

Case: Failures Double at One Site

Reno's GPU failure rate doubles in a month. Is it real, what explains it, and which probe to run first.

The primer

The third shape: a reliability number that moved. This is the case most likely to tempt a candidate into a theory on the first read. Build the ledger first.

Primer: Reno, Monday 2 November

GridDP tracks GPU failures per thousand GPU-days. Fleet-wide the rate has been about 0.9 for six months. Reno (200 servers, 1,600 GPUs) ran at 0.8 in September and 1.7 in October. Dallas and Amsterdam were unchanged. During October Reno took delivery of 64 new servers from a single vendor batch, racked into rows 7 to 9. On 3 October one of the hall's four cooling units was taken out of service for a compressor repair and is still out; the site lead reports inlet temperatures "within spec". To make a customer date in mid-October, burn-in on two batches was shortened from 48 hours to 12. The customer on rows 7 to 9 has opened a ticket about job restarts. The site lead believes the vendor batch is faulty and wants to raise a bulk RMA. What is the current state, what is the intended state, and how do we close the gap?

Current state

  • Reno failure rate: 0.8 in September, 1.7 in October, per 1,000 GPU-days. Telemetry-derived. Measured, but see below about the denominator.
  • Other sites: unchanged. Measured.
  • 64 new servers from one batch, racked in rows 7 to 9 during October. NetBox. Measured.
  • One of four cooling units out since 3 October. Facilities log. Measured.
  • Inlet temperatures "within spec". Site lead. Reported, no number given.
  • Burn-in shortened to 12 hours on two batches. Reported, and a decision someone made.
  • Customer ticket about job restarts on rows 7 to 9. Measured (the ticket exists); the cause is inferred.
  • "The vendor batch is faulty." Site lead. Inferred.

What the page does not say, ranked: the failure rate broken down by row, by server age and by failure type; the actual inlet temperatures by row; how many of October's failures were on the 64 new servers; whether the denominator rose correctly when 512 GPUs were added mid-month; whether the shortened burn-in batches are the new 64 or older arrivals.

Said back: "Reno's rate doubled in a month while other sites held. Three things changed at Reno in October: a new vendor batch, a cooling unit out, and shortened burn-in. The site lead favours the batch." The two questions: can I see the rate by row and by server age, and what are the inlet temperatures by row.

Orientation

Begin with the denominator, because a rate that doubles when the fleet grows by a third is suspicious on arithmetic alone. If the 512 new GPUs were added to the numerator as they failed but to the denominator only at month end, the October rate is overstated. Check that before anything else. Suppose it checks out: the doubling is real.

Three things changed, and each is a candidate cause with a different physical mechanism.

  • The vendor batch. Sixty-four servers with a common component defect. Killed if the failures on the new servers, per GPU-day, match the failures on the old servers in the same rows. Checked in an hour from telemetry and NetBox.
  • Cooling. One unit out of four means rows at the far end of the airflow run warmer, and GPU memory errors rise with temperature. "Within spec" is a report; the number is what matters, and the spec has a margin that disappears under sustained load. Killed if inlet temperatures by row are flat and within two degrees of September. Checked in minutes from the sensors, if they are read per row.
  • Shortened burn-in. Burn-in exists to fail the weak units before they reach the customer. Twelve hours instead of forty-eight moves those failures into the first weeks of service. Killed if the shortened-burn-in servers fail at the same rate as servers that had the full 48 hours. Checked from the burn-in log against the failure list.

The three overlap, which is why the site lead's theory is plausible and insufficient. The new batch is in rows 7 to 9; rows 7 to 9 are the ones furthest from the working cooling units; and the shortened burn-in applied to two batches, probably including this one. A bulk RMA fixes exactly one of the three. If the cause is cooling, the replacement servers will fail at the same rate in the same rows, and the vendor relationship will have been spent for nothing.

The governing constraint is informational: the three explanations can be told apart with data the site already has, and nobody has run the split. Until the split is run, every action is a guess. That makes the first action obvious.

Intended state

Two states, two clocks. By Wednesday 4 November: the October failures split by row, by server age and by burn-in duration, the inlet temperatures by row for September and October, and one cause named with the evidence that ruled out the other two. By 30 November: Reno's rate back at or below 1.0 per 1,000 GPU-days, measured over the full month, with the cause fixed and a written rule preventing its return.

The customer's intended state is separate and nearer: a response to their ticket by Tuesday that says what is known, what is being checked and when they will hear again. Not a cause, which is not yet known, and not a promise, which would be a one-way door.

Two of these actions are taken before the cause is known: moving load off the warm rows and restoring full burn-in. Both are reversible and cheap, and both are right regardless of which explanation wins. That is implicit guidance in Boyd's sense. The RMA is the expensive, hard-to-reverse action, so it waits for the split.

What the interviewer will push on

  • "The split shows the new servers failing at three times the rate of the old ones in the same rows." The batch explanation survives and cooling is weakened, since old servers in the warm rows are fine. Check burn-in next: if the new servers are also the ones with 12-hour burn-in, you still cannot tell batch from burn-in. Run the remaining 36 hours on a sample and count what fails. Then decide the RMA.
  • "Inlet temperatures are not logged by row." An informational constraint that is cheap to remove: a handheld reading per row today, and a sensor per row this month. Say that the lack of a per-row reading is itself a finding to carry into the rule-writing.
  • "The vendor is offering to swap the whole batch now, no questions." Tempting and still premature. If cooling is the cause the swap buys a month and the problem returns. Accept the offer in principle, ask for a week to confirm, and run the split. A vendor who offers that readily may know something; ask them what they have seen in the batch elsewhere.
  • "Why not just raise the fleet-wide target to 1.7?" Because the other two sites did not move and nothing in the physics changed. Changing the target to meet the number is the opposite of the loop.

Why this is the shape of the job

The company whose principles this guide quotes lists, among the problems it is working on, operating tens of millions of accelerators at high reliability. At that scale the Reno question is asked every day about some hall somewhere, and the answer cannot depend on a site lead's hunch. It depends on the splits being one query away: failures by row, by age, by batch, by burn-in hours, by inlet temperature, each joined on a server identity that all the systems agree on. That is the ontology team's work, and it is why a data engineer gets this case rather than a facilities engineer.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. What is the first thing to check, before any explanation?

    Show answer

    The denominator. A rate that doubles while the fleet grows by a third may be an accounting artefact if GPU-days were not accrued daily for the new servers. See orientation.

  2. Why is a bulk RMA the wrong first action even if the batch is the most likely cause?

    Show answer

    It is expensive, hard to reverse and fixes only one of three overlapping explanations. If cooling is the cause the replacements fail the same way. The split that tells the explanations apart is a day's work with data already on site. See orientation and pushback.

  3. Which two actions are taken before the cause is known, and what justifies them?

    Show answer

    Moving load off the warm rows and restoring 48-hour burn-in. Both are cheap, reversible and right under every explanation. That is implicit guidance: a well-oriented actor does not deliberate on them. See closing the gap.

  4. The new servers fail at three times the old rate in the same rows. Is the case closed?

    Show answer

    No. Cooling is weakened, but batch and shortened burn-in still overlap if the new servers are also the 12-hour ones. Rerun the remaining burn-in hours on a sample and count failures before deciding the RMA. See pushback.

Takeaway

  • Check the denominator before the explanation. Then list the things that changed; each is a cause with a mechanism and a cheap test.
  • Three overlapping explanations mean the loudest one is insufficient. The governing constraint is informational: run the split before the expensive action.
  • Take the cheap, reversible, always-right actions now; hold the one-way door for the evidence; tell the customer what is known and when they will hear more.
