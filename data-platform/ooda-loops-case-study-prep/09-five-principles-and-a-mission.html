Five Principles and a Mission

What the company says it is for and how it says it operates, what each principle is testing, and how to tell a story that shows it.

The first half of the round

Before the case there are fifteen or twenty minutes of behavioural questions, anchored on your resume. The interviewers are not collecting a list of achievements. They are checking whether the way you have worked matches the way they say they work, and they have told you what that is: five operating principles, published on the company's hiring pages, which its interview FAQ says are "the thing interviews are really testing for". This chapter takes the company's own words, says what each principle is testing, and shows what a story that answers it contains.

The company is Fluidstack, a GPU data-centre builder whose public record this site covers in its own profile. Facts below are from fluidstack.io as read in November 2026 and are linked. Nothing here comes from inside the company.

What the company says it is for

The home page leads with a claim about speed: "We build compute faster than anyone on the planet". Below it is a list of problems the company says it is working on in order to reach hundreds of gigawatts deployed within five years: redesigning compute and power infrastructure from first principles; deploying hundreds of gigawatts of solar; building 250 MW data centres in six months from breaking ground; onshoring manufacturing of compute and power equipment; and operating tens of millions of accelerators at high reliability. The framing around these is political as well as technical: the site argues that whoever builds infrastructure fastest shapes what the technology is used for, and asks readers to help "ensure democracy wins".

Two things follow for a candidate. First, the mission is physical. The company's product is energised halls on a date, and every software role, including an ontology team's, exists to make that happen faster or more reliably. Stories about data work land better when they end in something physical changing: a delivery that arrived, a hall that passed, a fleet that stopped failing. Second, the mission is explicitly about speed under constraints. Six-month builds and first-principles redesign are the same two principles, urgency and first principles, applied to concrete and copper. The case study is testing whether you think that way about anything.

The interview FAQ adds one preparation instruction: research the company and read "Situational Awareness", the 2024 essay by Leopold Aschenbrenner arguing that frontier AI will demand gigawatt-scale compute within the decade. The essay's author leads the fund that led the company's Series A. Read it. You do not have to agree with it, but you should be able to say what it claims and what in it you find weakest.

Read the right-hand column as a checklist against each story you plan to tell. A story can have a strong outcome and still show none of the five. "I built a pipeline that moved thirty terabytes a day" says nothing about ownership, urgency or first principles until it says who asked for it, what the deadline was and which assumption it broke.

The shape of a story

Interview advice usually offers an acronym. The loop is a better frame, because it is the one the second half of the round uses, and telling your stories in it shows the "fast pickup" they are scoring. A story in loop form has five parts and takes about two minutes.

  1. What was true. The current state when you arrived, in three facts with numbers. "Twelve source systems, a two-day setup per customer, and a team of two."
  2. What should have been true. The intended state you set, with its date and the constraint that made it the right target. Say if you set it yourself; that is the barrel.
  3. What you worked out. The orientation: the constraint that governed, the assumption you broke, the option you rejected and why. This is where first principles and taste live, and it is the part most candidates skip.
  4. What you did and how fast. The actions, with a clock. Name who did what, including the people who were not you.
  5. What you observed afterwards. The measurement that showed it worked, or did not, and what you changed. A story that ends at "and it shipped" has not closed the loop.

Credit is part of the shape, not a courtesy. The round scores "credit to others" under crisp communication, and the first-principles principle says "no egos". A story in which every good idea was yours reads as either untrue or as a team of one. Name the colleague who found the bug, the operator who told you the count was wrong, the manager who said no and was right.

What not to do with the principles

Do not say "this shows ownership" at the end of a story. The interviewers map stories to principles themselves and will notice when the mapping is done for them. Tell the story with the elements in the right-hand column present, and let them find it.

Preparing five stories

You need more stories than principles, because the behavioural questions will not be phrased as principles. Prepare six to eight, each in loop form, each tagged with the one or two principles its right-hand column fits. Then check the set.

  • Every principle is covered by at least one story, and no story is asked to carry more than two.
  • At least one story is a failure: a loop you ran that did not close, and what you observed afterwards. The round scores honesty, and a set of five successes is not honest.
  • At least one story has a physical outcome, since the company's product is physical.
  • At least one story has someone else's idea winning. That is the "no egos" test and almost nobody prepares for it.
  • Each story's numbers are ones you can defend if asked how you know them.

Then the fast-pickup test: choose one story and be ready to tell it in the vocabulary of the case. "The current state was three counts of one fleet; the intended state was one number with a reconciliation by Thursday; the gap was an unowned definition and nine duplicate records." If the interviewers hear you do that unprompted after the primer, they have their answer on pickup.

Why this company, in your words

The fifth principle has a question hiding in it: why here? The weak answer repeats the mission statement. The strong answer connects something you have verified about the company to something you want to do. The public record gives you plenty to verify: the lease schedule and the dates its claims will be tested against (profile, chapter 10), the way it finances a campus (chapter 04), what SemiAnalysis found when it tested the service and why the rating was later withdrawn (chapter 06). Pick one, say what you make of it, and say what you would want to work on because of it. That is first principles applied to the question of where to work, and it is noticeable.

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Name the five principles and, for each, the element a story must contain to show it.

Love of the game: no manager in the story. Build what matters: what changed for someone outside your employer, and why this next. See the five principles.

  2. Why tell stories in loop form rather than with an acronym?

    Show answer

    Because the second half of the round uses the loop, and telling your own work in it is the fast pickup they score. It also forces the orientation step into the story, where first principles and taste show. See the shape of a story.

  3. Which two kinds of story do most candidates fail to prepare?

    Show answer

    A failure whose loop did not close, and a story in which someone else's idea won. The first tests honesty; the second tests "no egos". See preparing stories.

Takeaway

  • The mission is physical and about speed under constraints. Stories about data should end in something physical changing.
  • Five principles, each with a test element: unasked ownership, a clock, a broken assumption, no manager in the story, consequence beyond the employer.
  • Tell stories as loops, credit others by name, prepare a failure and a story where someone else was right, and have one story ready in the case's vocabulary.
