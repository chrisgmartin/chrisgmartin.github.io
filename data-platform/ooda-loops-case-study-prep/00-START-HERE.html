The round this guide prepares for

Forty-five minutes with one or two engineers. The first part is behavioural: what you have done, told against your resume, and how it shows the way the team says it works. The second part is a case study. You read a one-page primer about a real operational problem, then work it with the interviewers in three moves: say what the current state is, say what the intended state is, and decide how to close the gap. It is collaborative. There is no answer key. The last part is your questions.

The case is framed as an OODA loop: observe, orient, decide, act. The framing comes from a 1970s fighter pilot and it is worth knowing properly, because the interviewers do, and because a loose version of it ("move fast, iterate") misses the part they are testing. Chapter 01 draws the real thing. Chapter 02 maps it onto the three moves the primer asks for.

This guide was written for one company's loop, a GPU data-centre builder whose public hiring pages are quoted in chapter 09. The method is general. Any operations, platform or infrastructure interview that hands you a messy present and asks for a plan is the same exercise.

What they say they look for

The hiring guide for the round names five things. Each one maps to a chapter, so you can see where the practice goes.

They look forWhat it means in the roomWhere this guide practises it
Their operating principles in your past workOwnership, urgency, first principles, love of the work, work that matters. Shown in stories, not claimed.09
Engineering tasteSpecific, opinionated views with a real example behind each one. "It depends" is a start, not an answer.04, 05
Problem-first thinkingStay with the problem before proposing anything. The first five minutes are questions.03
Fast pickupApply the loop to your own work with little prompting. Have one past project ready to tell as a loop.10
Crisp communicationDirect, short, honest. Credit the people who helped. Ask about what you do not know.10

Notice what is not on the list: a correct answer. The case has none. What is being watched is how you move from a page of facts to a plan, and whether you can be argued with on the way.

The running example: GridDP's fleet

The three worked cases in chapters 06 to 08 use GridDP, the site's made-up GPU company. The fleet is the one introduced in Building an Operational Ontology: three colocation sites (Dallas, Reno, Amsterdam), 600 servers with 8 GPUs each, about 200 more servers a quarter, and twelve operational systems that do not agree with each other. Every number in the cases is a teaching fixture, not a fact about a real company.

The cases are chosen to cover the three kinds of problem such a company has: a supply problem (servers that are late), a data problem (systems that disagree about what is deployed), and a reliability problem (a site whose failure rate has doubled). A primer in the interview will be one of these shapes or a mixture.

What to keep in mind

  1. Orientation is the work. Observing is reading the page. Deciding is picking from options. The hard part, and the part the loop was invented to describe, is the middle: building a picture of why the current state is what it is, from constraints and evidence, not from the last similar thing you saw. Chapter 04 is the longest for that reason.
  2. The intended state is a sentence with a date in it. Not "deliveries on time" but "64 servers racked and burned in at Reno by 30 November, with the customer told by the 12th if that will not happen". A gap you cannot measure is not a gap you can close.
  3. Every action comes with the observation that tells you it worked. The loop is a loop. A plan that ends at "act" is half a plan. Chapter 05 insists on this; the cases practise it.

How to read it

In order if you have an afternoon. Chapters 01 and 02 give you the loop and the interview's version of it. Chapters 03 to 05 take the loop apart one stage at a time. Chapters 06 to 08 run it on three cases, each as a primer followed by a worked answer, so you can try the primer yourself before reading on. Chapter 09 is the behavioural half. Chapter 10 is the plan for the room.

If you have ninety minutes, read 02, 06 and 10, then do 07 cold. If the interview is tomorrow, read 09 and 10, then work one case aloud with a timer.

Each chapter closes with questions to answer from memory, a takeaway and the sources it leans on. Facts about the company are stated as of November 2026 and linked to where they came from.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. The primer asks for three things. What are they, and which stage of the loop does each belong to?

    Show answer

    Current state (observe), intended state (orient, because choosing what "should be" is a judgement about constraints and purpose), and how to close the gap (decide, with the acts and the measurements that follow). See the round.

  2. Which of the five things they look for is tested most in the first five minutes of the case?

    Show answer

    Problem-first thinking. The first minutes should be questions about the primer, not proposals. Jumping to a solution is the most common way candidates lose the round. See what they look for.

  3. Why does this guide treat the intended state as a sentence with a date in it?

    Show answer

    Because the gap is the difference between two measurable states. Without a number and a date there is nothing to measure against, so there is no way to say whether an action closed anything. See what to keep in mind.

  4. What is the one thing not on the interviewers' list?

    Show answer

    A correct answer. The case is collaborative and open. The method, the questions and the way you take pushback are what is scored. See what they look for.

Takeaway

  • The round is forty-five minutes: stories against your resume, a one-page case worked as current state, intended state and gap, then your questions.
  • Five things are watched: the operating principles in your past work, taste, problem-first thinking, fast pickup and crisp communication. None of them is a right answer.
  • Orientation is the work; the intended state carries a date; every action carries its measurement.
