Section C · Three cases

Case: Three Counts of One Fleet

NetBox, finance and telemetry disagree about how many servers are deployed. A data problem worked as a gap to close.

The primer

A data problem this time, and the kind an ontology team is hired to fix. Work it yourself before reading on. The three numbers are built to reconcile; part of the exercise is finding out how.

Primer: how many servers do we have?

The board pack for Thursday needs one number: servers deployed across GridDP's three sites. Three sources give three answers. NetBox lists 621 devices with role "server" and status "active". The fixed-asset register kept by finance shows 589 servers capitalised. The telemetry store shows 597 servers that reported in the last 24 hours. Last quarter's pack used the NetBox number; the quarter before, finance's. The CFO has asked why the fleet appears to shrink and grow by thirty-odd servers depending on who is asked, and wants a number with an explanation by Wednesday evening. What is the current state, what is the intended state, and how do we close the gap?

Current state

The temptation is to pick a number. The current state is instead three facts about three systems, each with what its number actually counts.

  • NetBox: 621 records, role server, status active. Measured. Counts records a person created and has not retired. Updated by the site crews.
  • Finance: 589 capitalised servers. Measured. Counts assets with a paid invoice booked to the fixed-asset ledger. Updated at month end.
  • Telemetry: 597 reporting in 24 hours. Measured. Counts machines that are powered, networked and running the agent. Updated continuously.
  • "Deployed" has no written definition. Inferred from the fact that two quarters used two sources.

What the page does not say: which servers are in each count and not the others. That is the whole problem, and it is knowable today if the three systems share any key. Ask whether they do. In GridDP they share the serial number, badly: NetBox serials are typed by hand, finance records the serial from the invoice, telemetry reports the one burned into the board.

Orientation

The constraints here are informational and organisational, and the physical ones are the reason the numbers differ at all. A server is a physical thing with a lifecycle: ordered, received, racked, burned in, running, in repair, retired. Each system observes a different point on that lifecycle, and none of them is wrong about what it observes. NetBox counts from racking until someone remembers to retire the record. Finance counts from the month the invoice is paid. Telemetry counts from first boot until the machine goes dark, for any reason.

So the differences are not errors to be averaged away. They are sets of servers in particular states, and each set has a name. Working from the serials, the sets turn out to be:

Check the arithmetic against the primer. NetBox: 577 running, 20 awaiting invoice, 12 in repair, 3 retired with the record still open, and 9 duplicate records pointing at servers already counted: 621. Finance: the 577 running and the 12 in repair, all paid for; the 20 are not yet booked and the 3 are written off: 589. Telemetry: the 577 running and the 20 awaiting invoice, both alive: 597. Every difference is a set of servers in a known state, and the duplicate records are the only entries that are not servers at all.

The governing constraint is organisational: nobody owns the word "deployed". Until someone does, the three systems will go on being right about three different things, and the board will go on getting whichever one was nearest to hand.

Intended state

By Wednesday evening: one written definition of "deployed" with a named owner, one number for Thursday's pack derived from that definition, and the reconciliation table above attached so that each other number is explained rather than hidden. The definition this guide would propose: a server is deployed when it is racked and has passed burn-in, whether or not it is reporting today and whether or not finance has booked it yet. By that definition the number is 609: the 577 running, the 20 awaiting invoice, and the 12 in repair, which are deployed servers that happen to be unwell.

By the end of the month: the 9 duplicate records merged, the 3 retired records closed, and a rule that a board swap updates the existing record rather than creating one. Those are the identity fixes, and they belong to the ontology work described in the ontology guide: one identifier per physical server, with every native serial that has ever referred to it recorded against it.

Notice the two clocks. The board number is a Wednesday problem and is solved with a definition and a table. The identity problem is a month problem and is solved with a crosswalk and a rule. A candidate who proposes building the crosswalk first has solved the wrong loop for Thursday.

What the interviewer will push on

  • "Finance will not accept 609. They want the capitalised number." Then the pack carries two labelled numbers, deployed and capitalised, with the 20-server difference explained in one line. The intended state was one number with an explanation; two numbers each with an explanation is the same state. What is not acceptable is one unlabelled number.
  • "The serials do not match well enough to build the table by Wednesday." Then the Wednesday number is NetBox's 621 less the 3 known retirements, labelled as such, with the duplicate count estimated from a sample and the full reconciliation promised for the 30th. Say which facts in the number are measured and which are estimated.
  • "Why not just make telemetry the source of truth?" Because telemetry counts machines that are alive, and twelve deployed servers are in repair. A source of truth for "deployed" has to observe racking and burn-in, which telemetry does not. Each system is the truth about what it observes; the definition picks which observation the word means.
  • "Who should own the definition?" Whoever is accountable when the number is wrong in front of the board. Usually the head of data or the COO. Not finance alone, not site operations alone, because the word spans both.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Why is averaging or picking one of the three numbers the wrong move?

    Show answer

    Because each number is correct about a different point in the server lifecycle, and the differences are real servers in nameable states. Picking one hides those states; naming them makes the numbers agree. See orientation.

  2. What is the governing constraint in this case, and which kind is it?

    Show answer

    Nobody owns the word "deployed". Organisational. Until it is owned the systems will keep being right about different things. See orientation.

  3. Why are there two clocks in the intended state?

    Show answer

    The board number is a Wednesday problem solved by a definition and a reconciliation table. The duplicates and open retirements are a month problem solved by merges and a procedure. Solving the month problem first misses Wednesday. See intended state.

  4. What is the observation that keeps this from recurring?

    Show answer

    A weekly three-way count comparison in which every difference must land in a named set, with the unexplained set required to be empty. It catches the next duplicate the week it appears. See closing the gap.

Takeaway

  • Three systems, three lifecycle points, three correct numbers. The gap is a set of servers per difference, each with a name.
  • The governing constraint is an unowned definition. Fix that by Wednesday with a signature; fix identity by month end with merges and a rule.
  • Publish the number with its reconciliation, and run the comparison weekly with zero unexplained allowed.
