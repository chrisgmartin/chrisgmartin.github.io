Section B · The data

Labels, Ground Truth & Feedback Loops

Where labels come from, how late they arrive, what they systematically fail to include, and how to design the label pipeline so the model learns from the world rather than from its own decisions.

Labels are data with latency and bias

Features get most of the architectural attention; labels get a column name. Yet the label pipeline decides what the model can learn at all, and it has two properties features don't. Latency: the truth about a prediction arrives later — a click within seconds, a rental's actual duration in hours, a chargeback in up to 120 days, "did the forecast hold" in a week. Bias: you only observe outcomes for the actions you took. GridDP's fraud model sees chargebacks only on payments it approved; the ranker sees clicks only on listings it showed. The model's own decisions carve the shape of tomorrow's training data.

Treating labels as "whatever column happens to be there" produces models that are confidently wrong about the part of the world they never see. Treating them as a pipeline with an explicit delay, a censoring rule and a bias-correction strategy is what this chapter is about.

The architecture question is how those blend without contaminating each other — which needs a label store with provenance, below.

Delay, censoring and the label window

Chargebacks can be filed up to 120 days after a payment, with most arriving in the first 45. A payment from 20 days ago has no chargeback yet; calling it a negative is the most common label bug in fraud systems, and it biases the model toward approving recent-looking behaviour. The row is censored, not negative.

label maturity — fraud
  payment at T                                             today
  │◀────────────── label window (90 d) ──────────────▶│
  │                                                    │
  ├─ chargeback filed at T+31 ─▶ label = 1 (known at T+31)
  ├─ no chargeback by T+90     ─▶ label = 0 (known only at T+90)
  ├─ today < T+90              ─▶ label = NULL (censored; exclude or model survival)
  │
  practical rule: a row is trainable only when  now() ≥ prediction_ts + window
  → the newest 90 days of the spine are unlabeled, always. Design for it.

Three consequences for the pipeline. The label table must be append-only with a known_at timestamp, so a training set built on 1 June and one built on 1 September can both be reproduced (a label that flipped from 0 to 1 on 15 July is two rows, not an update). The training window must end at least one label-window before the build date, and the validation split should be the most recent mature period, not the most recent period. And for models where waiting 90 days is unacceptable, use a shorter proxy label (chargeback within 30 days captures ~70% of eventual chargebacks at GridDP) and track its correlation with the true label so you know when the proxy drifts.

Don't backfill labels in place

Updating label = 1 on the original payment row destroys the ability to rebuild the dataset as of any earlier date and hides how label maturity behaves. Append (payment_id, label, known_at, source); derive the current label with a window. The extra rows are trivial; the reproducibility is not.

Survivorship bias and the feedback loop

The fraud model approves 97% of payments and declines 3%. Chargebacks — the only fraud signal — exist only for the 97%. The declined 3% have no label ever. Retrain on this and the model learns that the patterns it already declines are "not fraud" (it never sees them lose money), and it slowly drifts toward approving them. Rankers have the same problem in mirror image: a listing that is never shown is never clicked, so it looks unattractive forever.

Whichever you use, the requirement on the data plane is the same: log the decision, the score, the policy version and the propensity alongside every prediction. Without that log, no correction is possible after the fact. This is the same served-vector log that chapter 08 needs for skew analysis — one table, several consumers.

Human-in-the-loop and weak supervision

Human labels are the most accurate and the most expensive, so route them where the model is least certain. A review queue that pulls payments with scores in the 0.35–0.65 band and returns labels within 24 hours gives you high-value examples at the decision boundary and a fast proxy for delayed chargebacks. Engineering requirements: a queue with assignment and SLA, a labeling UI that shows the served feature vector (so the reviewer and the model see the same thing), reviewer identity and rubric version on every label (reviewers disagree, rubrics change), and periodic double-labeling to measure inter-annotator agreement — if two analysts agree on 80% of cases, your label noise floor is 20%, and the model's precision can't be trusted above it.

Weak supervision goes the other way: many cheap, noisy labeling functions ("more than three distinct cards in ten minutes", "IP in a known proxy range", "email domain created this week") combined by a label model that estimates each function's accuracy from their agreements. It is the right tool for cold starts and for classes too rare to review, and the wrong tool as a permanent replacement for ground truth — its errors are correlated, and the model will learn the heuristics rather than the fraud.

The label store

The pipeline above needs a home. A label store is a small, opinionated table family, not a product:

label store — DDL
CREATE TABLE ml.labels (
  task          STRING,      -- 'fraud_chargeback_90d', 'ranking_click', 'capacity_actual_7d'
  entity_id     STRING,      -- payment_id, (session_id, listing_id), (region, gpu_model, hour)
  prediction_ts TIMESTAMP,   -- the decision moment this label belongs to
  label         DOUBLE,      -- 0/1, a value, or a rank position
  known_at      TIMESTAMP,   -- when this label became known — append-only
  source        STRING,      -- 'chargeback_feed' | 'reviewer' | 'weak:lf_card_velocity' | 'exploration'
  source_ref    STRING,      -- chargeback_id, reviewer_id + rubric@v, labeling-function version
  weight        DOUBLE       -- propensity / sampling weight, 1.0 by default
) PARTITIONED BY (task, days(known_at));

-- current label for a task, as of a build date (reproducible):
SELECT entity_id, prediction_ts, label
FROM ml.labels
WHERE task = 'fraud_chargeback_90d' AND known_at <= :build_date
QUALIFY ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY entity_id ORDER BY known_at DESC, source_rank(source)) = 1;

source is the column that keeps blends honest: a training set can choose "authoritative only", "authoritative + reviewer", or "everything with weights", and the manifest (ch. 02) records which. When a reviewer rubric changes, the old labels keep their source_ref and can be excluded or re-weighted rather than silently mixed.

Worked example: the ranker's labels

GridDP's ranker predicts which listings a renter will rent. The implicit label is "rented after being shown". The spine is (search_id, listing_id, position, shown_at); the label is rented_within_24h, known at most 24 hours later — fast, plentiful, and biased three ways: position (row 1 gets clicked more regardless of quality), exposure (unshown listings have no rows), and the loop (the current ranker decides what's shown). The label pipeline therefore logs position and the ranker's score and policy_version with every impression, reserves two of the ten slots on 5% of searches for randomly sampled eligible listings (exploration), and stores the label with weight = 1/propensity for those slots. Offline evaluation uses the exploration slice only; training uses everything with weights. Nothing about this is a modeling trick — it is table design plus a serving-side logging contract, which is why it lives in this guide.

When to choose what

The label decision heuristic
  • Always: append-only labels with known_at and source; train only on mature rows; validate on the most recent mature period.
  • Delayed labels (weeks+): add a short-horizon proxy label and track its agreement with the true one; alert when it drifts.
  • Your decisions filter the labels (fraud, ranking, lending): exploration traffic or propensity logging from day one — it cannot be added retroactively.
  • Uncertain band or rare class: a HITL queue with rubric versioning and agreement measurement.
  • Cold start: weak supervision to bootstrap, with a plan to replace it.
Optional: the label answer at four levels
  • L1 · Foundational. Uses whatever outcome column exists; treats missing outcomes as negatives; overwrites labels in place.
  • L2 · Practitioner. Knows about label delay and drops recent rows; has a review queue; hasn't logged propensities.
  • L3 · Senior. Append-only label store with known_at/source, censoring-aware windows, proxy labels with tracked agreement, rubric-versioned HITL, inter-annotator agreement.
  • L4 · Staff. Designs the decision log and exploration policy as part of the serving contract, reasons about survivorship bias per model, and can quantify the cost of exploration against the cost of a blind model.
