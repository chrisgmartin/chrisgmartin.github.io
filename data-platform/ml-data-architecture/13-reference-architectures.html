Section E · Putting it together

Reference Architectures Compared

Every component in this guide can be assembled three ways: around a cloud warehouse, around an open lakehouse, or around a stream. They are not interchangeable. This chapter puts them side by side at every layer and gives you a decision framework keyed to the only three variables that actually decide it — team size, latency need, and budget.

Three ways to build the same plane

The Data Platform Systems Design guide compares three analytics stacks — open-source lakehouse (A), Databricks-centric (B), Snowflake-centric (C). The ML data plane sits on top of one of those, but its own architectural fork is different and deserves its own names, because two companies on the same warehouse can build completely different ML planes. The fork is where the centre of gravity for ML data lives:

  • W — Warehouse-centric. The cloud warehouse is the system of record for features and training data. Feature views are warehouse tables; training sets are queries; the feature store is either the warehouse vendor's (Snowflake Feature Store, BigQuery ML) or Feast with the warehouse as its offline store. Online serving is a thin materialization to a KV store. Maps naturally onto Stack C.
  • L — Lakehouse-centric. Open table formats on object storage are the system of record; Spark (or Trino/Ray) computes features; the feature store is Databricks Feature Store on Unity Catalog or Feast on Iceberg/Delta; MLflow (or the equivalent) ties datasets to models; training reads sharded files directly from the lake. Maps onto Stacks A and B.
  • S — Streaming-first. The event log is the system of record; features are computed continuously by Flink (or Spark Structured Streaming / RisingWave) and written to both an online store and a lake for history; training sets are built by replaying the log or reading the streamed history. The warehouse is a consumer, not the centre. Sits beside any analytics stack.
The reframe

The three are answers to one question: what is the source of truth for a feature's history? A warehouse table, a lakehouse table, or a log. Everything else — tooling, cost shape, latency floor, team skills — follows from that answer.

W · Warehouse-centric

Choose W when the company already runs on Snowflake or BigQuery, the models are tabular, the freshness bar is hourly-to-daily, and the ML team is small enough that "SQL is the feature language" is a strength rather than a ceiling. Feature pipelines are dbt models; point-in-time joins are ASOF JOINs; the training-set builder writes a snapshot to a stage or directly to Parquet in the lake for the trainer to read.

Strengths: one governance model (the warehouse's), one skill set, fastest time to a first model, excellent for batch scoring, and every feature is instantly available to analysts. Weaknesses: the warehouse is a poor dataloader and a poor streaming engine, per-query pricing punishes iterative experimentation, sub-minute features need a bolt-on stream path that breaks the "one system" story, and unstructured data (images, text at volume, embeddings) is a second-class citizen.

L · Lakehouse-centric

Choose L when the data plane must serve both analytics and ML at scale, when datasets include unstructured or very large tabular data, when reproducibility matters (snapshot-pinned training sets are native), and when the team can run Spark or equivalent. It is the most general shape and the one most large ML organisations converge on.

Strengths: open formats mean no egress tax between engines — Spark computes features, Trino serves analysts, Ray or PyTorch reads shards directly; time travel gives free dataset versioning; storage is cheap and the same bytes serve everyone; embeddings and vectors live beside tabular data (Lance, Iceberg with vector extensions). Weaknesses: more moving parts (catalog, compaction, snapshot expiry, engine versions); real-time features still need a separate streaming path; the "managed" flavour (Databricks) reduces the operations burden at the price of vendor gravity.

S · Streaming-first

Choose S when the product's value depends on seconds — fraud at payment time, live pricing, real-time ranking on session context — and when the team can operate Kafka and Flink in production. Every feature is a continuously maintained aggregate; the online store is always fresh because it is written by the same job that writes the history; training sets are built from the same computed history, so offline/online parity is structural rather than checked.

Strengths: the lowest possible feature latency, parity by construction, replayable history (rebuild any feature by replaying the log), and natural fit for event-driven products. Weaknesses: the highest operational bar (state management, exactly-once, watermarks, backfills from a log are slow), a permanently running cost for every feature, and most models simply do not need it — a streaming-first plane for a churn model is engineering vanity.

The decision framework

Three variables decide it. Score each honestly and the architecture usually falls out.

VariablePoints toward WPoints toward LPoints toward S
Team size & skills1–3 ML people, SQL-native, no platform teamA platform team exists (or Databricks is bought); Spark competenceDedicated streaming/SRE capacity; on-call for Flink state

Two rules break ties. First, start where the analytics platform already is — a W plane on an existing Snowflake is worth more this quarter than a perfect L plane next year, and the migration path W → L is well-trodden (export feature views to Iceberg; keep the SQL). Second, add S as a lane, not a replacement: nearly every mature plane is L with a streaming lane for the handful of features that need it. Pure S is rare and usually belongs to companies whose product is the stream.

The vendor feature store is not a decision

"We'll use the Databricks / Snowflake feature store" answers the tooling question, not the architecture question. Both vendors' feature stores are thin over their table format and both need the same online store, the same point-in-time discipline, and the same skew checks. Decide W vs L vs S first; the feature store falls out.

GridDP's choice

Score GridDP: a platform team exists (the DPE guide built it), the data is large tabular plus a physical-telemetry firehose plus ticket text, reproducibility matters (fraud and pricing decisions are audited), and the tightest features — bid_depth_5m for pricing, device_velocity_24h for fraud at payment time — need seconds while everything else is fine at hourly. That is L with an S lane: Iceberg on S3 as the source of truth for feature history, Spark/dbt for batch features, Feast (Stack A) or the Databricks Feature Store (Stack B) as the registry and materializer, Redis as the online store, and one Flink job computing the six streaming features and writing them to both Redis and the lake. The warehouse engine (Trino or Databricks SQL) serves analysts off the same tables. MLflow pins dataset snapshots to model versions.

What GridDP explicitly does not do: put the firehose through the feature store (rollups happen in ClickHouse and land as hourly features), run pricing off batch (the S lane exists for exactly this), or build a second streaming path for churn (hourly is fine, and the always-on cost is not).

When to choose what

The reference-architecture heuristic
  • Small team, tabular, hourly is fine, already on a warehouse: W. Ship the first model this quarter.
  • Platform team, big or unstructured data, many experiments, audits: L. Open formats, snapshot-pinned datasets, shards straight to the trainer.
  • Seconds matter for a few tier-1 features: L + an S lane for those features only.
  • The product is the stream and you have the SRE capacity: S.
  • Migrating: W → L is routine; L → S is a lane, not a rewrite; S → anything means you over-built.
The answer at four levels
  • L1 · Foundational. Names tools ("we'd use Feast and Snowflake"); can't say what the source of truth for feature history is.
  • L2 · Practitioner. Distinguishes batch and streaming features; knows a feature store has offline/online halves; would pick the vendor's feature store on whatever warehouse exists.
  • L3 · Senior. Frames the choice as where feature history lives; compares the three shapes layer by layer on latency floor, cost shape, ops burden and lock-in; picks by team, latency and budget; adds streaming as a lane for specific features rather than as the architecture.
  • L4 · Staff. Sizes the decision to the company's trajectory — starts on the existing analytics stack and names the migration path; keeps open formats to avoid engine egress; puts only the features that pay for it on the always-on lane; can defend the choice to finance in cost-shape terms and to compliance in reproducibility terms.
