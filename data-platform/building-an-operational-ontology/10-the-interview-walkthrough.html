The Interview Walkthrough

The GridDP ontology designed end to end in forty minutes: what to draw, which numbers to say, the trade-offs to name, and the questions to ask back.

The prompt and the forty minutes

The prompt: "A company runs a first-party GPU fleet across three sites and has twelve operational systems. Design the system that lets people and agents ask questions about it." The candidate's job is to show a method more than a diagram. Forty minutes divide roughly like this, and the figures are a plan, not a rule:

MinutesStepWhat the interviewer should see
5ClarifyUsers, questions, sources, freshness and permissions fixed before drawing
5NumbersVolumes and budgets stated with arithmetic
10ArchitectureOne path from sources to consumers, with the four guarantees located
8Trade-offsThree decisions, each with its flip condition
7Follow-upsWrong merge, erasure, agent over-reach
5Questions backQuestions that show operating experience

The example is GridDP throughout, and its figures are illustrative: three sites (Dallas, Reno, Amsterdam), 600 servers and 4,800 GPUs growing by about 200 servers a quarter, beside a marketplace of about 40,000 provider machines.

Clarify before drawing

Five questions, in this order, because each answer changes the design.

  1. Who asks? Procurement, site operations, finance, executives, per-site contractors, and agents acting for any of them. Roles that differ in what they may see are what makes permissions a design driver.
  2. What are the two questions? "Which Reserved servers promised to customer X for November are at risk, and why?" and "show me everything about this server and where each fact came from". One needs plan against observation, the other needs identity and provenance.
  3. Which sources, and which are hard? Twelve, from the marketplace Postgres to Google Drive. The hard ones have no change log: vendor PDFs, Slack, spreadsheets.
  4. How fresh? Five minutes for telemetry-derived facts, one hour for ERP and ticketing, 24 hours for documents and Slack. Ask what a stale answer costs, because that sets what to spend on the tight tier.
  5. Who sees what? Site, rack and device inherit; finance documents are restricted; a contractor sees only their own site; an agent never sees more than its caller.

The architecture in one path

[12 sources] Postgres · NetSuite · procurement · vendor portals · carriers │ NetBox · Jira SM · PagerDuty · telemetry · Salesforce · Slack · Drive ▼ [Capture] log-based CDC · webhooks · polling · document and message ingestion │ (every record keeps source, row or span, observed time) ▼ [Identity] crosswalk + probabilistic match → one entity ID, review queue ▼ [Fact store] facts with provenance, valid time and transaction time │ plan and observation in one shape ├────────▶ [Permission graph] tuples: site → rack → device, roles ▼ [Query service] filter first · cite · freshness · gaps · audit record ▼ [People · dashboards · agents]

Walk it left to right and name where each guarantee lives. Identity is the stage after capture (chapter 02). Freshness is measured at capture and at read (chapter 03). Provenance and history live in the fact store (chapter 04). Permissions sit in a graph beside it and are applied by the query service, the only door (chapter 05 and chapter 07). Then answer the two questions by tracing them through the picture: the November question reads plan and observation facts and computes slack (chapter 08), and the server question reads six linked records with their sources.

Name three trade-offs

Pre-filter or post-filter. Pre-filtering through a list of permitted objects is exact and cheap while the visible set is small, which holds for 600 servers. It stops working at 30,000 documents because the list is capped at 1,000 results in OpenFGA's defaults. The answer is both: an access label on the index for pre-filtering and a batch check on the top results as the authority. It flips if the corpus is small enough to list, or if checking costs less than the index label's upkeep.

Golden record or keep everything with confidence. A golden record gives one answer per attribute and is easy to consume; it hides the disagreement, and the GD-7F21 "deployed" answer came from a survivorship rule. Keeping every source's value with a confidence is more honest and costs more at every read. Use survivorship for attributes where one source is authoritative by design, serial numbers for instance, and keep all values for contested ones such as lifecycle state. See the trace in chapter 09.

Freshness against cost. Moving NetSuite from a one-hour poll to five minutes means 12 times the API calls (60 ÷ 5), for facts whose consumers act on a daily cadence. Spend on the five-minute tier only where a stale fact changes a decision within the hour: telemetry-derived health and delivery tracking. It flips when a source offers a change log, because capture is then cheap at any latency.

Handle the follow-ups

Say each answer aloud before opening it.

"Two servers were merged into one identity by mistake. What now?"

Merge history is recorded, so a merge is reversible. A split reassigns the source records to two entities, rebuilds the derived facts that came from them, invalidates caches keyed by the old ID and sends the affected review items back through the queue. Answers given while the merge stood are found through the audit records. The guard is a lower threshold for automatic merges on serial-like keys and a human queue below it. See chapter 02.

"A deletion request arrives for a person named in Slack. Walk me through it."

List every place the data lives: raw, derived tables, search index, vector index, caches and materialised answers. Delete by provenance key in each, expire the snapshots that still hold it, rebuild derived tables by replay without the deleted records, and keep a receipt per store. Exceptions for legal retention are decided by the owner of the obligation, not by engineering. See chapter 06.

"How do you stop an agent reading a finance document it should not?"

The agent has no way to ask as someone else, because identity comes from the credential. Its effective permission is the intersection with its caller's. The service filters before the model sees anything and records the check. A scheduled red-team probe tries the path and expects zero restricted objects. See chapter 07 and chapter 09.

Questions to ask back

  • Which source is wrong most often today, and how do you find out?
  • When two systems disagree about a machine, who decides which is right?
  • How long does it take for a revoked permission to stop working everywhere?
  • What is the longest a delivery slip has gone unnoticed, and which source revealed it?
  • Has an agent acted on a wrong fact yet, and what was changed afterwards?

Each tells the interviewer that the candidate has operated such a system. This team exists outside GridDP. A late-2026 job posting at a GPU data-centre builder describes it: a platform that indexes operational systems, documents and telemetry, gives each machine one identity, compares plans with observations and serves answers within each caller's permissions. The site's profile of that company covers what to be able to say in the room.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. An interviewer interrupts the drawing at minute 6 and asks for your storage choice. What do you say, and what do you do about the order?

    Show answer

    Name the choice and the number behind it, such as columnar tables for about 11 TB a year at the ceiling, then return to the plan. Clarifying and numbers come first so the choice has reasons. See the numbers.

  2. The interviewer says freshness must be five minutes for every source. What do you ask, and what is the cost?

    Show answer

    Ask which decisions would change within the hour. Five minutes on a polled source multiplies calls by 12 against an hourly poll. Hold the tight tier to where it pays. See the trade-offs.

  3. The interviewer asks what stops you from over-engineering this for 600 servers. What is your answer?

    Show answer

    Several parts are small at this scale: the permission list fits under its cap and fact storage is modest. The parts that earn their cost are identity, provenance and permissions, because they cannot be added after the facts exist. Growth is 200 servers a quarter, not a jump. See the numbers.

  4. Choose one of the three trade-offs and argue the opposite side as strongly as you can.

    Show answer

    For example, a golden record for every attribute: consumers get one value, there is no confidence logic to explain, and agents get simpler tools. It wins when sources rarely disagree and the cost of a wrong value is low. See the trade-offs.

Takeaway

  • Fix users, questions, sources, freshness and permissions before drawing; each answer changes the design.
  • Every number is stated with its arithmetic and the decision it drives, or it is left out.
  • One path runs from sources through capture, identity, a fact store with provenance and a permission graph to a single query service.
  • The three trade-offs are pre-filter against post-filter, golden record against keep-all-with-confidence, and freshness against cost, each with a stated flip condition.
  • Wrong merges, erasures and agent over-reach are answered by mechanisms already in the design, not by new components.
