Section E · Operate it

Operating Guarantees

Freshness, quality and security as SLOs with error budgets; tracing a wrong or stale answer to its cause and making that failure detectable next time.

Three things to promise

An operational ontology is trusted when it is right, current and safe to ask. Each promise needs a number, or it is a hope. The SRE Workbook gives the form: treat a service level indicator (SLI) as "the ratio of two numbers: the number of good events divided by the total number of events". It also supplies the data-pipeline versions. Freshness is "the proportion of the data that was updated more recently than some time threshold". Correctness is "the proportion of records coming into the pipeline that resulted in the correct value coming out". A security indicator is not among them, so it has to be defined here.

The target is a service level objective (SLO) over a window. The Workbook's advice is a four-week rolling window as "a good general-purpose interval", and its reminder that "100% reliability is the wrong target". What is left, the share of bad events the SLO allows, is the error budget, and "to make error budget enforcement decisions, you need to start with a written policy". All GridDP figures in this chapter are illustrative.

Freshness as an SLO, with the arithmetic

The freshness targets from chapter 03 become SLOs by counting. For the telemetry-derived facts on the 4,800 Reserved GPUs, with a 5-minute target, a checker evaluates each GPU's facts once a minute: good if the newest observation is under 5 minutes old. Over a four-week window that is 4,800 × 1,440 × 28 = 193,536,000 evaluations. An SLO of 99 per cent allows 1,935,360 bad ones. As time, that is 1 per cent of 672 hours, or 6.72 hours of the whole fleet being stale; at 99.9 per cent it is 40 minutes.

Those numbers decide what is worth building. A source whose outage lasts two hours uses nearly a third of a 99 per cent window's budget (2 ÷ 6.72 = 30 per cent). That is the case for a freshness SLO per source tier, with the 1-hour tier (NetSuite, Jira) and the 24-hour tier (documents, Slack) given their own windows, and not one number for the whole ontology. A single 99 per cent figure averages a dead document pipeline against a healthy telemetry feed, and reports both as fine.

Freshness has to be measured where the consumer reads it. The Workbook's client-side option, making clients check the watermark when they ask for fresh data, matches the service from chapter 07: it returns each cited fact's age, and the same field feeds the SLI. A bad event is a served fact older than its target, so the SLI counts what users saw, not what the pipeline believed.

Correctness: tests where bad data enters, and where it becomes a fact

Correctness is measured against known answers, because nothing else knows what correct is. The Workbook's example pipeline used manually curated input with known good output. GridDP does the same with a golden set: 60 or so questions about specific servers with answers written by site operations, re-run after every change and weekly. The share answered correctly, with citations that resolve, is the correctness SLI.

Tests run at two points. At ingestion they check the source's shape: columns present, keys not null, values in the allowed set. Fact-level tests check the ontology's own invariants after identity and survivorship have run. dbt's built-in data tests map onto them directly. A test is a query that returns failing rows, and "if the data test returns zero failing rows, it passes". The four built-ins are unique, not_null, accepted_values and relationships, and each has an ontology reading: one entity ID per serial, no fact without a source reference, a lifecycle state from a closed list, and every fact's entity must exist.

models/facts.yml (sketch)
models:
  - name: server_lifecycle_facts
    columns:
      - name: fact_id
        data_tests: [unique, not_null]
      - name: source_ref            # row or document span the fact came from
        data_tests: [not_null]
      - name: lifecycle_state
        data_tests:
          - accepted_values:
              arguments:
                values: [designed, ordered, in_transit, deployed, rma, retired]
      - name: entity_id
        data_tests:
          - relationships:
              arguments:
                to: ref('entities')
                field: entity_id

The built-ins check one column each. The ontology's costly errors are between facts, which need a custom test written as a query: no entity may have two lifecycle facts with overlapping valid time and different states unless one of them is flagged as superseded.

tests/lifecycle_conflict.sql (sketch)
select a.entity_id, a.fact_id as fact_a, b.fact_id as fact_b
from   server_lifecycle_facts a
join   server_lifecycle_facts b
  on   a.entity_id = b.entity_id
 and   a.fact_id < b.fact_id
 and   a.valid_during && b.valid_during         -- overlapping valid time
 and   a.lifecycle_state <> b.lifecycle_state
where  not a.superseded and not b.superseded;

The data quality chapter of the curriculum and the systems-design guide's SLO section cover test placement and the general SLO machinery; here the tests are tied to identity and provenance.

Tracing a wrong answer to its cause

Take a post-incident review, illustrative: an agent told a procurement lead that GD-7F21 was "deployed" when it was in RMA after INC-2093. The review does not start with the model. It starts with the answer's own record.

  1. The citation. The answer cited one fact: lifecycle state deployed for entity GD-7F21, from NetBox device 5519, status "active". The citation resolves through the provenance model of chapter 04 to a row and an observed time.
  2. The competing fact. Querying all lifecycle facts for the entity shows a second one: rma, from a Jira Service Management change record linked to INC-2093, observed hours later than the NetBox row.
  3. The rule that picked. The survivorship rule from chapter 02 said NetBox wins lifecycle state. The newer fact lost to an older one because of a configured source order.
  4. The upstream cause. Site operations had not updated the NetBox status after the RMA, because the change was recorded only in Jira.

Four causes were possible: a wrong match, a stale ingest, a bad rule or a wrong source. The trace excludes the first two in minutes, because the identity was correct and the Jira fact had been ingested within its target. The cause is a rule that trusts one source's status above fresher evidence. The model did exactly what the service told it.

Making the failure detectable next time

A fix that corrects only this server will recur on the next one. Each cause gets a guard at the cheapest layer that catches it:

  • A test. The conflict query above fails when two non-superseded lifecycle facts disagree. It would have flagged GD-7F21 within one run. Where one source is authoritative by design, the test has a documented exception list rather than being weakened.
  • A monitor. The count and age of survivorship decisions where the losing fact is newer than the winner. A rising count means a source order is out of date. The cost is one extra column in the survivorship log.
  • A contract. Site operations own the NetBox status on RMA, and the RMA procedure includes updating it. The check is that every RMA change record is followed by a NetBox status change within a day. This moves the fix to the source, which is cheaper than permanent reconciliation.
  • A golden question. "What state is a server with an open RMA in?" joins the golden set, so the next rule change is tested against it.

Add the question to the golden set in every case, and choose the other guards by cost. A flip: when the conflict is rare and a human reads every answer, the test and the golden question suffice and the monitor is not worth building.

Security as a guarantee, not a budget

Freshness suits a budget because a stale fact is a degraded answer. A leaked finance document is not a degraded answer, and a rule such as "99.9 per cent of answers respect permissions" would allow a leak every thousand answers. The Workbook's point that 100 per cent is the wrong target is about reliability and does not cover this. Security guarantees are therefore invariants, tested continuously, and any violation stops the line. Three are measurable.

  • Every answer has a recorded check. The service writes, for each answer, the caller, the on-behalf-of user, the version of the authorisation model, the set of object types checked, the number of results dropped and an ID. The SLI is the share of answers with a record, and the target is all of them: an answer without a record fails the request in the service itself.
  • Red-team the agent path on a schedule. A contractor at Dallas asks an agent for Reno data; an agent holding a finance caller's session reads a document that contains an injected instruction to fetch restricted files. The pass condition is zero restricted objects in any response, any log or any citation. OWASP's three causes of excessive agency, functionality, permissions and autonomy, are the checklist for what the tools could do. The red-teaming chapter covers the method.
  • Revocation lands in time. A removed permission stops affecting answers within the target set in chapter 06, measured by a probe that revokes a test caller's access and polls until the answer changes.

Agent behaviour beyond permissions, such as whether an agent picks the right tool, is a quality question for the agent evaluation chapter and not an SLO of the ontology.

Incident review and the weekly report

The Workbook's example error budget policy gives a rule that transfers unchanged: when one incident consumes more than 20 per cent of the four-week budget, "the team must conduct a postmortem" containing "at least one P0 action item to address the root cause". When the budget for the preceding four weeks is spent, the example halts changes other than P0 issues and security fixes. For GridDP, "changes" means onboarding new sources and changing survivorship rules, the two things most likely to introduce a new fault, so those pause. A data review adds two columns to the usual timeline: event time, when the real-world thing happened, and knowledge time, when the platform knew. The gap between them for the RMA was the whole incident. The audit records give a list of who received the wrong answer, so the correction goes to them.

The weekly report is one page with six lines: freshness SLI per tier with budget used, golden-set pass rate, age of the oldest item in the match review queue, share of answers with a recorded permission check, red-team result, and open incidents with their action items. It reports the same lines every week, so a change in any of them is visible without reading.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. The ontology reports 99.5 per cent freshness for the month, and the site team says Reno telemetry facts were stale for most of a Tuesday. How can both be true, and what do you change?

    Show answer

    A single blended figure averages a failing source with healthy ones, and a monthly window hides a short outage. Measure per tier, over a four-week rolling window, and count served facts older than their target. See freshness as an SLO.

  2. A 99 per cent freshness SLO is set on telemetry facts. A source fails for two hours. How much of the four-week budget is gone, and what does the policy do?

    Show answer

    The budget is 6.72 hours; two hours is about 30 per cent. That exceeds the 20 per cent threshold, so a postmortem with a root-cause action item is due. See the arithmetic and incident review.

  3. Why does the unique-key test not catch the GD-7F21 failure, and what kind of test does?

    Show answer

    The two lifecycle facts had different IDs and were each valid on their own. The error is between facts, so it needs a custom query over overlapping valid time and differing states. See correctness.

  4. A review finds the model gave a correct summary of a wrong fact. A colleague wants to fine-tune the model. How do you respond?

    Show answer

    The citation led to a fact the service chose by a rule, so the model was not the cause. Trace through provenance and fix the rule, the test and the contract. See tracing a wrong answer.

  5. Argue for a 99.9 per cent permission-correctness SLO, then say why the design does not use one.

    Show answer

    A budget lets the team ship while accepting rare errors. A leak is irreversible and cannot be repaired by faster recovery, so the guarantee is an invariant: every answer is checked and recorded, red-team probes expect zero leaks, and one violation stops changes. See security as a guarantee.

Takeaway

  • Freshness, correctness and security each need an indicator that counts good events over total events, a window and a target.
  • A freshness SLO is set per source tier and measured on facts as served, and its error budget is arithmetic, such as 6.72 hours in a four-week window at 99 per cent.
  • Correctness is measured against a curated golden set, with tests at ingestion and at the fact level, including tests between facts.
  • A wrong answer is traced through its citation to a fact, a competing fact and the rule that chose, before anyone blames the model.
  • Each cause gets a guard at the cheapest layer that catches it: a test, a monitor, a contract and a golden question.
  • Security guarantees are invariants with a recorded check on every answer and scheduled red-team probes, not an error budget.
