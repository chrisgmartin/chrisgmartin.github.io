Permissions on the Graph

Access that follows the links, derived facts that inherit the strictest input, and agents that never see more than their user.

Why the join breaks row-level security

Ask "which servers promised for November are at risk, and why?" and the answer touches a Salesforce commitment, a NetSuite order, a NetBox rack, a carrier delay and a Slack thread where the site lead explained it. Each system has its own idea of who may read its rows. Postgres row security, a NetSuite role and a private Slack channel are each correct inside their own walls. None of them can say what the join may show to a vendor contractor at Reno, because none of them holds the join.

So the ontology holds one permission model of its own, and every source's policy is translated into it when data arrives. There is no alternative: the join happens here, and only here can it be governed.

GridDP's roles are fixed for the guide: procurement, site operations, finance, executives, per-site contractors, and agents acting for a user. The rules are: site, rack and server inherit downwards; finance documents are for finance and executives; a contractor sees only their own site; an agent never sees more than the person it acts for.

Permissions follow the links

The model already has the structure permissions need. A site contains racks, a rack holds servers, a server has facts. If a contractor is granted a site, they should see everything under it, and nothing anywhere else. That is a graph walk, and the cleanest way to express it is as relationships rather than lists.

Google's Zanzibar paper describes this model and OpenFGA is an open-source implementation of it: store facts like "vendor-7 is a contractor of site Reno" and "rack R12 is in site Reno", and define that whoever can view a site can view its racks, and whoever can view a rack can view its servers. A check walks the path. The win is that one edge grants an audience that would take hundreds of list entries to write out, and that moving a rack is one change.

Some rules are about values rather than structure: a contractor's access ends on a date, a document is readable only below a spending threshold. Attach those as conditions on the edge rather than building a second system. Prefer relationships for anything that follows the org chart or the site tree, and conditions for a limit on a single edge.

One consistency point matters. After a contractor is removed, the next few reads must not be served from a cache that still has the old grant. Use fresh reads for the minute after any revocation, and cached reads otherwise. If a stale grant can leak a vendor quote, the extra latency is cheap.

Derived facts: the strictest input wins

Most of what the ontology serves is derived. "Late by four days" is computed from a PO date and a carrier scan. "Reorder cost at risk" is computed from those plus the unit price. Who may see a derived fact?

Only someone who may read all of its inputs. If the audience of a derived fact were wider than that of any input, the fact would leak the input. A "cost at risk" flag computed from a unit price tells anyone who can see the flag something about the price.

In practice this is done with labels. Every source field is tagged at ingestion with a small set of audience labels (finance, site:reno, everyone). Every derived fact carries the union of its inputs' labels, computed when it is made and stored in the envelope's audience line. A caller may read the fact when they pass the check for every label on it. The label set is a few dozen, so the check is cheap.

Label the field, not the record. A PO's delivery date belongs to procurement and site operations; its unit price belongs to finance. A late flag derived from the date alone carries no finance label, so the site team can act on it. Label whole records and the permission system becomes the thing that blinds the people closest to the problem.

A count is a derived fact too

"Servers at risk per site" computed over restricted rows inherits their restriction, unless a declassification rule says otherwise. Allow declassification, because without it the rule produces answers nobody may see and people export spreadsheets instead. But make it a named rule with a named approver, so widening an audience leaves a record.

Documents, and the lag problem

A document's audience is its source's: a Drive file's sharing list, a Slack channel's members. Ingestion copies those into the permission graph, and a sentence cited as evidence inherits its document's audience.

The sharp edge is lag. GridDP refreshes document content every twenty-four hours, which is fine. It is not fine for permissions: someone removed from a private channel at ten must stop seeing its messages at once, not tomorrow. So permissions sync on their own faster path, in minutes, and fail closed. When the permission sync for a source is older than its limit, that source's documents are hidden and the gap is reported.

A fact can be broader than its evidence. "Shipment delayed four days" may be visible to the site team while the vendor PDF it came from is finance-only. The default that holds up: show the fact, say that evidence exists and is restricted, and name who can open it. Hide the fact only when the fact itself would give away what the document says, as a negotiated price would.

Agents act as their user

An agent asks on behalf of a person and may never see more than that person. The query service authenticates both the agent and the user, runs every check as the user, and treats the agent's own identity as a further restriction: a tool may narrow what the user can reach, never widen it. This is the same confused-deputy rule the LLM security guide applies to tools, applied to the data layer.

The model is never asked to filter. Anything that reaches a model's context can reach the user, an injected instruction, or a log. Filtering happens before data reaches the model, which chapter 09 builds into the serving path.

Every answer leaves a record: who asked, on whose behalf, which version of the permission model, which fact identifiers came back, and which checks passed. Store identifiers, not values, so the audit log is not a second copy of restricted data. "Which contractor accounts read finance-labelled facts last week?" becomes a query.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. Postgres row security, a NetSuite role and a Slack channel list are each configured correctly. Why does that not settle who may see an answer that joins all three?

    Show answer

    The join happens in the ontology over rows no source holds together, so no source can govern it. The ontology needs its own model with each source's policy translated in. See why the join breaks row-level security.

  2. A new rack is added at Reno. What has to be written for the Reno contractor to see its servers, and what for them to stay out of Dallas?

    Show answer

    Only the rack's "in site Reno" edge; servers inherit through the rack. Nothing is written for Dallas, because the absence of a path is the denial. See permissions follow the links.

  3. Site operations cannot see the "late" flag because it was derived from a PO. What went wrong and how do you fix it without exposing the price?

    Show answer

    Labels were applied to the whole PO record. Label fields instead: the date is for procurement and site-ops, the price for finance. A flag derived from the date alone carries no finance label. See the strictest input wins.

  4. Document content refreshes every 24 hours. Someone proposes syncing permissions on the same schedule. What is wrong, and what should happen when the sync falls behind?

    Show answer

    A removal would stay effective for up to a day. Sync permissions separately and fast, and fail closed: hide that source's documents past the limit and report the gap. See documents, and the lag problem.

  5. Should an agent hold its own grants, or run every check as its user? When does the answer change?

    Show answer

    As its user, with its own identity only narrowing scope, so it can never exceed the person. A background agent with no user, such as a nightly report, needs narrow grants of its own and a named owner. See agents act as their user.

Takeaway

  • No source system can govern a join it does not hold. The ontology has one permission model and translates every source's policy into it.
  • Permissions follow the links: one edge grants a subtree, moving a rack is one change, and denial is the absence of a path.
  • A derived fact is readable only by someone who may read all its inputs. Label fields, not records, and allow declassification only through a named, recorded rule.
  • Permission sync must be faster than content sync and fail closed. Reads after a revocation bypass the cache.
  • Agents run as their user and may only narrow access. Every answer records who asked, what came back and which checks passed.
