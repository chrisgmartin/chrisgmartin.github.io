Who this is for

You run a data platform at a company whose operations live in a dozen tools. Procurement works in NetSuite, the site team in NetBox and Jira, sales in Salesforce, and everyone in Slack and Google Drive. Each tool describes the same server, order or incident in its own words, with its own keys. Nobody can answer a plain question like "is the Reno delivery going to be late?" without opening four of them.

The fix has a name: an operational ontology. One model of the things the company cares about, one identity per thing, every fact tagged with where it came from and who may see it, kept fresh enough to act on. This guide shows how to design one. It is a modelling guide first. The plumbing underneath (change capture, lakehouse tables, streaming) is covered elsewhere on this site and is linked where it matters.

It assumes you know what a warehouse is, what change data capture does and how a data test works. If those are new, start with The DE Craft.

The running example: GridDP's fleet

GridDP is the site's made-up GPU marketplace, introduced in the systems-design guide. For this guide it gains one thing: alongside the marketplace, GridDP now builds and runs its own fleet of servers for customers who want reserved capacity. Everything below is a teaching fixture, not a fact about a real company.

FixtureValue
SitesThree colocation sites: Dallas, Reno, Amsterdam
Fleet600 servers, 8 GPUs each, growing by about 200 servers a quarter
Source systemsTwelve: NetSuite, a procurement tool, vendor portals (emailed PDFs), carrier tracking, NetBox, Jira Service Management, PagerDuty, the telemetry store, Salesforce, Slack, Google Drive, and the marketplace's own Postgres
The one server we followSerial GD-7F21: designed as BOM revision B, ordered on PO 4471 line 3, shipped late by four days, racked at Reno in rack R12 unit 31, hit by a memory fault on 14 October 2026 (incident INC-2093), sent back for repair
The two questions"Which servers promised for November are at risk, and why?" and "Show me everything about GD-7F21 and where each fact came from."
Who asksProcurement, site operations, finance, executives, per-site contractors, and software agents acting for any of them

The whole guide is built around that one server and those two questions. If a design cannot answer both, it is not finished.

Three ideas to keep in mind

  1. Name the things before you wire the systems. The model (which objects exist, how they link, what can be done to them) is the product. Pipelines serve it. Chapters 02 and 03 draw GridDP's model in full before any data moves.
  2. The unit of work is the fact, not the table. A fact is one value about one object, with its source, its time, its confidence and its audience attached. Once every fact carries that envelope, the hard things (corrections, citations, "what did we know on Tuesday?") become lookups. Chapter 05 is about the envelope.
  3. Agents raise the stakes, not the rules. A software agent answering from the ontology must see only what its user may see, cite what it used and say what is missing. That is the same promise a dashboard owes a person. Chapters 06 and 09 enforce it.

How to read it

In order, ideally. Chapter 01 says what the thing is. Chapters 02 to 04 build the model and its identities. Chapters 05 and 06 make the facts trustworthy. Chapters 07 to 09 make the model operational: actions, plan against reality, and serving. Chapter 10 is about living with the model after launch. Chapter 11 is the interview version.

If you have ninety minutes, read 01, 02 and 06. If you are preparing for an interview, read everything, then 11 twice.

Every chapter ends with a few questions to answer from memory, a short list of takeaways and the primary sources it leans on. External facts are stated as of November 2026.
