Section C · Keep it true

What Every Fact Carries

Source, two times, a maker, a confidence and an audience: the envelope that makes a fact trustworthy.

A fact and its envelope

A fact is one value about one object: GD-7F21 has health state faulted. On its own, that sentence is a rumour. What turns it into something a site lead or an agent can act on is the envelope around it.

Each line of the envelope answers a question someone will ask within a month of launch.

  • Source answers "show me where that came from". It names the row, the document page or the message, precisely enough that a reviewer lands on the sentence.
  • True from and recorded answer "what did we know on Tuesday?" The next section is about why there are two.
  • Made by answers "which rule produced this, and what else did it produce?" If the rule is wrong, every fact it made can be found and remade.
  • Confidence answers "how sure are we?" It is 1.0 for a database field and lower for anything that came through a match or a parser.
  • Audience answers "who may see this?" Chapter 06 is about how it is computed.

The envelope is written at the moment the fact is made, by the thing that made it. It cannot be reconstructed later. By the time someone asks, the raw record, the rule's code and the identity mapping have all moved on.

Two clocks

A fact has one clock for the world and one for the platform. Shipment 1Z-…-88, carrying GD-7F21, was due on Monday and did not arrive. The carrier posted the exception on Wednesday morning. So the delay is true from Monday and recorded Wednesday.

Why keep both? Because someone made a decision on Tuesday. A customer was told the delivery was on track, and that was a reasonable thing to say given what the platform knew. Finance will ask about it. With two clocks, the question "what did we believe on Tuesday?" is a filter on the recorded time, and the question "what was true on Tuesday?" is a filter on the true-from time. With one clock, the second question overwrites the first.

The same split settles the incident. Memory errors on GD-7F21 began at 03:12 on 14 October. The incident was opened and linked at 09:41. A review at 08:00 would have shown the server healthy, and correctly so: that is what the platform knew. A review of what was true at 08:00 shows it already failing. Both answers are right, to different questions.

The writing rule that makes this work: never update a fact in place. A correction closes the old version by setting its recorded-until time and inserts a new version with its own recorded time. If the correction also changes when the claim became true, the new version carries a different true-from. The curriculum's slowly changing dimensions are the one-clock version of this. The second clock is what makes going back and correcting the past safe.

Not every fact needs two clocks. A GPU temperature reading is true at its sample time and nobody corrects it later, so one timestamp is enough and it lives in the telemetry store anyway. Keep both clocks for facts that people decide on and that sources revise: state, location, dates, owners, promises.

Evidence that stays put

A source that says "Jira" is a label, not evidence. Evidence is a locator that opens the original plus a check that it is still the same original. For a database row that is the row's key and its version. For a document it has to go finer than the file: the page and the character range, or the Slack channel and the message timestamp, so the reviewer lands on the sentence and not on a forty-page quote.

Two details carry the weight. The original object is copied into a raw store at ingestion and addressed by its hash, so if the vendor re-uploads the quote under the same name, the fact still points at what was actually read. And a short quote of the supporting sentence is stored with the fact, so a reader can see the basis without opening the document, which matters when the document is more restricted than the fact.

Facts extracted from documents also record the extractor and its version. If a PDF parser has been misreading a date column since September, the repair is a query: find every fact whose maker is that parser version, re-extract from the hashed originals, write corrections as new versions. Without the version column, the repair is a manual search.

A link to a live file proves nothing

If evidence is a URL to a Drive document, the document can be edited after the fact was made, and the link then supports a different claim than the one stored. Snapshot and hash at ingestion. The storage for thirty thousand documents is small next to one disputed vendor quote nobody can reproduce.

Freshness as a number

"Real time" is not a number and cannot be tested. The ontology states a target per source and measures the age of every fact against it. GridDP uses three tiers: five minutes for facts derived from telemetry (a failing GPU matters now), one hour for the business systems (orders, incidents, promises change on human timescales), and twenty-four hours for documents and Slack (a faster tier would cost more and change no decision).

TierSourcesWhy this and not faster
5 minutesTelemetry-derived state: GPU and server healthA rack going dark needs a page within minutes
1 hourNetSuite, procurement, carrier, NetBox, Jira, PagerDuty, SalesforceOrders and tickets change on human timescales; most have no change log, so they are polled
24 hoursVendor PDFs, Google Drive, SlackA vendor's email is routinely a day old before it is sent; nobody acts on a spreadsheet within the hour

Two rules about measuring. First, count every expected fact, not every arrival. A source that goes silent produces no late events to count, so a measure built on what arrived stays green while the data rots. Measuring the age of each fact catches the silence. Second, a derived fact is no fresher than its stalest input. "Server at risk" reads the carrier, Salesforce, NetBox and possibly a vendor PDF; its age is the age of the oldest input it used, and the answer should say so.

How changes get from the source systems into the ontology quickly is a pipeline question with its own chapters: change data capture in the curriculum, and ingestion in the systems-design guide. The ontology's part is to state the target, measure the age, and show it on every answer.

Tracing one fact back

On 14 October the health fact for GD-7F21 reads faulted. A reviewer asks why. With the envelope, the answer is five lookups, each by identifier.

  1. The fact says it was made by rule derive_health version 14, from three input facts.
  2. Two inputs are memory-error counts from the telemetry store, true from 03:12. Their evidence is a metric query and a time range.
  3. The third input is the incident fact, whose source is INC-2093 and a specific Jira change record.
  4. That change record opens in the raw store, with its hash, exactly as it was on 14 October.
  5. The rule version points at the code that turned three inputs into faulted.

Two more answers fall out of the same rows. Filtering to what was recorded before 08:00 shows no faulted fact, which explains why the earlier review passed. And listing every fact made by rule version 14 is the blast radius if the rule turns out to be wrong. That is what "where did each fact come from?" means in practice: a chain of identifiers, each one resolvable, ending at something that has not changed.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. The carrier posts on Wednesday that a shipment has been late since Monday. Which two times does the delay fact carry, and what can a Tuesday review still show?

    Show answer

    True from Monday, recorded Wednesday. Filtering on recorded time up to Tuesday shows the "on time" belief, so a promise made then can be judged on what was known. See two clocks.

  2. Why is "source: Jira" not evidence, and what is?

    Show answer

    It is a label. Evidence is a locator that opens the exact original (issue key plus change record, or page plus character range) and a hash that proves the original has not changed. See evidence that stays put.

  3. The PDF parser has misread a date column since September. What must each fact already hold for the repair to be a query?

    Show answer

    The extractor name and version in "made by", plus the hashed original and its locator. Select facts made by that version, re-extract, write corrections as new versions. See evidence that stays put.

  4. A freshness dashboard built from arriving events shows PagerDuty 100% green, yet nobody has been paged in a week during an outage. What did it miss?

    Show answer

    A silent source produces no late events. Measure the age of every expected fact instead, so silence lowers the number. See freshness as a number.

  5. Finance asks for every source to run at the five-minute tier "to be safe". What do you say?

    Show answer

    Faster tiers cost streaming and index work and change no decision for documents or orders. Raise a source only when a decision depends on it within the hour, such as a Slack channel where outages are declared. See freshness as a number.

Takeaway

  • Every fact carries an envelope: source, true-from, recorded, made-by, confidence, audience. It is written when the fact is made and cannot be reconstructed later.
  • Two clocks let the platform answer both "what was true?" and "what did we know?" Corrections insert new versions and never update in place.
  • Evidence is a hashed copy of the original plus a locator fine enough to land on a sentence, and the extractor's version.
  • Freshness is a target per source and a measured age per fact, counted over every expected fact so silence shows. A derived fact is as old as its oldest input.
  • With the envelope, "why does this say faulted?" is five lookups by identifier, and "what else did that rule produce?" is one query.
