Plan Versus Observation

Turning the graph into business signals: comparing schedules, orders and budgets with what telemetry, tickets and shipments say happened, and surfacing the gaps that matter.

The gap is a query

GridDP's plans live in one set of systems and its observations in another. Plans: the customer commitments and delivery promises in Salesforce, the delivery dates on purchase-order lines in NetSuite, site schedules in Drive spreadsheets and budgets. Observations: carrier tracking, NetBox, telemetry and Jira tickets. Today a person reconciles them by hand, in a weekly meeting, from memory of which spreadsheet is current. The ontology makes the comparison a query, provided both sides are stored in the same shape: a fact about an object, with a valid time, a source and a flag for whether it is a plan or an observation. All GridDP figures in this chapter are illustrative.

milestone gap (sketch, SQL)
select p.object_id, p.milestone,
       p.valid_at                                   as planned_at,
       coalesce(o.valid_at, f.forecast_at)          as expected_at,
       coalesce(o.valid_at, f.forecast_at) - p.valid_at as slip
from   facts p                                      -- kind = 'plan', the frozen baseline
left join facts o on o.object_id = p.object_id      -- kind = 'observation'
                 and o.milestone = p.milestone
left join forecasts f on f.object_id = p.object_id
                     and f.milestone = p.milestone
where  p.kind = 'plan' and p.baseline = true;

The baseline flag matters more than the join. A plan that is edited in place, so that the new delivery date replaces the old, turns every slip into "on track" because the plan moved to meet reality. The baseline plan is kept as a fact with its own valid time, and a revised plan is a second fact that points at the first. The bitemporal history from chapter 04 makes this cheap: the question "what did the plan say on 1 October" is already answerable.

A server is a chain of milestones

Every Reserved server passes through the same steps: purchase order placed, vendor ships, carrier delivers, rack and cable, burn-in, customer handover. GD-7F21 went through them with one irregularity, a shipment that arrived four days late. Each step has a planned date and an expected date, which is the observed date once the step is done and a forecast before that. For a shipment in transit the forecast comes from the carrier's estimate, or from the plan if no estimate exists, and is labelled as a forecast.

The table shows why the join needs identity: the left and right columns name the same server with different keys, and only the crosswalk from chapter 02 lets one query read across them. It also shows that the inputs are not equally fresh. The signal inherits the age of its oldest input.

Each step also has a duration after it. That is what turns a late milestone into a business consequence, because a four-day slip in a step with ten days of slack is nothing, and the same slip with no slack misses a commitment.

When a server is "at risk"

The rule is critical-path arithmetic. For a milestone, the latest finish is the commitment date minus the duration of every step after it. Slack is the latest finish minus the expected date. A server is at risk when slack on any upstream step falls to zero or below, and "watch" when it falls below a buffer.

Take customer X's November commitment: 64 servers at Reno live by 30 November. The steps after delivery are 3 days to rack and cable, 4 days of burn-in and 1 day for handover and acceptance: 8 days. The latest delivery that still meets the promise is therefore 30 − 8 = 22 November. The Q4 intake is 200 servers, about 15 a week over 13 weeks. Of the 64 servers promised to X, 40 sit in a shipment planned for 6 November and 24 in one planned for 18 November.

The delay does not endanger shipment A and uses all of B's slack. That is the answer to the first question: 24 servers, 192 GPUs at 8 per server, are at risk, and the reason is a single carrier delay on shipment B, with the evidence being the PO line, the carrier scan and the Salesforce commitment. The service returns those three facts with their sources, because "at risk" without a reason is an alert nobody can act on.

Slack, not lateness, is the signal

A server that is four days late is routine; the ontology would otherwise report it every time. A server whose slack has reached zero is a decision. Alerting on lateness measures the carrier. Alerting on slack measures the promise.

The second recurring question, "show me everything about GD-7F21 and where each fact came from", uses the same facts from the other side. The service lists the six linked records, PO 4471 line 3, the shipment, NetBox device 5519, the Prometheus instance, INC-2093 and the RMA, and for each fact its source and observed time. The plan-versus-observation view of the server is one row: planned delivery, observed delivery, a slip of four days. Provenance is what lets someone check the row against the carrier's page.

Parallel prerequisites and silence

A server does not go live because it arrived. It also needs a rack position that NetBox shows free, power and cabling ready at the site, and a network handover. These run in parallel with delivery, and the critical path is the longest of them. Slack for a server is the minimum of the slack on each path. A site whose rack readiness slips to 24 November holds every server destined for it, even when every shipment is on time. That is why the site schedules in Drive belong in the ontology as plan facts, in the same shape as PO dates, and why the signal names the binding path ("held by rack readiness at Reno", not "late").

Two rules keep the forecasts honest. First, an expected date is never earlier than now: a milestone planned for Tuesday with no observation by Wednesday has an expected date of Wednesday at the earliest, and its slack shrinks each day without anyone updating a spreadsheet. Silence is evidence. Second, every input to a slack figure carries its own age. A site schedule from a spreadsheet has a freshness target of 24 hours; if the signal says "zero slack" from a plan that is 20 hours old, the page says so, and the owner knows to check the source before moving trucks. The answer envelope from chapter 07 carries this information.

Ranking gaps so people keep reading them

With 200 new servers a quarter and five or more shipments, there will be several slipped milestones at any time, and a naive rule produces one alert per server. If shipment B slips, 24 or 40 alerts for one cause train people to ignore the channel. The SRE book's chapter on monitoring is direct about it: a page should be about something "urgent, actionable, and actively or imminently user-visible", and "if a page merely merits a robotic response, it shouldn't be a page". Three practices follow.

  • Group by cause. The unit of an alert is the delayed shipment, with the 24 affected servers and the commitment attached, not each server.
  • Rank by impact on the customer. Sort first by GPUs at risk against a dated commitment, then by remaining slack, then by any penalty clause recorded in Salesforce. Zero slack on a commitment is a page; positive slack under the buffer is a ticket or the daily digest; the rest stays on the dashboard.
  • Page on the symptom, ticket the cause. The symptom is slack reaching zero. A late supplier whose slack is still 16 days is information, and the owner can be told when the slack changes.

Measure the alerting itself with the attributes the SRE Workbook uses for alert strategies. Precision is the proportion of pages about a commitment that was really at risk. Recall is the proportion of missed commitments that were paged beforehand. Detection time is how long after slack reaches zero the page arrives. A rule with high recall and low precision pages for every shipment and is ignored within a month. A rule with high precision and low recall is quiet right up to the missed date. At about five shipments a quarter, the sample is too small to tune on percentages, so review each missed or falsely paged commitment by hand.

The thresholds flip with the contract. If a missed date carries a penalty or a customer is waiting on a launch, the buffer grows and the page fires earlier. Without a penalty and with spare capacity elsewhere, it can wait.

Per-object signals and aggregate metrics

Executives ask a different question: how many of the promised servers are on track this month, by site. That is an aggregate, and it belongs in the semantic layer rather than in the object graph. dbt's MetricFlow, as its documentation puts it, helps "define and manage the logic for your company's metrics": a metric is defined once in version-controlled YAML over semantic models whose entities are the join keys and whose dimensions are the ways to slice. The page notes that without dimensions "a metric is simply a number for all time". A GridDP metric would be the ratio of servers with positive slack to servers with a dated commitment, sliced by site and month. The systems-design guide's serving chapter shows the mechanics and the stack choices.

The two views must not disagree. The metric reads the same slack facts the signal reads; if it recomputes slack in its own SQL, the dashboard will say 93 per cent on track while the page says 24 servers are at risk, and the first number will lose the argument. The semantic layer sees only the aggregate and cannot say which servers. The object signal says which, and cannot say whether the trend is improving. Use the object signal to act and the metric to report. The permission model from chapter 05 still applies: a contractor sees slack for their own site only, and the metric must be computed from rows that caller may see or from a rollup safe for them.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. A project manager edits the delivery date on a purchase-order line to the carrier's new estimate. A month later the on-time metric is 100 per cent although two commitments were missed. How did that happen, and how does the model prevent it?

    Show answer

    The plan was overwritten, so the gap vanished. The baseline plan is kept as its own fact and a revision is a second fact pointing at it; bitemporal history answers "what did the plan say then". See the gap is a query.

  2. Shipment B is planned for 18 November, the promise is 30 November, and the steps after delivery take 3, 4 and 1 days. It slips six days. What is the slack, and what do you do?

    Show answer

    Latest finish is 22 November; the expected date is 24 November; slack is minus 2 days. The commitment will be missed unless a step shortens, so it is a page for the owner. See when a server is at risk.

  3. Two servers are each four days late. One has 16 days of slack and one has none. Why should they produce different outputs?

    Show answer

    Lateness is a fact about a carrier; slack is a fact about a promise. The first is routine, the second needs a decision. Alerting on lateness trains people to ignore alerts. See slack, not lateness.

  4. Forty servers share one late shipment. The monitoring tool pages once per server. What do you change, and where might your change be wrong?

    Show answer

    Group alerts by cause, so one alert names the shipment and the 40 servers. It can be wrong when servers in the shipment carry different commitments, because they need different owners and urgency; group by cause but rank by commitment. See ranking gaps.

  5. The dashboard reports 93 per cent of promised servers on track while the on-call page says 24 are at risk. Name two likely causes and the fix.

    Show answer

    The metric computes slack with its own logic or reads an older plan baseline. Define the metric over the same slack facts the signal uses, so there is one definition. See per-object signals and aggregate metrics.

  6. A carrier has not reported a scan for shipment B since Tuesday, and it is now Thursday. The plan said Wednesday. What should the slack calculation do, and what should the signal show?

    Show answer

    The expected date cannot be earlier than today, so slack shrinks each day without any update. The signal shows the silence as a gap beside the figure and the age of its inputs. See parallel prerequisites and silence.

Takeaway

  • Plans and observations are stored as the same kind of fact with valid time, so the gap between them is a query and not a meeting.
  • The baseline plan is frozen as a fact, and a revision is a new fact, so a slip cannot be hidden by editing the plan.
  • A server is at risk when slack on any upstream step reaches zero, where slack is the commitment date minus the downstream durations minus the expected date.
  • Every signal names its cause and attaches the facts behind it, so the reader can act on it and check it.
  • Slack is the minimum over parallel prerequisites, silence counts as evidence, and every input shows its age.
  • Alerts are grouped by cause and ranked by customer impact, and only zero slack on a dated commitment pages anyone.
  • The semantic layer reports aggregates over the same slack facts the object signals use, so the two never disagree.
