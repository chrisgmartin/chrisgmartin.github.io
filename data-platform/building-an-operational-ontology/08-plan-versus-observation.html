Section D · Make it operational

Plan Versus Observation

Plans and what happened stored in the same shape, so 'which servers are at risk' is a query with a reason attached.

The gap is a query

GridDP's plans live in some systems and its observations in others. Plans: the customer commitment in Salesforce, the promised date on the PO line in NetSuite, the site schedule in a Drive spreadsheet. Observations: carrier scans, NetBox rack-ins, telemetry, Jira tickets. Today a person compares them in a weekly meeting, from memory of which spreadsheet is current.

The ontology makes the comparison a query, on one condition: both sides are stored in the same shape. A plan is a fact about an object with a date, a source and a flag saying it is a plan. An observation is a fact about the same object with a date, a source and a flag saying it is an observation. "Planned delivery 18 November" and "delivered 22 November" are two facts about one shipment, and the gap between them is subtraction.

One rule protects this. The plan is frozen. If someone edits the PO's delivery date to match the carrier's new estimate, every slip becomes "on track", because the plan moved to meet reality. So the original plan stays as a fact with its own dates, and a revised plan is a second fact that points at the first. Chapter 05's two clocks already give you "what did the plan say on 1 October?" for free.

A server is a chain of milestones

Every server passes through the same steps: order placed, vendor ships, carrier delivers, rack and cable, burn-in, customer handover. Each step has a planned date and an expected date. The expected date is the observed date once the step is done, and a forecast before that: the carrier's estimate, or the plan if there is no estimate.

Notice that the two columns name the same server under different keys, and only the crosswalk of chapter 04 lets one query read across them. Notice too that the inputs are not equally fresh: a spreadsheet plan may be a day old, a carrier scan an hour. The signal inherits the age of its oldest input and should say so.

Slack, not lateness

A late shipment is not, by itself, a problem. A late shipment that leaves no time for the steps after it is. The difference is slack: the latest date a step can finish and still keep the promise, minus the date it is expected to finish.

Customer X is promised 64 servers at Reno, live by 30 November. After delivery there are three days of racking and cabling, four days of burn-in and one day for handover: eight days. So the latest delivery that keeps the promise is 22 November. Of the 64 servers, 40 are in a shipment planned for 6 November and 24 in one planned for 18 November. The second shipment slips four days.

ShipmentServers for XPlannedExpectedLatest that keeps the promiseSlack
A406 Nov6 Nov22 Nov16 days
B, before the delay2418 Nov18 Nov22 Nov4 days
B, after a 4-day delay2418 Nov22 Nov22 Nov0 days

That is the answer to the guide's first question. Twenty-four servers are at risk. The reason is one delay on shipment B. The evidence is the PO line, the carrier scan and the Salesforce commitment, each with its source. "At risk" without the reason is an alert nobody can act on; with it, the site lead knows which truck to chase.

Alert on slack, not on lateness

A four-day slip is routine; the ontology would otherwise report it every time. Slack reaching zero is a decision. Alerting on lateness measures the carrier. Alerting on slack measures the promise.

Parallel prerequisites, and silence

A server does not go live because it arrived. It also needs a rack position that NetBox shows free, power and cabling ready, and a network handover. These run alongside delivery, and the one with the least slack is the one that matters. A site whose rack readiness slips to 24 November holds every server bound for it, even if every shipment is on time. That is why the site schedule belongs in the model as plan facts in the same shape as PO dates, and why the signal names the binding step ("held by rack readiness at Reno") rather than just "late".

Two rules keep forecasts honest. First, an expected date is never earlier than today. A milestone planned for Tuesday with no observation by Wednesday is expected Wednesday at the earliest, and its slack shrinks each day without anyone updating a spreadsheet. Silence is evidence. Second, every input to a slack figure carries its age. If "zero slack" rests on a site plan that is twenty hours old, the page says so, and the owner checks the plan before moving trucks.

Ranking gaps so people keep reading them

With two hundred new servers a quarter there will be several slipped milestones at any time. If shipment B slips, a naive rule pages once per server: twenty-four alerts for one cause, and within a month the channel is muted. Three habits from the SRE books fix it.

  • Group by cause. The unit of an alert is the delayed shipment, with its servers and commitment attached, not each server.
  • Rank by what is at stake. GPUs at risk against a dated commitment first, then remaining slack, then any penalty clause. Zero slack on a dated promise is a page. Positive slack under a buffer is a ticket or the daily digest. The rest stays on the dashboard.
  • Page on the symptom, ticket the cause. The symptom is slack reaching zero. A late supplier with sixteen days of slack is information, and its owner can be told when the slack changes.

Review each missed or falsely paged commitment by hand. At five shipments a quarter the sample is too small to tune on percentages.

Per-object signals and aggregate metrics

Executives ask a different question: what share of promised servers is on track this month, by site. That is an aggregate, and it belongs in the semantic layer, where a metric is defined once over the same slack facts, sliced by site and month. The serving chapter of the systems-design guide covers the mechanics.

The one rule is that the two views must not disagree. The metric reads the same slack facts the signal reads. If it recomputes slack in its own SQL, the dashboard will say 93 per cent on track while the page says 24 servers are at risk, and the dashboard will lose the argument. Use the object signal to act and the metric to report. Permissions still apply: a contractor's metric is computed from the rows they may see, or from a rollup declassified for them.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. A project manager edits the PO delivery date to match the carrier's new estimate. A month later the on-time metric reads 100 per cent although two promises were missed. What happened?

    Show answer

    The plan was overwritten, so the gap vanished. Freeze the original plan as its own fact and record a revision as a second fact pointing at it. See the gap is a query.

  2. Shipment B is planned for 18 November, the promise is 30 November, and the steps after delivery take eight days. It slips six days. What is the slack, and what do you do?

    Show answer

    Latest delivery is 22 November; expected is 24 November; slack is minus two days. The promise will be missed unless a step shortens, so it is a page for the owner. See slack, not lateness.

  3. Two servers are each four days late. One has sixteen days of slack and one has none. Why should they produce different outputs?

    Show answer

    Lateness is a fact about a carrier; slack is a fact about a promise. One is routine, the other needs a decision. See slack, not lateness.

  4. The carrier has not reported a scan for shipment B since Tuesday. It is Thursday and the plan said Wednesday. What should the slack calculation do?

    Show answer

    Treat the expected date as no earlier than today, so slack shrinks each day without any update, and show the silence as a gap beside the figure. See parallel prerequisites, and silence.

  5. The dashboard says 93 per cent on track while the on-call page says 24 servers are at risk. Name the likely cause and the fix.

    Show answer

    The metric computes slack with its own logic or an older plan. Define it over the same slack facts the signal uses, so there is one definition. See per-object signals and aggregate metrics.

Takeaway

  • Plans and observations are facts of the same shape, so the gap between them is a query, not a meeting.
  • The original plan is frozen; a revision is a new fact. A slip cannot be hidden by editing the plan.
  • Slack is the latest finish that keeps the promise minus the expected finish. A server is at risk when slack on any step reaches zero.
  • Every signal names its cause and attaches the facts behind it. Slack is the minimum over parallel steps, silence counts, and every input shows its age.
  • Group alerts by cause, rank by what is at stake, page only on zero slack. The semantic layer reports over the same facts the signals use.
