The Interview Walkthrough

The GridDP ontology designed in forty minutes: what to draw first, which numbers to say, and the trade-offs to name.

The prompt and the forty minutes

"A company runs its own GPU fleet across three sites and has twelve operational systems. Design the system that lets people and agents ask questions about it and act on the answers." The interviewer wants to see a method more than a diagram. Here is how the time divides.

The example is GridDP throughout, and its numbers are teaching fixtures: three sites, 600 servers with 8 GPUs each, 200 more servers a quarter, twelve sources.

Minutes 1 to 5: clarify

Five questions, in this order, because each answer changes the design.

  1. Who asks? Procurement, site operations, finance, executives, per-site contractors, and agents acting for any of them. Roles that differ in what they may see make permissions a design driver from the first minute.
  2. What are the two questions that matter most? "Which servers promised for November are at risk, and why?" and "Show me everything about this server and where each fact came from." One needs plan against observation; the other needs identity and provenance.
  3. Which sources, and which are hard? Twelve. The hard ones have no change log and no keys: vendor PDFs, Slack, spreadsheets.
  4. How fresh? Five minutes for telemetry-derived state, an hour for business systems, a day for documents. Ask what a stale answer costs, because that sets what to spend on the fast tier.
  5. Who sees what? Site, rack and server inherit; finance documents are restricted; a contractor sees one site; an agent never sees more than its user.

Minutes 6 to 10: say the numbers

Every number comes with the decision it drives. A number without a decision is trivia.

Minutes 11 to 20: draw the model

This is the part that separates an ontology answer from a pipeline answer. Draw the object types in three rows, as in chapter 02: plans and commerce on top (Customer, Commitment, PurchaseOrder, POLine, Shipment), the fleet in the middle (Site, Rack, Server, GPU, Vendor), evidence below (Person, Document, Incident, RMA). Write the owning system under each. Draw the links with their names and point out the two that no system holds: fulfilled by and covers.

Then trace both questions across the picture out loud. The November question walks Commitment, covers, Server, fulfilled by, POLine, Shipment, and compares dates. The server question lists every link and property on one Server with its source. If the interviewer asks "why is Commitment separate from Customer?" or "why is GPU an object?", the answers from chapter 03 are ready: a customer holds several promises; a GPU fails and is swapped on its own.

Finish the ten minutes with the four obligations and where each lives: identity in the crosswalk, freshness measured per fact against a tier, provenance in each fact's envelope, permissions as a graph beside the model, all enforced at one door. Name the actions last: RaiseRMA, Reallocate, ConfirmDelivery, each through the same door.

Minutes 21 to 28: three trade-offs

Each with the condition under which you would choose the other way.

  • One winner or every candidate? A single golden value per property is easy to consume and hides disagreement; the wrong "deployed" answer in chapter 10's trace came from exactly that. Keep every source's value with its confidence and compute the winner. Choose a single stored value only for consumers that cannot show several, as a rebuildable copy.
  • Filter before or after the query? Listing a caller's permitted objects is exact and cheap while the set is small, which holds for six hundred servers and fails for thirty thousand documents. Use both: a coarse label in the index to pre-filter, an exact check on the top results as the authority. Choose one alone only when the corpus is small enough to list or the check is cheaper than keeping the label current.
  • Freshness against cost. Polling NetSuite every five minutes instead of hourly is twelve times the calls for facts people act on daily. Spend on the fast tier only where a stale fact changes a decision within the hour: telemetry-derived health and carrier tracking. The answer flips when a source offers a change log, because then speed is cheap.

Minutes 29 to 35: the follow-ups

Say each answer aloud before opening it.

  1. "Two servers were merged into one identity by mistake. What now?"

    Show answer

    Merges are recorded, so a merge is reversible. Issue a new identifier for the wrongly joined part, close the old crosswalk rows, re-resolve facts from their stored native keys, evict caches keyed by the old identifier, and find the answers given while the merge stood through the audit records. The guard is a high auto-link threshold and a review queue below it. Chapter 04.

  2. "A deletion request arrives for a person named in Slack. Walk me through it."

    Show answer

    Walk provenance forward from the person's identifier to every fact, index entry, cached answer and stored report. Destroy each by identifier, rewrite the files that held them, expire old snapshots, add the person to the erasure ledger every rebuild consults, and keep a receipt per store with a probe that reads zero. Counsel decides the exceptions. Chapter 10.

  3. "How do you stop an agent reading a finance document it should not?"

    Show answer

    The agent cannot ask as anyone else, because identity comes from the credential. Its permission is the intersection of its own and its user's. The service filters before the model sees anything and records the check. A scheduled probe tries the path and expects zero. Chapter 06 and chapter 09.

  4. "Procurement and site operations disagree about what 'delivered' means. Who wins?"

    Show answer

    Neither system. It is a definition, so the named owner of the lifecycle decides and writes it down, and if both meanings answer real questions both become properties. Chapter 10.

Minutes 36 to 40: questions to ask back

  • Which source is wrong most often today, and how do you find out?
  • When two systems disagree about a machine, who decides which is right?
  • How long does it take for a revoked permission to stop working everywhere?
  • What is the longest a delivery slip has gone unnoticed, and which source revealed it?
  • Has an agent acted on a wrong fact yet, and what changed afterwards?

Each one tells the interviewer you have run such a system. Teams like this exist. A late-2026 job posting at a GPU data-centre builder describes one: a platform that indexes operational systems, documents and telemetry, gives each machine one identity, compares plans with observations and serves answers within each caller's permissions. The site's profile of that company covers what to be able to say in the room.

✓ Check yourself

Answer from memory before opening each one.

  1. The interviewer interrupts at minute six and asks for your storage choice. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Relational tables for facts with a permission graph beside them, because the objects number in the hundreds and the hard parts are identity and permissions, not traversal volume. Then return to the plan: the model comes before the storage. See say the numbers.

  2. The interviewer says every source must be five minutes fresh. What do you ask, and what is the cost?

    Show answer

    Which decisions would change within the hour. Five-minute polling of a source without a change log is twelve times the calls of hourly polling, for no decision changed. Hold the fast tier to telemetry and carrier tracking. See three trade-offs.

  3. The interviewer asks what stops you over-engineering this for six hundred servers.

    Show answer

    Most of it is small at this scale: the permitted set fits a list, the fact tables are modest. The parts that earn their cost are identity, provenance and permissions, because they cannot be added after the facts exist. Growth is steady, not a jump. See say the numbers.

  4. Pick one trade-off and argue the other side as strongly as you can.

    Show answer

    For a single golden value per property: consumers get one answer, there is no confidence logic to explain, agent tools are simpler. It wins when sources rarely disagree and the cost of a wrong value is low. See three trade-offs.

Takeaway

  • Fix users, questions, sources, freshness and permissions before drawing. Each answer changes the design.
  • Every number comes with the decision it drives.
  • Spend ten minutes on the model: three rows of object types with owners, the links with names, both questions traced across it, the four obligations placed, the actions named.
  • Three trade-offs, each with the condition that flips it: one winner or every candidate, filter before or after, freshness against cost.
  • Wrong merges, erasures, agent over-reach and definition disputes are answered by mechanisms already in the design.
